# Outbreak Investigation Template

## Purpose

Wildlife disease outbreaks can have devastating effects on populations, ecosystems, and — in the case of zoonotic pathogens — public health. A systematic approach to outbreak investigation ensures that events are characterized accurately, causes are identified efficiently, and control measures are implemented effectively. This chapter provides a step-by-step template for investigating wildlife disease outbreaks, followed by a worked example.

---

## Step-by-Step Investigation Framework

### Step 1: Confirm the Outbreak

Before mobilizing resources, verify that an outbreak is actually occurring.

**Actions:**
- Determine whether the observed mortality or morbidity exceeds the expected baseline for the species, location, and time of year
- Rule out normal seasonal events (e.g., post-breeding mortality in salmon, mass emergence die-offs in insects)
- Consult historical records, local wildlife managers, and surveillance databases (e.g., WHISPers, NWHC records)
- Differentiate between a true disease event and a non-infectious cause (e.g., mass poisoning, habitat destruction, weather event)

**Key questions:**
- How many animals are affected? Over what area? Over what time period?
- Is the mortality/morbidity ongoing or has it ceased?
- Are multiple species affected or just one?

### Step 2: Define the Case

Establish a clear case definition to ensure consistent counting and classification.

**Components of a case definition:**
- **Species** affected
- **Clinical signs** or pathological findings that qualify an animal as a "case"
- **Geographic boundaries** of the investigation area
- **Temporal boundaries** (onset date to present or end date)

**Classification:**
- **Confirmed case** — Meets clinical criteria AND has laboratory confirmation of the causative agent
- **Probable case** — Meets clinical criteria AND has epidemiological linkage to confirmed cases but lacks lab confirmation
- **Suspect case** — Meets clinical criteria only

### Step 3: Establish a Timeline

Construct a detailed chronology of the event.

**Information to collect:**
- Date the first affected animal was observed
- Date the event was reported to authorities
- Temporal pattern of mortality/morbidity (single pulse, ongoing, cyclical)
- Recent environmental events (weather extremes, water level changes, agricultural activities, construction)
- Recent movements or introductions of animals in the area
- Timing relative to migration, breeding, molting, or other biological cycles

**Visualization:**
- Create an epidemic curve (epi curve) plotting the number of new cases over time
- Map cases geographically to identify spatial patterns

### Step 4: Conduct Field Investigation

Deploy to the site to gather firsthand data.

**Field assessment checklist:**
- [ ] GPS coordinates and detailed description of the affected area
- [ ] Photographs and video documentation of the site and affected animals
- [ ] Environmental conditions (temperature, water quality, weather)
- [ ] Habitat description and recent changes
- [ ] Presence of potential toxicant sources (agricultural operations, industrial discharge, algal blooms)
- [ ] Observation of affected and unaffected animals (behavioral abnormalities, species composition)
- [ ] Count and distribution of dead and sick animals
- [ ] Presence of scavengers or predators that may remove carcasses (which could bias counts)

**Personnel safety:**
- Conduct a risk assessment for zoonotic pathogens before field work
- Use appropriate PPE (minimum: gloves, eye protection; additional protection based on suspected agent)
- Establish decontamination procedures for personnel and equipment

### Step 5: Generate Hypotheses

Based on initial data, develop hypotheses about the cause of the outbreak.

**Consider the following categories:**
- **Infectious agents** — Bacterial (avian cholera, anthrax), viral (highly pathogenic avian influenza, canine distemper, hemorrhagic disease), fungal (white-nose syndrome, chytridiomycosis), parasitic (trematode infections, aberrant parasite migration)
- **Toxicants** — Pesticides, heavy metals (lead, mercury), harmful algal bloom toxins (microcystins, brevetoxins, domoic acid), petroleum products
- **Environmental** — Extreme weather, hypoxia, salinity changes, habitat alteration
- **Nutritional** — Starvation due to prey collapse, seasonal nutritional stress
- **Trauma** — Mass collision events (powerlines, wind turbines), predator-driven stampedes

**Factors that help narrow hypotheses:**
- Species specificity (single species vs. multi-species)
- Age/sex distribution of affected animals
- Spatial pattern (clustered around a point source vs. widely distributed)
- Temporal pattern (sudden onset vs. gradual increase)
- Clinical signs and gross pathology

### Step 6: Collect Samples

Systematic sample collection is critical for diagnosis.

**Fresh dead animals (priority specimens):**
- Full necropsy with systematic organ sampling
- Fresh tissues in whirl-pak bags on ice for microbiology and toxicology
- Fixed tissues in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histopathology
- Swabs (oropharyngeal, cloacal, lesion) in viral and bacterial transport media
- Blood (cardiac) for serology, PCR, and toxicology
- Stomach/crop contents for toxicology and diet analysis
- Ectoparasites and endoparasites for identification

**Live sick animals:**
- Blood samples (whole blood, serum, plasma)
- Swabs (oropharyngeal, cloacal) for pathogen detection
- Fecal samples
- Detailed clinical examination and documentation

**Environmental samples:**
- Water samples (for pathogen detection, toxicology, harmful algal bloom analysis)
- Soil samples (if soil-borne pathogen suspected)
- Invertebrate or prey species samples (if biotoxin bioaccumulation suspected)
- Air samples (if aerosolized pathogen suspected, e.g., Histoplasma)

**Sample handling:**
- Maintain chain of custody documentation
- Ship samples according to IATA regulations and receiving laboratory instructions
- Contact the diagnostic laboratory BEFORE shipping to confirm sample requirements and shipping protocols

### Step 7: Analyze Data

Compile and analyze all collected information.

**Epidemiological analysis:**
- Calculate attack rates by species, age class, sex, and location
- Construct and analyze the epidemic curve for temporal patterns
- Map cases to identify spatial clustering or point-source patterns
- Calculate case fatality rates

**Laboratory analysis:**
- Coordinate with diagnostic laboratories for pathogen identification, toxicology, and histopathology
- Ensure appropriate controls are included (samples from unaffected animals or areas)
- Request typing or sequencing of pathogens for epidemiological linkage and source attribution

**Statistical analysis (if sample size permits):**
- Compare affected vs. unaffected areas or populations
- Evaluate risk factors using appropriate statistical methods

### Step 8: Implement Control Measures

Based on the identified or suspected cause, take action to mitigate the outbreak.

**Disease-specific measures:**
- **Infectious disease**: Carcass removal to reduce environmental pathogen load, water level management, hazing to disperse aggregations, vaccination (if available and approved), movement restrictions
- **Toxicosis**: Identify and eliminate the source, remediation of contaminated areas, provision of alternative food/water sources
- **Harmful algal blooms**: Public notification, water body closure, monitoring for bloom resolution
- **Nutritional**: Supplemental feeding (rarely recommended; evaluate risks of disease transmission and habituation)

**General measures:**
- Restrict public access to the affected area if there is zoonotic risk
- Coordinate with land managers, wildlife agencies, and public health authorities
- Monitor the effectiveness of control measures and adjust as needed

### Step 9: Report and Communicate

Document the investigation and share findings.

**Internal reporting:**
- Complete investigation report including all data, analyses, and conclusions
- After-action review with the response team to identify lessons learned

**External reporting:**
- Report to relevant wildlife management agencies (state/federal)
- Report to WOAH if the disease is a notifiable animal disease
- Report to WHISPers or equivalent surveillance database
- Publish findings in peer-reviewed literature if the event has scientific significance
- Communicate with the public through appropriate channels (press releases, agency websites)

## Worked Example: Avian Cholera Outbreak in a Waterbird Colony

### Background

In late February, a wildlife refuge manager in the Central Valley of California reported finding approximately 150 dead snow geese (*Anser caerulescens*) and Ross's geese (*Anser rossii*) around a managed wetland unit. Mortality appeared to be ongoing.

### Step 1: Confirm the Outbreak

The refuge has historical records of annual avian cholera outbreaks during the wintering waterfowl season, but the magnitude of this event exceeded the 5-year average. Consultation with the USGS National Wildlife Health Center confirmed that the mortality rate was above the expected baseline. The outbreak was confirmed as a significant disease event warranting investigation.

### Step 2: Case Definition

- **Confirmed case**: Dead or moribund waterfowl of any species found within the affected refuge units between February 20 and the end of the event, with *Pasteurella multocida* isolated from tissues
- **Probable case**: Dead waterfowl found within the affected units during the event period with gross pathology consistent with avian cholera (petechial hemorrhages on heart and liver, hepatic necrosis) but without lab confirmation
- **Suspect case**: Dead waterfowl found within 5 km of the refuge during the event period without pathological or laboratory data

### Step 3: Timeline

- **February 18**: Refuge biologist noted 12 dead geese during routine survey
- **February 20**: Carcass count increased to 85; mortality clearly ongoing
- **February 22**: 150+ carcasses collected; investigation initiated
- **February 23**: Field team deployed; fresh carcasses collected for necropsy
- **February 24–28**: Continued daily carcass collection; mortality peaked on February 26 (estimated 60 new carcasses) then declined
- **March 5**: No new carcasses found for three consecutive days; outbreak declared over
- **Total mortality estimate**: Approximately 480 birds over 15 days

### Step 4: Field Investigation

The field team documented the following:
- Carcasses were concentrated along the margins of two shallow wetland impoundments
- Water temperature was 14 C, within the range favorable for *P. multocida* survival
- Bird density on the affected units was extremely high (estimated 50,000 geese)
- Species affected: primarily snow geese (approximately 80%) and Ross's geese (approximately 15%), with scattered northern pintails and American coots (approximately 5%)
- No obvious toxicant sources identified in the area
- Adjacent agricultural fields had been recently flooded for post-harvest decomposition

### Step 5: Hypotheses

**Primary hypothesis**: Avian cholera caused by *Pasteurella multocida*, consistent with the annual epidemiological pattern, species affected, gross pathology, and site history.

**Alternative hypotheses considered and rejected**:
- Avian botulism — Unlikely given the cold water temperatures and the species most affected (botulism more commonly affects dabbling ducks in warm weather)
- Highly pathogenic avian influenza — Could not be excluded initially; samples submitted for HPAI testing as a precaution
- Toxicosis — No identified source; multiple species affected in a pattern consistent with infectious disease

### Step 6: Sample Collection

- 15 fresh-dead carcasses submitted to NWHC for full necropsy, bacteriology, and histopathology
- Oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs from 10 fresh carcasses submitted for avian influenza PCR
- Water samples collected from three locations within the affected units
- Liver and heart tissues from five carcasses cultured on blood agar and MacConkey agar

### Step 7: Data Analysis

- *Pasteurella multocida* serotype 1 was isolated from 14 of 15 submitted carcasses, confirming the diagnosis
- HPAI PCR was negative for all 10 swabs tested
- The epidemic curve showed a classic propagated-source pattern with a peak approximately 7 days after the first detected cases, consistent with bird-to-bird transmission amplified by high density
- Attack rate was highest in snow geese and Ross's geese, consistent with known species susceptibility

### Step 8: Control Measures

- **Carcass removal**: Daily collection and disposal of all accessible carcasses to reduce environmental bacterial load and scavenger-mediated dispersal (over 480 carcasses removed total)
- **Water management**: Water levels in the most heavily affected unit were drawn down to reduce bird density and expose contaminated sediments to UV light and desiccation
- **Hazing**: Non-lethal hazing (propane cannons, vehicles) was used to disperse concentrations and encourage birds to redistribute to unaffected units
- **Monitoring**: Daily carcass surveys continued for two weeks after the last detected mortality

### Step 9: Reporting

- Full investigation report submitted to the US Fish and Wildlife Service refuge manager and Regional Wildlife Health Coordinator
- Event reported to WHISPers database
- Annual avian cholera management plan for the refuge was updated to include earlier water level management and lower bird density thresholds for hazing activation
- After-action review identified the need for pre-positioned carcass collection supplies at the refuge to reduce response time in future events

### Lessons Learned

- High bird density on limited wetland habitat was the primary risk factor driving the outbreak magnitude
- Proactive water and bird density management before the peak of wintering waterfowl use could reduce outbreak severity
- Rapid carcass removal remains the most effective practical control measure for avian cholera outbreaks
- Coordination between refuge management and wildlife health authorities was effective but could be initiated earlier through automated mortality threshold alerts
