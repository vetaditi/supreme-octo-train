# Triage and Euthanasia Decision Matrix

## Purpose

Triage in wildlife medicine requires balancing individual animal welfare against available resources, species conservation priorities, and the realistic potential for return to function in the wild. Unlike companion animal practice, the endpoint for most wildlife patients is release — and an animal that cannot be released to a fully functional life presents a fundamentally different ethical calculus.

This chapter provides structured tools for triage prioritization, prognosis scoring, quality of life assessment, and euthanasia decision-making in wildlife veterinary practice.

---

## Triage Classification System

### Priority Categories

| Priority Level | Description | Examples | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P1 — Critical, Treatable** | Life-threatening condition with reasonable prognosis if treated immediately | Active hemorrhage, open fracture with viable tissue, acute toxicosis with known antidote | Immediate treatment |
| **P2 — Urgent** | Serious condition requiring prompt treatment but not immediately life-threatening | Closed fracture, moderate dehydration, emaciation with responsive underlying cause | Treat within 2–4 hours |
| **P3 — Non-urgent** | Stable condition that will benefit from treatment but can tolerate delay | Minor wounds, mild parasitism, flight feather damage requiring time to molt | Treat when resources allow |
| **P4 — Observation** | Condition may resolve without intervention; monitoring indicated | Fledgling birds, mildly stunned window-strike birds, apparently orphaned neonates | Monitor; intervene only if condition worsens |
| **P5 — Expectant/Euthanasia** | Injuries incompatible with survival or return to function; or suffering that cannot be meaningfully alleviated | Bilateral blindness in raptors, spinal cord transection, advanced systemic disease, bilateral leg fractures in wading birds | Humane euthanasia |

## Prognosis Scoring System

### Wildlife Prognosis Score (WPS)

Score each parameter from 0 (worst) to 3 (best). Total scores guide disposition decisions.

| Parameter | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Body condition** | Emaciated (BCS 1/5) | Thin (BCS 2/5) | Fair (BCS 3/5) | Good to excellent (BCS 4–5/5) |
| **Hydration** | Severe (>10%) | Moderate (7–10%) | Mild (5–7%) | Normal |
| **Injury severity** | Multiple severe injuries or non-repairable | Single severe injury, repairable | Moderate injury | Minor or no injury |
| **Neurological status** | Comatose or severe deficits | Moderate deficits | Mild deficits | Normal |
| **Chronicity** | Chronic (weeks), secondary complications | Subacute (days), early complications | Acute (hours to 1–2 days) | Peracute, no secondary changes |
| **Species release potential** | Species cannot be released (non-native invasive, etc.) | Marginal — species has very specific habitat/behavioral needs | Moderate — common species, adaptable | High — hardy species, adaptable habitat |
| **Age class suitability** | Geriatric or neonate without conspecific rearing | Very young, requiring extended hand-rearing | Juvenile, approaching independence | Adult or independent juvenile |

### Score Interpretation

| Total Score | Interpretation | Recommended Action |
|---|---|---|
| 18–21 | Excellent prognosis | Treat and rehabilitate for release |
| 13–17 | Good to fair prognosis | Treat; reassess at defined intervals |
| 8–12 | Guarded prognosis | Treat if resources allow; set clear reassessment milestones with defined decision points |
| 4–7 | Poor prognosis | Euthanasia recommended unless exceptional circumstances (endangered species, research value) |
| 0–3 | Grave prognosis | Euthanasia indicated |

## Quality of Life Assessment

### Adapted Five Domains Model for Wildlife

The Five Domains Model provides a welfare assessment framework that can be applied to wild animals in care:

#### Domain 1: Nutrition
- Is the animal eating voluntarily and maintaining or gaining weight?
- Is the diet species-appropriate?
- Can the animal self-feed, or does it require assisted feeding?

#### Domain 2: Environment
- Is the housing appropriate for the species (size, substrate, temperature, humidity, lighting)?
- Can the animal express normal resting behaviors?
- Is noise and visual disturbance minimized?

#### Domain 3: Health
- Is pain being effectively managed?
- Are injuries healing on an expected timeline?
- Are there secondary complications (infection, pressure sores, stress-related disease)?

#### Domain 4: Behavioral Interactions
- Can the animal interact with conspecifics (where appropriate and safe)?
- Is the animal displaying normal species-typical behaviors?
- Are abnormal behaviors present (stereotypies, self-mutilation, excessive fear responses)?

#### Domain 5: Mental State
- What is the overall affective state of the animal?
- Is the animal showing signs of chronic distress (persistent anorexia, withdrawal, learned helplessness)?
- Is there evidence of habituation and coping, or is stress increasing over time?

### Quality of Life Scoring

| Domain | Score 1 (Poor) | Score 2 (Marginal) | Score 3 (Adequate) | Score 4 (Good) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nutrition | Refuses food; losing weight | Accepts assisted feeding; weight stable | Eats voluntarily; weight stable | Self-feeds eagerly; gaining weight |
| Environment | Inappropriate housing; unable to thermoregulate | Minimally adequate housing | Appropriate housing; resting comfortably | Optimal housing; using space normally |
| Health | Uncontrolled pain; worsening condition | Pain partially controlled; slow healing | Pain well managed; healing on track | Minimal pain; healing well |
| Behavioral | Stereotypies, self-harm, persistent panic | Reduced activity; abnormal behaviors present | Some normal behaviors; abnormal behaviors rare | Normal species-typical behavior repertoire |
| Mental state | Chronic distress, learned helplessness | Intermittent distress; some coping | Generally calm; occasional stress | Calm, alert, responsive |

**Total score 5–8**: Euthanasia should be strongly considered
**Total score 9–12**: Welfare concerns present; reassess within defined timeframe; modify care plan
**Total score 13–16**: Welfare adequate; continue current management
**Total score 17–20**: Welfare good; animal is a candidate for progression in rehabilitation

## Euthanasia Decision Framework

### When Is Euthanasia Indicated?

Euthanasia is indicated when one or more of the following criteria are met:

1. **The animal is suffering and suffering cannot be meaningfully alleviated**
   - Uncontrollable pain despite multimodal analgesia
   - Severe respiratory distress unresponsive to treatment
   - Terminal disease with progressive decline

2. **The animal cannot be returned to a functional life in the wild**
   - Permanent loss of flight in a volant species
   - Bilateral blindness in a visually dependent predator
   - Loss of a limb critical to species-specific locomotion or foraging
   - Chronic neurological deficits precluding normal behavior

3. **The animal poses an unacceptable risk to other animals or humans**
   - Confirmed or strongly suspected rabies
   - Highly pathogenic avian influenza in a species that could transmit to poultry or other wildlife
   - Non-native invasive species that cannot legally be released

4. **Rehabilitation is not in the animal's best interest**
   - The duration of captivity required would cause greater suffering than euthanasia
   - The animal is a species that tolerates captivity extremely poorly (e.g., some cetaceans, large migratory birds)
   - The animal is a neonate of a species prone to imprinting, and conspecific rearing is not available

5. **Resources are insufficient to provide adequate care**
   - During mass casualty events, resources must be directed to animals with the best prognosis
   - Treating one animal with a poor prognosis at the expense of multiple animals with good prognoses is ethically indefensible

### Species-Specific Euthanasia Considerations

| Taxon | Key Considerations |
|---|---|
| **Raptors** | Binocular vision is essential for most hunting species; loss of one eye may be compatible with release in some buteos and vultures but not accipiters or falcons. Bumblefoot can become chronic and refractory. |
| **Waterfowl** | Loss of waterproofing is a critical concern; chronic feather damage may preclude release. Angel wing deformity in adults is permanent. |
| **Songbirds** | Small body size limits surgical options; chronic foot or leg injuries may prevent perching and foraging. High metabolic rate means rapid decline if not eating. |
| **Marine mammals** | Prolonged captivity causes severe stress in many species. Chronic entanglement injuries may compromise thermoregulation permanently. Federal authorization required for euthanasia decisions. |
| **Sea turtles** | Cold-stunned turtles often recover well with supportive care. Fibropapillomatosis prognosis depends on tumor burden and location. Buoyancy disorders may indicate internal pathology. |
| **Terrestrial mammals** | Limb amputation is generally not compatible with release for most species. Orphaned carnivore neonates require careful imprinting prevention. Consider rabies risk in all neurological cases. |
| **Bats** | White-nose syndrome has population-level implications; individual treatment decisions should consider the broader epidemiological context. Wing membrane damage can heal but requires extended rehabilitation. |

## Ethical Frameworks for End-of-Life Decisions

### Utilitarian Perspective

- Focuses on maximizing overall well-being and minimizing suffering across all affected parties
- Supports euthanasia when suffering cannot be alleviated or when resources can save more animals elsewhere
- May prioritize endangered species over common species based on population-level benefit

### Rights-Based Perspective

- Each animal has inherent value and a right to life
- Euthanasia is justified only to prevent irremediable suffering
- Challenges utilitarian resource allocation that values some species over others

### Virtue Ethics Perspective

- Emphasizes the character of the decision-maker: compassion, wisdom, courage, and honesty
- A virtuous clinician neither prolongs suffering out of reluctance to euthanize nor euthanizes prematurely out of convenience
- Supports reflective practice and emotional self-care for clinicians making these decisions

### Compassionate Conservation

- Emerging framework that emphasizes individual animal welfare within conservation practice
- Challenges the assumption that population-level goals always override individual welfare
- Advocates for the principle of "first, do no harm" applied to individual wild animals
- Acknowledges tension between this approach and traditional conservation triage

## Welfare Indicators to Monitor

### Signs of Adequate Welfare
- Voluntary food intake
- Normal sleep-wake cycles
- Species-typical grooming behavior
- Appropriate responses to stimuli (neither excessive fear nor abnormal tameness)
- Progressive healing and functional improvement

### Signs of Compromised Welfare Requiring Reassessment
- Persistent anorexia beyond the expected species-specific stress period
- Self-mutilation or stereotypic behavior
- Failure to heal on expected timeline
- Progressive weight loss despite nutritional support
- Excessive fear responses that do not diminish with habituation
- Development of secondary conditions (pressure sores, aspergillosis, pododermatitis)

## Documentation Requirements

Every euthanasia decision should be documented with:

- Date, time, and personnel involved
- Clinical justification (specific criteria met from the framework above)
- Method of euthanasia (must comply with AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals)
- Confirmation of death
- Disposition of remains (necropsy, sample collection, carcass disposal)
- Notification of relevant agencies (especially for listed species or cases with legal implications)

Euthanasia is a clinical skill and an ethical responsibility. It should never be performed casually, but neither should it be delayed when it is clearly in the animal's best interest. Clinicians should be supported with training, mentorship, and access to peer consultation for difficult decisions.
