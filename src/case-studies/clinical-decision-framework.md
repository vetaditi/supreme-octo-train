# Clinical Decision Frameworks

## Purpose

Clinical decision trees provide structured pathways for managing common wildlife presentations. These frameworks are designed to guide clinicians through the critical steps of assessment, stabilization, diagnosis, and treatment planning. They are particularly valuable for less-experienced practitioners, for training purposes, and for use in field conditions where rapid decision-making is essential.

These decision pathways are guides, not rigid protocols. Clinical judgment must always be applied in context, and deviations from the framework should be documented with rationale.

---

## Decision Tree 1: Bird Unable to Fly

### Initial Assessment

1. **Is the bird in immediate danger?** (road, predator, water)
   - YES → Secure the bird safely; contain in ventilated box; minimize handling
   - NO → Observe briefly before capture to assess behavior

2. **Assess mentation and responsiveness**
   - Unresponsive/moribund → Proceed to critical care stabilization; consider humane euthanasia if severe injuries present
   - Responsive → Continue assessment

3. **Whole-body palpation and visual inspection**
   - Obvious fracture (wing droop, crepitus, instability) → Immobilize; radiograph
   - No obvious fracture → Evaluate for soft tissue injury, neurological deficits, or systemic illness

4. **Wing-specific evaluation**
   - Asymmetric wing extension → Radiograph to rule out fracture, luxation, or soft tissue swelling
   - Symmetric but weak → Consider systemic causes (toxicosis, infection, metabolic disease, emaciation)
   - Normal extension but no flight → Evaluate flight feather condition (damage, molt), patagial integrity, and body condition

5. **Diagnostic pathway**
   - Radiograph whole body (VD and lateral)
   - Minimum blood panel: PCV/TS, blood glucose, blood lead
   - If neurological signs present: consider West Nile virus, avian influenza, organophosphate toxicosis, head trauma

6. **Disposition decision**
   - Repairable fracture with good prognosis → Treat
   - Irrepairable fracture (open, comminuted articular, joint luxation with chronic changes) → Euthanasia or educational placement assessment
   - Systemic illness → Treat underlying cause; reassess flight potential during recovery

## Decision Tree 2: Raptor with Ocular Trauma

### Initial Assessment

1. **Stabilize the patient first** — Address life-threatening injuries, dehydration, and hypothermia before detailed ophthalmic examination

2. **Ophthalmic examination** (ideally with magnification and direct ophthalmoscopy)
   - Assess both eyes systematically
   - Document: menace response, dazzle reflex, pupillary light response (direct and consensual), anterior chamber depth, lens position, fundic examination

3. **Classify the injury**

   | Finding | Likely Diagnosis | Prognosis for Release |
   |---|---|---|
   | Hyphema, normal PLR, intact globe | Blunt trauma, anterior uveitis | Fair to good with treatment |
   | Lens luxation | Blunt trauma or chronic uveitis | Poor for hunting raptors; guarded for others |
   | Corneal perforation, collapsed anterior chamber | Penetrating trauma | Poor to grave |
   | Retinal detachment or hemorrhage | Blunt trauma, hypertension | Grave for diurnal raptors |
   | Phthisis bulbi (shrunken globe) | Chronic, end-stage eye | Permanent; assess unilateral vs bilateral |
   | Pecten damage (avian-specific) | Intraocular trauma | Variable; monitor for secondary complications |

4. **Unilateral vs bilateral involvement**
   - Bilateral blindness → Euthanasia in most raptor species (hunting raptors cannot survive without binocular vision)
   - Unilateral blindness → Species-dependent assessment; some buteos and vultures may compensate; accipiters and falcons require binocular vision for prey capture
   - Unilateral with intact vision in remaining eye → Consider flight testing and prey capture assessment before release decision

5. **Treatment pathway**
   - Anti-inflammatory therapy: topical and systemic NSAIDs or corticosteroids (species considerations for corticosteroid use)
   - Mydriatic/cycloplegic agents for anterior uveitis (atropine 1% topically)
   - Topical antibiotics if corneal ulceration present (avoid aminoglycosides in species sensitive to ototoxicity)
   - Reassess at 7, 14, and 28 days minimum

## Decision Tree 3: Marine Mammal Stranding

### Field Response

1. **Scene safety** — Assess hazards: surf conditions, crowd control, animal size and temperament, zoonotic risk (seal finger, leptospirosis, marine Brucella)

2. **Classify the stranding event**
   - Single live stranding
   - Single dead stranding (document and sample for mortality investigation)
   - Mass stranding event → Activate mass stranding response protocol; contact NOAA/regional stranding network

3. **Species identification and protected status** — All marine mammals in US waters are protected under the Marine Mammal Protection Act; additional protections may apply under ESA

4. **Live animal assessment**

   | Parameter | Evaluate |
   |---|---|
   | Mentation | Alert, responsive, obtunded, moribund |
   | Respiration | Rate, effort, abnormal sounds |
   | Body condition | Visible ribs, spinal processes, temporal fossa wasting |
   | Skin | Lesions, rake marks, entanglement injuries, sun damage |
   | Mobility | Can the animal move; is it hauled out appropriately for species |
   | Environmental context | Tide cycle, water temperature, time of day |

5. **Decision pathway for live pinnipeds**
   - Healthy-appearing pup hauled out → Likely normal behavior; monitor from distance; do NOT relocate
   - Emaciated, lethargic, or injured → Contact authorized stranding responder; provide shade and keep observers at distance
   - Entangled → Do not attempt disentanglement without training and authorization

6. **Decision pathway for live cetaceans**
   - Small cetacean on beach → Keep skin moist; support upright; avoid covering blowhole; minimize noise; contact stranding network immediately
   - Large cetacean on beach → Human safety is primary concern; do not attempt to push animal; contact stranding network for heavy equipment and veterinary support
   - Refloatable animal → Assess body condition and injuries before refloat attempt; satellite tag if possible

7. **Sample collection from dead animals**
   - Standard samples: morphometrics, photographs, skin/blubber biopsy, blood (cardiac if fresh dead), swabs (blowhole, rectum, lesions), stomach contents, parasites
   - Archive frozen tissue for contaminant analysis and genetics

## Decision Tree 4: Turtle with Shell Fracture

### Assessment

1. **Species identification** — Box turtle, aquatic turtle, tortoise, or sea turtle (different regulations and biology for each)

2. **Fracture classification**
   - **Location**: Carapace (dorsal), plastron (ventral), bridge
   - **Severity**: Hairline crack, displaced fracture, missing shell fragments
   - **Contamination**: Clean, contaminated, grossly infected
   - **Coelomic exposure**: Is the coelomic cavity open to the environment?

3. **Assessment of underlying damage**
   - Radiographs (DV and lateral/horizontal beam)
   - Assess for spinal involvement (sensation and motor function of limbs and tail)
   - Evaluate for internal hemorrhage (coelomic fluid analysis if cavity is exposed)
   - Check cloacal tone and function

4. **Prognosis determination**

   | Finding | Prognosis |
   |---|---|
   | Simple carapace crack, no displacement, no coelomic exposure | Good |
   | Displaced fracture with coelomic exposure, no spinal damage | Fair with aggressive treatment |
   | Spinal cord transection (hind limb paralysis, loss of cloacal tone) | Grave; euthanasia recommended |
   | Massive shell loss (>30% of carapace or plastron) | Poor to grave |
   | Fracture with maggot infestation but viable tissue | Fair if debrided and treated promptly |

5. **Treatment pathway**
   - Clean and debride wound; remove necrotic tissue and foreign material
   - Manage coelomic exposure: flush with sterile saline; temporary coverage with sterile adhesive drape
   - Fracture repair options: epoxy/fiberglass patches, zip ties, orthopedic wire, surgical plates for large species
   - Systemic antibiotics (ceftazidime 20 mg/kg IM q72h or enrofloxacin 5–10 mg/kg IM q24h)
   - Analgesia (meloxicam 0.2 mg/kg IM or SC q24–48h)
   - Housing: dry dock with access to shallow water for hydration; appropriate temperature gradient
   - Healing timeline: Shell fractures require months to years for complete repair; set realistic expectations

## Decision Tree 5: Mammal with Suspected Rabies Exposure

### Critical Safety Protocol

1. **Human safety is the absolute priority** — Do NOT handle any mammal exhibiting neurological signs without appropriate PPE (heavy gloves, face protection, long sleeves)

2. **Assess the scenario**
   - Was a human or domestic animal bitten or exposed to saliva from the suspect animal?
     - YES → This is a public health emergency; contact local health department immediately
     - NO → Proceed with wildlife assessment

3. **Evaluate clinical signs**

   Signs consistent with rabies in mammals:
   - Behavioral changes (nocturnal animals active during day, loss of fear of humans)
   - Aggression or unusual tameness
   - Excessive salivation, difficulty swallowing
   - Ataxia, circling, paresis progressing to paralysis
   - Self-mutilation
   - Vocalization changes

4. **Differential diagnoses for neurological signs in mammals**
   - Rabies
   - Canine distemper virus (in carnivores, raccoons, mustelids)
   - Lead or other heavy metal toxicosis
   - Organophosphate/carbamate poisoning
   - Head trauma
   - Cerebrospinal nematodiasis (e.g., *Baylisascaris* larva migrans)
   - Toxoplasmosis (in marsupials and some other species)

5. **Decision pathway**
   - If human exposure has occurred → Animal must be confined or euthanized for rabies testing per local health authority guidelines; do NOT release
   - If no human exposure and animal is suffering → Euthanasia with brain preservation for rabies testing (do NOT damage the brain; use approved methods such as intracardiac injection of barbiturate under sedation)
   - If no human exposure and signs are mild/ambiguous → Confine and observe if safe to do so; consult state veterinarian or public health authority
   - **Never vaccinate and release** a wild mammal with suspected rabies; pre- or post-exposure vaccination does not rule out active infection

6. **Sample submission**
   - Ship the intact head (or whole animal if small) on ice (NOT frozen) to an approved rabies testing laboratory
   - Follow chain-of-custody procedures if the case has public health or legal implications

## Decision Tree 6: Orphaned Neonate

### Verification of Orphan Status

1. **Is the animal truly orphaned?**
   - Many species leave young unattended for extended periods (deer fawns, rabbit kits, fledgling birds)
   - Unless the parent is confirmed dead or the young is in immediate danger, the default action is to **leave the animal and monitor**

2. **Species-specific norms**

   | Species Group | Normal Parental Absence | Action |
   |---|---|---|
   | Deer fawns | Up to 12 hours between feedings | Leave unless injured or in immediate danger |
   | Rabbit kits | Mother visits nest only at dawn and dusk | Leave; check nest the next day |
   | Fledgling songbirds | On the ground but parents nearby | Leave unless in danger from cats/traffic |
   | Nestling songbirds | Featherless, eyes closed | Return to nest if possible; if nest destroyed, create substitute |
   | Raptor chicks | Parents may be away hunting | Monitor from a distance for several hours |
   | Marine mammal pups | Variable; seal mothers forage for hours | Do not approach; monitor for 24+ hours |

3. **If confirmed orphaned, assess condition**
   - Hydration status, body temperature, body condition, injuries
   - Hypothermic or dehydrated neonates require stabilization before feeding

4. **Initial care pathway**
   - Warm slowly to species-appropriate temperature
   - Rehydrate with warmed oral or subcutaneous fluids before offering any nutritional support
   - Do NOT feed cow's milk to any wildlife species
   - Use species-appropriate milk replacer formulas (consult NWRA or IWRC guidelines)
   - Minimize human contact and imprinting risk, especially in raptors and mammals destined for release

5. **Long-term planning**
   - Transfer to licensed rehabilitator as soon as possible
   - Ensure conspecific socialization when available
   - Document all care for regulatory compliance

## Decision Tree 7: Oiled Bird

### Stabilization Before Washing

1. **Do NOT wash the bird immediately** — Stabilization is the priority; washing an unstable bird dramatically increases mortality

2. **Initial stabilization (24–48 hours minimum before washing)**
   - Thermoregulation: Maintain ambient temperature at 85–90 F (29–32 C) for waterbirds
   - Rehydration: Oral or subcutaneous fluids (avoid IV in severely compromised birds)
   - Nutritional support: Gavage feed with appropriate liquid diet once hydrated
   - GI decontamination: Administer activated charcoal (if ingestion suspected and bird can tolerate oral dosing) or provide access to water to encourage natural passage
   - Stress reduction: Quiet, dark enclosure; minimize handling

3. **Pre-wash assessment**
   - Is the bird stable enough for the washing process (1–2 hours of handling)?
   - PCV > 25%, TP > 3.0 g/dL, hydration adequate, temperature normal
   - If not → Continue stabilization; reassess daily

4. **Washing protocol**
   - Use 1–2% solution of dishwashing detergent (Dawn or equivalent) in water at 104 F (40 C)
   - Work systematically from head to tail, with the grain of the feathers
   - Multiple wash cycles may be needed until water beads off feathers
   - Rinse thoroughly with clean water at the same temperature until no detergent residue remains

5. **Post-wash recovery**
   - Dry under warm air flow; do not towel dry vigorously
   - Provide heated pool access for preening and natural waterproofing restoration
   - Monitor waterproofing daily: bird should float high and dry
   - Do NOT release until waterproofing is fully restored (bird can float for extended period without becoming wet to the skin)

6. **Release criteria**
   - Full waterproofing restored
   - Normal body weight and condition
   - Blood values within normal limits
   - Active, alert, and feeding independently
   - Appropriate weather and environmental conditions at release site

## General Principles Across All Decision Frameworks

- **Document every decision point** and the rationale for the path chosen
- **Reassess frequently** — Initial assessments may miss problems that become apparent over time
- **Communicate with stakeholders** — Wildlife agencies, rehabilitation networks, and the public may all have legitimate interests in case outcomes
- **Know your limits** — Refer to specialists or better-equipped facilities when a case exceeds your resources or expertise
- **Consider population-level implications** — Individual treatment decisions should be informed by the species' conservation status, disease ecology, and the potential for released animals to contribute to or detract from wild populations
