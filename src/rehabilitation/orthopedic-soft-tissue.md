# Orthopedic and Soft Tissue Surgery

## Overview

Surgical intervention is frequently required in wildlife rehabilitation for fracture repair, wound management, and correction of anatomical defects. The wildlife surgeon must balance the imperative for precise anatomical and functional restoration with the practical constraints of working with wild patients. Surgical outcomes in wildlife must meet a higher functional standard than in companion animals: the patient must be capable of full, unassisted survival in the wild.

## Fracture Classification

### Classification Systems

Fractures in wildlife patients are classified by the same criteria used in companion animal surgery, with additional emphasis on features that affect the prognosis for return to wild function.

| Criterion | Categories | Prognostic Significance |
|-----------|-----------|------------------------|
| Skin integrity | Open (compound) vs. Closed (simple) | Open fractures have higher infection risk and worse prognosis |
| Fragment number | Simple (two fragments), Comminuted (three or more fragments) | Comminuted fractures are technically harder to repair and heal less predictably |
| Location | Diaphyseal, metaphyseal, epiphyseal/articular | Articular fractures compromise joint function; poor prognosis in most wildlife |
| Displacement | Non-displaced, displaced, overriding | Displaced fractures require surgical reduction |
| Bone involved | Weight-bearing (femur, tibia, humerus) vs. Non-weight-bearing | Weight-bearing bone fractures in large animals carry guarded prognosis |
| Chronicity | Acute (<72 hours), Subacute (3–14 days), Chronic (>14 days) | Chronic fractures with established callus, contracture, or infection are difficult to repair |

## Fracture Management Methods

### Intramedullary Pinning (IM Pin)

- **Indication**: simple, transverse or short oblique diaphyseal fractures
- **Technique**: introduce a Steinmann pin or Kirschner wire retrograde or normograde through the medullary canal to align and stabilize fragments
- **Advantages**: relatively simple, quick, good rotational control when combined with cerclage wire
- **Disadvantages**: poor rotational stability alone; not suitable for comminuted fractures without supplemental fixation
- **Wildlife applications**: commonly used in avian humeral and femoral fractures; mammalian tibial and femoral fractures

### External Skeletal Fixation (ESF)

- **Indication**: open fractures, comminuted fractures, fractures with soft tissue loss
- **Configuration types**:
  - Type I: pins on one side of the bone, connected by a single bar
  - Type II: pins through both cortices, connected by bars on both sides
  - Type III: combination of Type I and II (maximal stability)
  - Tie-in configuration: combines IM pin with ESF (IM pin serves as one element of the fixation)
- **Advantages**: minimally invasive application, preserves blood supply to fracture site, allows staged removal as healing progresses
- **Disadvantages**: pin tract infection, external apparatus may snag on enclosure features, requires patient compliance
- **Wildlife applications**: widely used in avian and mammalian fractures; the standard of care for open and comminuted fractures

### External Coaptation (Splints and Bandages)

- **Indication**: distal limb fractures, fractures in very small patients, stabilization for transport, supplementary stabilization after pin/ESF
- **Types**:
  - Modified Robert Jones bandage — heavily padded compression bandage for temporary stabilization
  - Spica splint — figure-of-eight wrap incorporating the body wall for wing fractures in birds
  - Ball bandage — encases the foot and digits; used for pododermatitis or toe fractures
  - Schroeder-Thomas splint — traction splint for distal limb fractures in small mammals
  - Custom thermoplastic splints — moldable to specific anatomical requirements
- **Advantages**: non-invasive, inexpensive, no anesthesia required for application in some cases
- **Disadvantages**: limited indications, risk of pressure sores, difficult to maintain in active wild animals, requires frequent monitoring and changes

### Bone Plating

- **Indication**: articular fractures, complex comminuted fractures requiring precise anatomical reduction
- **Technique**: open approach, anatomical reduction, fixation with bone plates and screws
- **Advantages**: rigid fixation, anatomical alignment, good functional outcomes when performed correctly
- **Disadvantages**: invasive, requires extensive surgical exposure, implant removal may be needed, expensive
- **Wildlife applications**: less commonly used due to technical demands and cost; reserved for high-value species or cases where other methods are inadequate

## Wing Fractures in Birds

Wing fractures are among the most common and consequential injuries in avian rehabilitation. The wing must heal with near-perfect alignment and full range of motion for flight to be restored.

### Prognostic Guidelines by Location

| Fracture Site | Prognosis for Flight | Preferred Repair Method |
|--------------|---------------------|----------------------|
| Mid-shaft humerus | Fair to Good | IM pin ± cerclage; ESF |
| Proximal humerus | Poor | ESF; often non-releasable |
| Distal humerus / elbow | Poor | Challenging repair; ESF with transarticular fixation |
| Mid-shaft radius/ulna | Good (if one bone intact) | Splint if one bone intact; ESF if both fractured |
| Carpometacarpus | Fair | ESF or external coaptation |
| Phalanges | Good | External coaptation, buddy taping |

### Key Principles for Avian Fracture Repair

- Use the lightest fixation that provides adequate stability; heavy implants impair flight
- Preserve periosteal blood supply; avoid excessive soft tissue stripping
- Avian bone is pneumatized (hollow and air-filled) in some species; IM pins must be sized to avoid cortical blowout
- Post-operative physical therapy (passive range of motion) is essential to prevent joint stiffness and tendon adhesions
- Flight testing is mandatory before release (see Release Criteria chapter)

## Shell Repair in Chelonians

Shell fractures are common in chelonians, primarily from vehicle strikes, predator attacks (dogs, raccoons), and lawnmower injuries.

### Assessment

- Evaluate the extent of shell damage: carapace (dorsal), plastron (ventral), or both
- Determine if the coelomic cavity is exposed (open shell fracture — surgical emergency)
- Assess for spinal cord involvement: loss of hindlimb function indicates spinal damage and carries a poor prognosis
- Radiograph to identify internal organ damage, retained shell fragments, and spinal integrity

### Repair Methods

| Method | Indication | Technique |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| Epoxy/fiberglass patch | Small, clean defects without coelomic exposure | Clean, debride; apply fiberglass cloth with epoxy resin over the defect |
| Cable tie or wire fixation | Displaced but reducible fracture fragments | Drill holes on either side of the fracture line; reduce and fix with stainless steel wire or cable ties |
| Zip-tie bridge | Multiple mobile fragments | Connect adjacent fragments with zip ties threaded through drill holes |
| Bone plate (mini/micro plates) | Complex fractures requiring rigid fixation | Screws into shell bone; plate across fracture lines |
| Wound management only | Large defects with tissue loss | Wet-to-dry bandaging or negative-pressure therapy until granulation; long-term healing by second intention |

### Healing Timeline

- Shell fractures heal slowly: 12–18 months for full bone union
- Interim releases are possible once the coelomic cavity is sealed and the animal is otherwise functional
- Monitor for signs of infection (discharge, odor, discoloration) throughout the healing period

## Wound Management

### Wound Assessment

- **Age of wound** — fresh (<6 hours), subacute (6–24 hours), chronic (>24 hours)
- **Contamination level** — clean, contaminated, infected (see Triage chapter for classification)
- **Tissue viability** — assess perfusion, color, and integrity of wound margins
- **Underlying damage** — palpate for fractures, tendon disruption, joint involvement

### Wet-to-Dry Bandaging

A standard debridement technique for contaminated and infected wounds.

1. Irrigate the wound thoroughly with sterile saline or dilute chlorhexidine (0.05%)
2. Apply a sterile gauze moistened with saline directly to the wound bed
3. Cover with dry gauze, then a conforming bandage layer, then a protective outer layer
4. Change the bandage every 12–24 hours; the inner wet layer dries and adheres to necrotic tissue and debris, which is removed (mechanically debrided) when the bandage is changed
5. Continue until the wound bed is clean and granulation tissue is present (typically 3–7 days)
6. Transition to non-adherent dressings once the wound is clean and granulating

### Negative Pressure Wound Therapy (VAC Therapy)

- **Indication**: large wounds with tissue loss, wounds requiring granulation tissue formation, surgical site dehiscence
- **Technique**: place an open-cell foam dressing into the wound cavity, seal with adhesive drape, and connect to a continuous or intermittent suction device at -75 to -125 mmHg
- **Advantages**: promotes granulation tissue, reduces edema, mechanically removes exudate, improves local blood flow
- **Challenges in wildlife**: requires patient compliance and secure dressing application; may require sedation for dressing changes; modified portable vacuum systems can be adapted for wildlife use
- **Duration**: change dressings every 48–72 hours; continue until the wound bed is ready for closure or has healed by second intention

### Primary Closure

- Reserved for clean, fresh wounds with adequate tissue for tension-free closure
- Debride wound margins minimally to create fresh edges
- Close in layers if deep tissue involvement is present
- Use absorbable suture (Monocryl, PDS) for subcutaneous and fascial layers; nylon or staples for skin
- Avoid excessive tension; consider relaxing incisions or advancement flaps for large defects

## Abscess Management

### Mammalian Abscesses

- Typically contain liquid pus; lance, drain, and flush with dilute chlorhexidine or saline
- Establish drainage (leave wound open or place a Penrose drain for 3–5 days)
- Culture and sensitivity testing guides antibiotic selection
- Systemic antibiotics if cellulitis or systemic infection is present

### Reptilian Abscesses

- Reptilian abscesses contain caseous (cheese-like, solid) exudate that does not drain passively
- Surgical excision of the entire abscess capsule and contents is required
- Flush the cavity with dilute chlorhexidine
- Pack with antibiotic-impregnated beads (optional) or allow to heal by second intention
- Systemic antibiotics: guided by culture; empirical therapy with ceftazidime (20 mg/kg IM every 72 hours) or enrofloxacin (5–10 mg/kg IM every 24–48 hours)

## Ophthalmic Conditions

Eye injuries are common in wildlife, particularly from trauma (window strikes, vehicle collisions) and infectious diseases.

### Common Presentations

| Condition | Species Commonly Affected | Management |
|-----------|--------------------------|------------|
| Corneal ulcer | All taxa | Fluorescein stain to confirm; topical antibiotic (ofloxacin, ciprofloxacin) every 6–8 hours; atropine 1% for ciliary spasm; E-collar or tape eyelid if self-trauma risk |
| Corneal foreign body | Birds (especially raptors) | Remove under magnification with fine forceps or needle tip; treat as for corneal ulcer afterward |
| Hyphema (blood in anterior chamber) | Trauma cases across taxa | Anti-inflammatory therapy (topical prednisolone if cornea is intact; systemic meloxicam); monitor for secondary glaucoma |
| Lens luxation | Trauma cases, especially raptors | Surgical extraction if causing secondary complications; enucleation if chronic |
| Proptosis (globe prolapse) | Small mammals, birds | Immediate replacement if viable; enucleation if globe is ruptured or optic nerve is avulsed |
| Uveitis | Infectious diseases, trauma | Topical anti-inflammatory, mydriatic; treat underlying cause |
| Phthisis bulbi (shrunken globe) | End-stage ocular disease | Non-functional eye; enucleation for comfort |

### Prognostic Considerations

- Unilateral blindness may be compatible with release in some species (owls are a notable exception; binocular vision is critical for their hunting strategy)
- Bilateral blindness is a non-releasable condition in virtually all species
- Assess visual function with menace response, pupillary light reflex, tracking behavior, and obstacle avoidance testing
- Ophthalmic conditions require frequent re-evaluation; what appears to be a minor injury may progress to globe-threatening disease

## Postoperative Care

### Pain Management

| Drug | Dose Range | Route | Duration | Species Considerations |
|------|-----------|-------|----------|----------------------|
| Meloxicam | 0.1–0.5 mg/kg | PO, IM, SC | Every 24 hours | Widely used across taxa; avoid in dehydrated or renally compromised animals |
| Buprenorphine | 0.01–0.03 mg/kg | IM, SC | Every 8–12 hours | Mammals; partial mu-agonist; provides good somatic analgesia |
| Tramadol | 2–10 mg/kg | PO | Every 8–12 hours | Mammals and birds; variable efficacy between species |
| Butorphanol | 0.5–2 mg/kg | IM | Every 4–6 hours | Birds (excellent visceral analgesic); mammals |
| Gabapentin | 5–10 mg/kg | PO | Every 12–24 hours | Neuropathic pain; useful as multimodal adjunct |

### Activity Restriction

- Confine surgical patients to small, padded enclosures during the initial healing phase (2–4 weeks for most fractures)
- Prevent flight in avian patients until fracture healing is confirmed radiographically
- Gradually increase enclosure size and activity opportunities as healing progresses
- Begin controlled physical therapy (passive range of motion for joints, swimming for aquatic species) once surgical stability allows

### Monitoring

- Radiographic assessment of fracture healing every 2–3 weeks
- Monitor for implant complications: pin migration, pin tract infection, implant failure
- Monitor wound healing: infection, dehiscence, granulation progress
- Track body weight and body condition throughout the surgical recovery period
- Assess functional outcomes: range of motion, weight bearing, wing extension, flight ability
