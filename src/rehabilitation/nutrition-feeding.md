# Nutrition and Feeding

## Overview

Nutritional management is a cornerstone of wildlife rehabilitation. Malnutrition is a presenting factor in a significant proportion of wildlife admissions, and appropriate dietary support accelerates healing, prevents secondary complications, and prepares animals for the energetic demands of life in the wild. Species-specific dietary knowledge, understanding of life-stage requirements, and awareness of nutritional disorders are essential skills for the wildlife rehabilitator.

## Species-Specific Diets

### Insectivorous Birds (Swallows, Warblers, Wrens)

- **Staple diet**: commercially available insectivore diets (e.g., Mazuri Insectivore Diet), supplemented with live insects
- **Live prey**: mealworms, wax worms, crickets (gut-loaded with calcium-rich diets), small flies (Drosophila for very small species)
- **Caution**: mealworms alone are nutritionally deficient (high fat, low calcium); always supplement with calcium powder
- **Feeding frequency**: every 30–60 minutes for nestlings during daylight hours; 3–4 times daily for adults

### Raptors (Hawks, Eagles, Owls, Falcons)

- **Staple diet**: whole prey items — mice, rats, day-old chicks, quail
- **Whole prey advantages**: bones provide calcium, fur/feathers provide fiber for pellet formation, organ meats provide vitamins A and D
- **Avoid**: muscle meat alone (nutritionally incomplete), processed meats, human food
- **Feeding frequency**: once or twice daily for adults; 3–4 times daily for nestlings and branchers
- **Casting**: raptors produce pellets (castings) of indigestible material; monitor pellet production as an indicator of normal GI function

### Waterbirds (Ducks, Geese, Pelicans, Herons)

- **Herbivorous species (geese, swans)**: chopped leafy greens, aquatic vegetation, waterfowl pellets
- **Piscivorous species (herons, pelicans, cormorants)**: whole fish (smelt, silversides, capelin); supplement with thiamine (vitamin B1) 25 mg/kg fish to prevent thiamine deficiency
- **Dabbling ducks**: mixed grain, waterfowl pellets, chopped greens, duckweed
- **Diving ducks**: aquatic invertebrates, small fish, waterfowl pellets

### Herbivorous Mammals (Deer, Rabbits, Herbivorous Marsupials)

- **Ruminants**: high-quality grass hay (timothy, orchard grass), supplemented with browse (native browse species preferred); avoid grain-heavy diets that cause acidosis
- **Lagomorphs**: unlimited grass hay, fresh leafy greens, commercial rabbit pellets in controlled amounts
- **Macropods**: grass hay, kangaroo/wallaby pellets, browse; avoid high-sugar fruits

### Carnivorous Mammals (Foxes, Mustelids, Small Felids)

- **Staple**: whole prey (mice, rats, chicks), commercially balanced carnivore diets
- **Supplements**: taurine supplementation for felids if not receiving whole prey
- **Avoid**: raw pork (risk of Aujeszky's disease), raw salmon/trout from Pacific coast (salmon poisoning in canids)
- **Feeding frequency**: once or twice daily for adults

### Reptiles

- **Insectivorous lizards**: gut-loaded crickets, dubia roaches, mealworms; dusted with calcium + vitamin D3 powder
- **Herbivorous lizards (iguanas, uromastyx)**: dark leafy greens (collard, mustard, dandelion greens), squash, limited fruit; avoid lettuce (nutritionally poor) and spinach (oxalate binder)
- **Snakes**: appropriately sized whole prey (mice, rats); frozen-thawed is safer and more practical than live prey
- **Chelonians**: herbivorous species — grasses, weeds, leafy greens, hay; omnivorous species — add invertebrates, commercial turtle pellets
- **Crocodilians**: whole prey (fish, mice, rats, chicks) appropriate to body size

### Amphibians

- **Frogs and toads**: live insects — crickets, mealworms, roaches, earthworms; dusted with calcium powder
- **Aquatic amphibians (axolotls, aquatic newts)**: bloodworms, tubifex, small fish, commercial amphibian pellets
- **Caution**: amphibians are particularly sensitive to vitamin A deficiency; supplement with beta-carotene or retinol periodically

## Neonatal and Orphan Feeding

### Mammalian Neonates

#### Formula Selection

| Species Group | Recommended Formula | Protein (%) | Fat (%) |
|--------------|-------------------|-------------|---------|
| Canids (foxes, coyotes) | Esbilac (puppy milk replacer) | 33 | 40 |
| Felids (bobcats, lynx) | KMR (kitten milk replacer) | 42 | 25 |
| Cervids (deer fawns) | Zoologic 33/40, Fox Valley 33/40 | 33 | 40 |
| Lagomorphs (rabbits, hares) | Fox Valley 32/40, Multi-Milk | 32 | 40 |
| Sciurids (squirrels) | Fox Valley 32/40, Esbilac | 32–33 | 40 |
| Macropods (joeys) | Wombaroo kangaroo milk (stage-specific) | Stage-variable | Stage-variable |
| Pinnipeds (seal pups) | Fish-based slurry, Zoologic 33/40 with herring oil | 33 | 50+ |

#### Feeding Schedules

| Age Category | Feeding Interval | Volume Per Feed |
|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Newborn (eyes closed) | Every 2–3 hours (including nighttime feeds for the first week) | 3–5% body weight per feed |
| Eyes opening | Every 3–4 hours | 5–7% body weight per feed |
| Active, mobile | Every 4–6 hours | 7–10% body weight per feed |
| Weaning | 3–4 times daily with solid food introduction | Reduce to 5% body weight; supplement with solids |

#### Technique

- Warm formula to 37–39°C before feeding
- Use species-appropriate nipples or syringes: elongated nipples for ungulates, small syringes for rodents, catamaran-style nipples for marsupial joeys
- Feed with the animal in a natural feeding position (ventral recumbency for carnivores, standing/sternal for ungulates; **never** on the back)
- Stimulate urination and defecation after each feed (warm damp cloth to perineum) for very young mammals that cannot eliminate independently
- Weigh daily; neonates should gain 5–10% body weight per day

### Avian Neonates

- **Altricial species (passerines, raptors)**: hand-feed with blunt-tipped forceps or syringes
- **Passerines**: commercial hand-rearing formula (Kaytee Exact, Mazuri), mixed to a smooth consistency; feed every 20–30 minutes during daylight hours for hatchlings, extending to every 45–60 minutes as the bird grows
- **Raptors**: finely diced whole prey (pinkie mice initially, progressing to larger items); supplement with calcium
- **Precocial species (ducks, quail, shorebirds)**: self-feeding from day one; provide appropriate-sized food (crumbled chick starter, finely chopped greens, small invertebrates) in shallow dishes; ensure constant access to water

## Refeeding Syndrome Prevention

Refeeding syndrome is a life-threatening metabolic derangement that occurs when severely malnourished animals are refed too aggressively. The sudden shift from catabolic to anabolic metabolism causes rapid intracellular uptake of phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium, leading to acute electrolyte depletion.

### Risk Factors

- BCS 1 or 1.5 (severe emaciation)
- Starvation of more than 3–5 days
- Chronic wasting diseases
- Very young or very old animals

### Clinical Signs

- Lethargy, weakness, and tremors within 12–72 hours of refeeding
- Cardiac arrhythmias and acute cardiac failure
- Respiratory distress
- Seizures
- Hemolytic anemia
- Death

### Prevention Protocol

1. **Rehydrate first** — correct dehydration with oral or subcutaneous fluids before introducing food (12–24 hours of fluid therapy)
2. **Start slowly** — begin at 25–50% of the calculated caloric requirement on day 1
3. **Increase gradually** — increase by 10–15% of the target daily over 3–5 days until full caloric requirements are met
4. **Electrolyte supplementation** — supplement phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium during the refeeding period
5. **Thiamine supplementation** — administer vitamin B1 (thiamine) 1–2 mg/kg IM before and during refeeding (thiamine is a critical cofactor in carbohydrate metabolism)
6. **Monitor** — weigh daily; monitor behavior, fecal output, and hydration; blood electrolytes if available

## Diet Transition Protocols

### Captivity to Wild Diet

Animals in rehabilitation must be transitioned to a diet that closely resembles their natural diet before release.

- **Raptors**: transition from hand-fed food to self-caught live prey in a flight cage before release
- **Insectivores**: shift from commercial diets to live, wild-type insect species available in the release environment
- **Herbivores**: introduce native browse, grasses, and forbs from the intended release site during the pre-release phase
- **Omnivores**: provide a diverse diet including naturally foraged components alongside commercial foods; gradually reduce commercial components

### Weaning Protocol for Orphans

1. Begin introducing solid food at the species-appropriate weaning age
2. Offer solid food before each formula feeding to encourage exploration
3. Gradually reduce formula volume and frequency over 1–2 weeks
4. Ensure adequate solid food intake before discontinuing formula entirely
5. Monitor body weight throughout; any decline requires reassessment of the weaning timeline

## Natural Food Procurement

- Cultivate native plants and grasses on facility grounds for browse and forage
- Collect wild-type insects from pesticide-free environments
- Maintain mealworm, cricket, or dubia roach colonies for consistent insect supply
- Source whole prey (mice, rats) from reputable commercial breeders; frozen-thawed whole prey is a safe and practical option
- Fish: source from verified suppliers; avoid fish from contaminated waterways; supplement with thiamine as noted above
- Document the source and storage conditions of all food items

## Prey Item Sourcing

### Live Prey for Pre-Release Conditioning

Certain species (particularly raptors and carnivorous mammals) benefit from live prey exposure before release to maintain or develop hunting skills.

- **Ethical requirements**: minimize suffering of prey items; use methods approved by institutional ethics committees
- **Species**: live mice, rats, or fish offered in enrichment settings within the enclosure
- **Assessment**: the animal's ability to locate, pursue, and dispatch live prey is a key release criterion
- **Documentation**: record the date, prey type, and the animal's behavioral response

### Prey Nutritional Enhancement

| Enhancement Method | Procedure | Purpose |
|-------------------|-----------|---------|
| Gut loading | Feed prey insects/mice a calcium-rich, vitamin-fortified diet 24–48 hours before use | Increases calcium and micronutrient content of the prey item |
| Dusting | Coat prey items with calcium/vitamin D3 powder immediately before feeding | Supplements calcium for species at risk of metabolite bone disease |
| Thiamine injection | Inject fish with 25 mg/kg thiamine before feeding to piscivores | Prevents thiamine deficiency in fish-eating species |
| Variety | Rotate prey species to provide a balanced nutrient profile | Prevents dietary imbalances from single-prey diets |

## Hydration

Adequate hydration is a prerequisite for successful nutritional management.

- Assess hydration status at intake: skin turgor, mucous membrane moisture, eye position (sunken eyes indicate dehydration)
- Estimate dehydration percentage: mild (3–5%), moderate (5–8%), severe (8–12%)
- Replacement volume: body weight (kg) × dehydration percentage × 1000 = mL deficit
- Replace deficit over 24–48 hours via subcutaneous, oral, or intravenous routes depending on severity
- Maintenance fluid requirement: approximately 50–80 mL/kg/day for most species (higher for small species, birds, and reptiles)
- Provide fresh water ad libitum in all enclosures; ensure water dishes are appropriate to the species (shallow for small birds and amphibians, deep for waterfowl)
