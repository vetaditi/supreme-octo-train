# Triage and Assessment

## Overview

Triage is the process of rapidly evaluating incoming wildlife patients to prioritize treatment, allocate resources, and determine prognosis. Effective triage prevents the expenditure of limited resources on non-viable cases while ensuring that animals with good prognoses receive timely care. The wildlife rehabilitator must balance compassion with pragmatism, making difficult decisions grounded in clinical evidence and species biology.

## Initial Assessment Protocol

### Scene Assessment and History

Before handling the animal, gather as much information as possible:

- **Circumstances of rescue** — how, when, and where was the animal found?
- **Duration of the problem** — acute (hours) versus chronic (days to weeks) presentations affect prognosis
- **Observed behavior before capture** — was the animal mobile, alert, responsive to stimuli?
- **Known hazards at the site** — road trauma, cat attack, window strike, oil spill, electrocution, toxin exposure
- **Other animals involved** — clutch mates, den mates, or flock members that may also need assistance
- **Contact with domestic animals or humans** — relevant to disease risk and behavioral assessment

### Hands-On Examination

Perform a systematic physical examination as soon as the animal is safely restrained. Minimize handling time to reduce stress.

#### Rapid Assessment (First 60 Seconds)

1. **Responsiveness** — alert, depressed, obtunded, or comatose
2. **Breathing** — rate, depth, pattern; open-mouth breathing indicates respiratory distress in most species
3. **Hemorrhage** — visible active bleeding requiring immediate intervention
4. **Posture** — standing, sternal, lateral; head tilt, limb asymmetry, wing droop
5. **Obvious injuries** — fractures, wounds, burns, missing limbs

#### Systematic Examination

| System | Assessment Parameters |
|--------|----------------------|
| Cardiovascular | Heart rate, pulse quality, mucous membrane color, CRT |
| Respiratory | Rate, depth, pattern, lung sounds, air sac sounds (birds) |
| Neurological | Mentation, pupillary light reflex, cranial nerve function, withdrawal reflexes, proprioception |
| Musculoskeletal | Palpation of long bones, joints, spine; range of motion; crepitus; wing symmetry (birds) |
| Integument | Wounds, ectoparasites, feather/fur condition, dehydration (skin turgor) |
| Eyes | Pupil size and symmetry, corneal clarity, fundic exam if possible, fluorescein stain |
| Oral cavity | Mucous membrane color, dental condition, foreign bodies, trichomoniasis lesions (raptors, pigeons) |
| Abdomen | Palpation for masses, organomegaly, fluid, pain response |
| Cloaca/perineum | Soiling (diarrhea), prolapse, parasites, reproductive status |

## Body Condition Scoring

Body condition score (BCS) provides a standardized assessment of nutritional status and is one of the most important prognostic indicators.

### Mammalian BCS (1–5 Scale)

| Score | Description | Interpretation |
|-------|-------------|----------------|
| 1 | Emaciated: prominent skeletal structures, no palpable fat, severe muscle wasting | Poor prognosis; chronic debilitation; refeeding syndrome risk |
| 2 | Thin: easily palpable skeletal structures, minimal fat cover, some muscle wasting | Guarded prognosis; requires nutritional rehabilitation |
| 3 | Normal: skeletal structures palpable with moderate fat cover, good muscle mass | Expected for a healthy animal; good prognostic indicator |
| 4 | Overweight: skeletal structures difficult to palpate, moderate fat deposits | Uncommon in wild animals; may indicate captive-held or urban-adapted animal |
| 5 | Obese: skeletal structures not palpable, extensive fat deposits | Rare in wild animals; may complicate anesthesia and recovery |

### Avian BCS (1–5 Scale, Based on Keel Prominence)

| Score | Keel Assessment | Pectoral Muscle |
|-------|----------------|-----------------|
| 1 | Keel very prominent, sharp, protruding | Severely atrophied; concave on either side of keel |
| 2 | Keel prominent, easily palpable | Reduced; keel protrudes above muscle surface |
| 3 | Keel palpable, level with pectoral muscles | Normal fullness; convex muscle contour |
| 4 | Keel partially obscured by muscle/fat | Well-developed; difficult to distinguish keel edge |
| 5 | Keel not palpable | Excessive muscle/fat; unusual in wild birds |

## Injury Classification

### Wound Classification

| Class | Description | Examples |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| Class I (Clean) | Uninfected, no inflammation, closed primarily | Surgical incisions, fresh lacerations |
| Class II (Clean-contaminated) | Minor contamination, minimal inflammation | Recent bite wounds before infection, minor abrasions |
| Class III (Contaminated) | Open wounds with moderate contamination | Cat-caught wildlife, road trauma with soil contamination |
| Class IV (Dirty/Infected) | Established infection, devitalized tissue, pus | Chronic wounds, maggot-infested injuries, abscesses |

### Fracture Classification

- **Open vs. closed** — open fractures communicate with the external environment and carry a higher infection risk
- **Simple vs. comminuted** — comminuted fractures (multiple fragments) carry a worse prognosis
- **Location** — mid-shaft fractures are generally more amenable to repair than articular or periarticular fractures
- **Age** — fresh fractures have better prognosis than chronic fractures with established callus, muscle contracture, or infection

## Prognosis Determination

### Favorable Prognostic Indicators

- BCS 2.5 or higher
- Acute injury (less than 48 hours)
- Clean or clean-contaminated wound
- Simple, closed fracture in a favorable location
- Alert mentation with normal neurological function
- Adequate hydration
- No evidence of systemic infection
- Species with established rehabilitation protocols and good documented outcomes

### Unfavorable Prognostic Indicators

- BCS 1 or 1.5 (severe emaciation)
- Chronic injury with established infection or muscle contracture
- Open, comminuted fractures of weight-bearing bones or wing joints (birds)
- Head trauma with persistent neurological deficits (circling, seizures, blindness)
- Bilateral eye injury (blindness in species reliant on vision)
- Evidence of systemic disease: septicemia, aspergillosis, advanced viral infections
- Spinal cord injury with paralysis
- Severe burns (>30% body surface area)
- Young animals that have imprinted on humans (non-releasable behavioral defect)

### Species-Specific Considerations

- **Raptors** — wing fractures involving the elbow or shoulder joint carry a poor prognosis; humeral fractures in large raptors rarely heal with adequate flight function
- **Waterbirds** — loss of waterproofing (oil contamination, feather damage) is a significant barrier to release; must be fully resolved before release
- **Marine mammals** — chronic malnutrition and parasitism in stranded pinnipeds carry guarded prognoses
- **Chelonians** — shell fractures, even extensive ones, often heal with appropriate management; good prognosis if no spinal cord involvement
- **Bats** — wing membrane tears require complete healing; any residual loss of membrane compromises flight

## Euthanasia Decision-Making

### When Euthanasia Is Appropriate

Euthanasia is a humane and ethical outcome when:

- The animal's injuries or disease preclude return to a functional life in the wild
- The animal is suffering and cannot be effectively treated
- Treatment would require a duration of captivity that is not in the animal's welfare interest
- The species has no conservation justification for intensive, long-term care
- Resources needed for treatment would compromise care of other patients with better prognoses

### Decision-Making Framework

1. **Can this animal be treated?** — Is treatment technically feasible with available resources?
2. **Can this animal recover fully?** — Will the animal regain all functions necessary for survival in the wild?
3. **Can this animal be released?** — Does the animal's behavior, physical condition, and disease status allow safe release?
4. **Is the expected duration of care proportionate?** — Months of captivity for a wild animal carries significant welfare and behavioral costs
5. **Are resources available?** — Does treating this animal prevent care of others with better outcomes?

### Methods

- Euthanasia must be performed by or under the direct supervision of a licensed veterinarian
- Acceptable methods: IV barbiturate overdose (pentobarbital 85–100 mg/kg IV), inhaled anesthetic overdose (isoflurane/CO₂)
- For field euthanasia when IV access is impractical: intracardiac pentobarbital (only in deeply anesthetized or unconscious animals), intracoelomic in reptiles and amphibians
- Physical methods (cervical dislocation, decapitation) are acceptable for small species when performed by trained personnel according to AVMA guidelines
- Document the reason for euthanasia, method used, and drugs administered

## Documentation Requirements

### Intake Record

Every animal admitted to rehabilitation must have a complete intake record including:

- Date and time of admission
- Species, age class (neonate, juvenile, subadult, adult), sex (if determinable)
- Body mass and body condition score
- Location found (GPS coordinates preferred)
- Circumstances of rescue and finder contact information
- Physical examination findings (systematic, as described above)
- Initial assessment: provisional diagnosis and prognosis
- Initial treatment plan or euthanasia decision with justification
- Photographs: dorsal, ventral, lateral views; close-up of injuries
- Unique patient identifier (case number)

### Ongoing Medical Record

- Daily observation notes: behavior, appetite, fecal output, wound status
- All treatments administered: drug, dose, route, time, administered by
- Diagnostic results: radiographs, blood work, culture results
- Surgical reports with detailed operative notes
- Anesthesia records
- Weight tracking (minimum weekly)
- Behavioral observations relevant to release assessment

### Outcome Record

- Date of outcome (release, death, euthanasia, transfer)
- Outcome category and details
- Release location (GPS coordinates) and method (soft vs. hard release)
- Post-release monitoring plan (if applicable)
- Final body mass and condition
- Band, tag, or transmitter identification numbers
