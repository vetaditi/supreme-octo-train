# Husbandry and Housing

## Overview

Appropriate husbandry and housing are foundational to successful wildlife rehabilitation. The captive environment must meet the species' physiological needs while minimizing stress, preventing habituation to humans, and promoting recovery of natural behaviors. Poor housing design is a common and preventable cause of secondary injury, prolonged rehabilitation, and failed outcomes.

## Species-Appropriate Enclosures

### Design Principles

- **Size** — enclosures must be large enough to allow species-typical locomotion (flight for birds, swimming for aquatic species, climbing for arboreal species)
- **Security** — prevent escape and exclude predators (including feral cats, raptors, and snakes)
- **Minimized human contact** — one-way viewing panels, visual barriers, and covered feeding stations reduce habituation
- **Progressive sizing** — move patients from small intensive care enclosures to larger pre-release enclosures as they recover
- **Easy cleaning** — surfaces should be non-porous, smooth, and resistant to disinfection chemicals

### Enclosure Types by Taxon

| Taxon | Intensive Care | Rehabilitation | Pre-Release |
|-------|---------------|----------------|-------------|
| Passerine birds | Small cloth-covered cage (30×30×30 cm) | Indoor aviary (2×2×2 m) | Outdoor flight aviary (6×3×3 m minimum) |
| Raptors | Padded box or ICU carrier; minimize visual stimulation | Mews (2×2×2.5 m); perching at multiple heights | Flight cage (30×6×4 m minimum); live prey testing |
| Waterbirds | Indoor pen with shallow water tray | Pool enclosure (access to swimming water) | Large pond enclosure with haul-out areas |
| Small mammals | Ventilated box or small cage with nest box | Larger cage with substrate and enrichment | Outdoor enclosure simulating natural habitat |
| Large mammals | Padded stall or crate | Paddock with shelter | Large natural enclosure; minimal human contact |
| Reptiles | Ventilated tub or terrarium with heat gradient | Species-appropriate vivarium with UV, heat, and hides | Outdoor enclosure at appropriate season |
| Amphibians | Moist container with ventilation | Terrarium with water feature and land area | Outdoor enclosure near release site habitat |
| Marine mammals | Padded mat in quiet room (pinnipeds); pool access when stable | Graduated pool access; saltwater preferred | Seaside holding pen or transport to release site |

## Substrate

Substrate selection affects foot health, hygiene, and species comfort.

- **Raptors** — AstroTurf or coir matting over solid floors to prevent bumblefoot (pododermatitis); perches wrapped in coir rope or covered in AstroTurf
- **Waterbirds** — smooth, padded surfaces (foam mats, rubber matting) to prevent keel and hock lesions; access to water for natural foot care
- **Passerines** — newspaper or paper towel (ICU); natural branch perches at various diameters (rehabilitation)
- **Small mammals** — shredded paper, aspen shavings (avoid cedar and pine), or hay; nest boxes with appropriate bedding
- **Reptiles** — paper towel (ICU and quarantine); coconut coir, cypress mulch, or appropriate naturalistic substrate (rehabilitation)
- **Amphibians** — moist sphagnum moss, coco coir, or damp paper towels; substrate must be non-toxic and retain moisture without becoming waterlogged

## Temperature and Humidity

### Thermal Management

- Maintain enclosures within the species' thermoneutral zone
- Provide heat gradients rather than uniform temperatures (especially reptiles and neonates)
- Heat sources: ceramic heat emitters, radiant heat panels, heating pads (external to enclosure to prevent thermal burns)
- Cooling: evaporative coolers, misting systems, frozen water bottles, air conditioning

### Temperature Guidelines

| Taxon | ICU Temperature | Rehabilitation Temperature |
|-------|----------------|---------------------------|
| Neonatal mammals | 30–34°C (incubator) | Species-specific adult range |
| Adult mammals | 18–24°C (temperate species) | Outdoor ambient or climate-controlled |
| Passerine birds | 28–32°C (debilitated) | 18–24°C |
| Raptors | 18–22°C | Outdoor ambient |
| Tropical reptiles | 28–32°C (warm end) | 24–32°C gradient |
| Temperate reptiles | 24–28°C (warm end) | 20–28°C gradient |
| Amphibians | 18–24°C (most temperate species) | Species-specific |

### Humidity

- Most reptiles and amphibians: 50–80% relative humidity; some tropical species require 80–100%
- Marine mammals and waterbirds: keep skin/feathers moist; misting may be necessary
- Avoid excessively dry environments for all taxa; dehydration exacerbates many clinical conditions
- Monitor humidity with calibrated hygrometers in all reptile and amphibian enclosures

## Lighting

### Natural Photoperiod

- Maintain species-appropriate photoperiod to support circadian rhythms, immune function, and behavioral normality
- Use timers on artificial lighting to simulate natural day-night cycles
- Avoid continuous lighting in ICU settings; even debilitated animals benefit from dark periods

### Ultraviolet Radiation Requirements

UV-B radiation is essential for vitamin D3 synthesis and calcium metabolism in many reptile and some amphibian species.

| Species Group | UV-B Requirement | Recommended UV Index |
|--------------|------------------|---------------------|
| Diurnal lizards (bearded dragons, monitors, iguanas) | High | UVI 3–5 (basking zone) |
| Chelonians (most tortoises, freshwater turtles) | High | UVI 3–5 |
| Diurnal snakes | Moderate | UVI 2–3 |
| Nocturnal reptiles | Low | UVI 1–2 (shade zone equivalent) |
| Diurnal amphibians | Low-Moderate | UVI 1–3 |
| Nocturnal amphibians | Low | UVI 0–1 |

- Use commercial reptile UV-B fluorescent tubes or mercury vapor lamps
- Replace UV-B bulbs every 6–12 months (output declines before visible light dims)
- Ensure no glass or plastic between the lamp and the animal (these materials filter UV-B)
- Provide UV-exposed basking areas and shaded retreat areas simultaneously

## Noise Reduction

Wildlife in rehabilitation are highly susceptible to noise-induced stress, which delays healing and promotes immunosuppression.

### Strategies

- Locate enclosures away from high-traffic areas, machinery, and loud activities
- Use solid walls rather than mesh between enclosures to reduce interspecies visual and auditory stress
- Minimize conversations near patients; speak in low tones
- Avoid music, radios, or television in animal housing areas
- Schedule noisy maintenance activities (mowing, construction) during a consistent daily window so animals can habituate
- Use sound-absorbing materials (acoustic panels, dense vegetation) around enclosures

## Visual Barriers

Visual barriers are essential for reducing stress and preventing habituation.

- Cover cage fronts with cloth or opaque sheeting in ICU areas
- Use one-way glass or observation ports for monitoring without disturbing the animal
- Provide hides, nest boxes, and dense vegetation within enclosures for visual security
- Avoid locating enclosures where domestic animals (dogs, cats) can be seen by the patients
- Use visual barriers between enclosures housing different species, especially predator-prey combinations

## Enrichment

Enrichment promotes species-typical behavior and prevents the development of stereotypic or abnormal behaviors during extended rehabilitation.

### Categories of Enrichment

| Type | Examples | Purpose |
|------|----------|---------|
| Foraging | Scatter feeding, puzzle feeders, hiding food in substrate | Stimulates natural foraging behavior |
| Structural | Branches, logs, rocks, water features, tunnels | Provides complexity and exercise opportunities |
| Sensory | Novel scents (herbs, soil from habitat), natural sounds | Stimulates exploration and environmental awareness |
| Social | Visual or auditory access to conspecifics (when appropriate) | Maintains social behavior; prevents isolation stress |
| Cognitive | Novel objects, problem-solving tasks (appropriate to species) | Prevents boredom and stereotypy |

### Enrichment Cautions

- Never use enrichment that promotes human interaction or habituation
- Remove any enrichment that causes injury or excessive stress
- Rotate enrichment items regularly to maintain novelty
- Document enrichment provided and behavioral responses

## Hygiene Protocols

### Daily Cleaning

- Remove fecal material, uneaten food, and soiled substrate at least once daily
- Replace water in drinking and bathing containers at least twice daily
- Spot-clean surfaces with dilute chlorhexidine (0.05%) or quaternary ammonium solution
- Replace disposable substrate (newspaper, paper towels) daily

### Deep Cleaning

- Perform full enclosure disinfection between patients and at minimum every 7–14 days during occupation
- Remove the animal to a temporary holding container
- Remove all substrate, furnishings, and feeding dishes
- Wash all surfaces with detergent, rinse thoroughly, then apply disinfectant (10% bleach, Virkon S, or F10SC) for the recommended contact time
- Rinse again and allow to dry completely before reintroducing the animal
- Disinfect or replace all furnishings and feeding equipment

### Disinfectant Selection

| Disinfectant | Effective Against | Contact Time | Limitations |
|-------------|------------------|-------------|-------------|
| Sodium hypochlorite (bleach) 10% | Bacteria, viruses, fungi | 10 minutes | Corrosive; inactivated by organic matter; must pre-clean |
| Chlorhexidine 2% | Bacteria, some viruses | 10 minutes | Poor activity against non-enveloped viruses and spores |
| F10SC (quaternary ammonium + biguanide) | Bacteria, viruses, fungi, spores | 10–30 minutes | Excellent broad-spectrum; safe for use around animals at dilution |
| Virkon S (potassium peroxymonosulfate) | Bacteria, viruses, fungi | 10 minutes | Good broad-spectrum; mild irritant at working concentration |
| Trifectant | Bacteria, viruses, fungi | 10 minutes | Similar spectrum to Virkon S |

## Biosecurity Between Patients

### Principles

- Treat every patient as potentially infectious to other patients and to humans
- Separate species taxonomically (e.g., do not house raptors adjacent to passerines or waterfowl)
- Isolate new admissions for a minimum quarantine period (7–14 days recommended)
- Isolate any animal showing signs of infectious disease immediately

### Protocols

- Designate separate equipment (nets, gloves, feeding utensils) for each enclosure or patient group
- If shared equipment is unavoidable, disinfect thoroughly between uses
- Wash hands or change gloves between handling different patients
- Work in order from lowest infection risk to highest (healthy animals first, sick animals last)
- Use foot baths or disposable shoe covers when entering isolation or quarantine areas
- Maintain a log of personnel entering isolation areas

### Disease Screening

- Perform fecal parasite screening on all new admissions
- Blood smear or PCR testing for hemoparasites in avian patients from endemic areas
- Aspergillus screening (tracheal wash or serology) in raptors and waterbirds, especially those in prolonged care
- Chlamydia screening in psittacines and pigeons
- Canine distemper and parvovirus testing in wild carnivores
- Consult with the supervising veterinarian on species-specific screening protocols
