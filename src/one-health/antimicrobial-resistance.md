# Antimicrobial Resistance in Wildlife

## Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the most pressing One Health challenges of the 21st century. While the role of clinical overuse in human and veterinary medicine is well documented, the wildlife dimension of AMR is increasingly recognized as critical. Wildlife species serve simultaneously as reservoirs, sentinels, and victims of antimicrobial resistance, linking environmental contamination to human and animal health risk in complex and often underappreciated ways.

## Wildlife as Reservoirs of AMR

### Mechanisms of AMR Acquisition in Wildlife

Wildlife can acquire resistant bacteria and resistance genes through several pathways:

- **Direct contact** with humans, domestic animals, or their waste products
- **Environmental exposure** to antimicrobial residues in water, soil, and sediments
- **Horizontal gene transfer** from environmental bacteria carrying resistance determinants
- **Consumption of contaminated food sources** (e.g., scavenging on livestock carcasses, foraging near agricultural operations)
- **Intrinsic resistance** in some wildlife-associated bacterial species, unrelated to anthropogenic antimicrobial use

### Evidence for Wildlife AMR Reservoirs

Studies across diverse taxa and geographies have demonstrated AMR in wildlife:

| Wildlife Group | Resistant Organisms Found | Geographic Examples |
|---------------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| Migratory waterfowl | ESBL-producing *E. coli*, fluoroquinolone-resistant *Campylobacter* | Europe, North America, Arctic |
| Gulls and seabirds | ESBL-producing *E. coli*, VRE, MRSA | Worldwide coastal areas |
| Raptors | Fluoroquinolone-resistant *E. coli*, CTX-M-producing Enterobacteriaceae | Europe, Middle East |
| Wild ungulates | Multidrug-resistant *Salmonella*, MRSA | Africa, North America |
| Wild primates | ESBL-producing *E. coli*, resistant *Staphylococcus* spp. | Central Africa, Southeast Asia |
| Marine mammals | Methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus*, MDR *E. coli* | Global coastlines |
| Bats | CTX-M-type ESBL producers, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae | Multiple continents |
| Reptiles | Intrinsically resistant *Salmonella*, acquired MDR | Tropical regions |

### Migratory Species as AMR Vectors

Migratory wildlife, particularly birds, have been identified as potential long-distance vectors for AMR dissemination:

- Arctic-breeding shorebirds have been found carrying ESBL-producing *E. coli* despite minimal direct contact with anthropogenic antimicrobials
- Migratory flyways may connect AMR hotspots (agricultural regions, wastewater outflows) with remote environments
- Stopover sites where migratory birds congregate can serve as mixing zones for resistant bacteria from diverse geographic origins

## Wildlife as Sentinels for AMR

### The Sentinel Concept Applied to AMR

Wildlife species that have limited direct antimicrobial exposure yet carry resistant organisms serve as sentinels for environmental AMR contamination:

- **Indicator species** for waterway contamination: aquatic birds, fish, amphibians
- **Peridomestic species** reflecting local environmental AMR burden: urban-adapted birds, rodents, mesocarnivores
- **Apex predators** bioaccumulating resistant organisms through trophic transfer
- **Species at the livestock-wildlife interface** reflecting agricultural AMR spillover

### Designing Sentinel Surveillance Programs

Effective AMR sentinel surveillance in wildlife requires:

1. **Species selection criteria**:
   - Defined habitat range and known ecology
   - Adequate sample sizes achievable through ethical collection
   - Relevant exposure pathways (aquatic, terrestrial, peridomestic)
   - Representative of the environmental compartment of interest

2. **Sampling protocol standardization**:
   - Consistent anatomical sampling sites (cloacal, fecal, oral)
   - Standardized transport media and cold chain maintenance
   - Metadata collection (GPS coordinates, habitat type, proximity to agriculture/wastewater)

3. **Laboratory methods**:
   - Selective media for target resistant organisms
   - Standardized antimicrobial susceptibility testing (EUCAST or CLSI breakpoints)
   - Molecular characterization of resistance genes
   - Whole genome sequencing for detailed epidemiological analysis

4. **Data integration**:
   - Linking wildlife AMR data with environmental, livestock, and human health datasets
   - Spatial analysis to identify contamination sources and transmission corridors

## Environmental AMR

### Sources of Environmental Antimicrobial Contamination

The environment serves as both a reservoir and a highway for AMR, with wildlife caught in the flow:

#### Wastewater and Sewage
- Municipal wastewater treatment plants release antimicrobial residues and resistant bacteria into waterways
- Hospital effluents contain particularly high concentrations of clinically important resistant organisms
- Wastewater treatment may reduce but does not eliminate resistant bacteria or resistance genes

#### Agricultural Runoff
- Antimicrobials used in livestock production enter the environment through manure application, feedlot runoff, and direct excretion
- Manure lagoons and land-applied biosolids are concentrated sources of resistant bacteria
- Aquaculture operations release antimicrobials and resistant bacteria directly into aquatic ecosystems

#### Pharmaceutical Manufacturing
- Manufacturing effluents in some regions release extremely high concentrations of antimicrobial compounds
- Downstream water sources may contain antimicrobials at selective concentrations, promoting resistance development

#### Other Sources
- Companion animal waste (urban environments)
- Pesticide co-selection (certain pesticides may co-select for antimicrobial resistance)
- Heavy metal contamination (co-selection through shared genetic elements)

### Environmental Resistome

The environmental resistome -- the totality of resistance genes in environmental microbiomes -- is vast and predates human antimicrobial use. However, anthropogenic activities have dramatically altered its composition and distribution:

- Soil bacteria have carried resistance genes for millions of years as defense against naturally produced antibiotics
- Human activities have amplified clinically relevant resistance genes and mobilized them onto transferable genetic elements
- Environmental bacteria serve as both sources and recipients of resistance genes in dynamic exchange with wildlife-associated microbiota

## Key Resistant Organisms in Wildlife

### MRSA (Methicillin-Resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*)

- Detected in wild hedgehogs, wild boar, rodents, birds of prey, marine mammals, and bats
- In hedgehog populations, a novel MRSA lineage (mecC-positive) appears to have evolved through natural selection pressure from dermatophyte-produced beta-lactam compounds, independent of clinical antimicrobial use
- Wildlife MRSA strains can carry additional virulence and resistance genes
- Peridomestic wildlife (e.g., urban hedgehogs) may serve as a bridge between environmental and human MRSA populations

### ESBL-Producing Enterobacteriaceae

- Extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) producing *E. coli* is the most commonly reported AMR organism in wildlife globally
- CTX-M-type ESBLs predominate, with CTX-M-1 and CTX-M-15 groups most common
- Wildlife ESBL isolates frequently carry multiple resistance genes on transferable plasmids
- Phylogenetic analysis often links wildlife isolates to human and livestock lineages, supporting anthropogenic origin

### Carbapenem-Resistant Organisms

- Carbapenems are last-resort antimicrobials for human infections; resistance in wildlife is of grave concern
- Reports of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae in gulls, crows, and wild mammals near urban areas
- The carbapenemase genes *bla*NDM, *bla*OXA-48, and *bla*KPC have all been detected in wildlife isolates
- Presence in wildlife suggests environmental dissemination from clinical settings

### Colistin-Resistant Organisms

- Colistin resistance, particularly the mobile *mcr* genes, has been detected in wildlife-associated *E. coli*
- The *mcr-1* gene, originally described in livestock and linked to agricultural colistin use, has been found in wild birds and rodents
- Transfer of *mcr* genes between wildlife and domestic animal or human bacterial populations is a significant concern

## Surveillance Methods

### Phenotypic Approaches
- Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (disk diffusion, broth microdilution) of bacteria isolated from wildlife samples
- Selective culture using antimicrobial-supplemented media to detect specific resistant phenotypes
- Standardized interpretation using EUCAST or CLSI breakpoints appropriate for animal isolates

### Genotypic Approaches
- PCR-based detection of specific resistance genes (e.g., *mecA*, *bla*CTX-M, *mcr*, *vanA*)
- Whole genome sequencing (WGS) for comprehensive resistance gene profiling and phylogenetic context
- Metagenomic analysis of wildlife fecal or environmental samples to characterize the full resistome without prior culture

### Integrated Surveillance Frameworks
- **AGISAR (WHO Advisory Group on Integrated Surveillance of Antimicrobial Resistance)**: Provides guidance on integrating animal, food, and human AMR surveillance
- **GLASS (Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System)**: Primarily human-focused but increasingly recognizing the wildlife component
- **National AMR action plans**: Many countries now include wildlife surveillance components, though implementation varies widely

## Implications for Human and Animal Health

### Direct Risks
- Wildlife-to-human transmission of resistant organisms through direct contact (handling, bites), environmental contamination of water/food, or vector-borne routes
- Wildlife-to-livestock transmission at shared grazing areas, water sources, and feeding sites
- Potential for wildlife to serve as persistent environmental reservoirs that re-seed resistant organisms into domestic animal and human populations after control efforts

### Indirect Risks
- Wildlife movement and migration can spread resistant organisms across geographic boundaries, undermining regional containment efforts
- Environmental persistence of resistance genes in wildlife-associated ecosystems creates long-term reservoirs
- Co-selection pressures in the environment (heavy metals, biocides) may maintain resistance in the absence of direct antimicrobial exposure

### Implications for Wildlife Health
- AMR can complicate treatment of wildlife patients in rehabilitation settings
- Resistant infections in endangered species may reduce survival and complicate conservation management
- AMR in wildlife populations may indicate broader environmental degradation affecting population health

## Mitigation Strategies

### Reducing Environmental Contamination
- Improve wastewater treatment to remove antimicrobial residues and resistant organisms
- Implement antimicrobial stewardship in livestock production
- Regulate pharmaceutical manufacturing effluents
- Reduce antimicrobial use in aquaculture

### Surveillance and Research
- Expand systematic AMR surveillance in wildlife, with standardized methods and data sharing
- Investigate transmission dynamics between wildlife, environment, domestic animals, and humans
- Study the ecology of resistance genes in environmental reservoirs
- Develop risk assessment frameworks for wildlife-mediated AMR dissemination

### One Health Policy
- Include wildlife AMR in national AMR action plans
- Fund integrated surveillance systems that span human, animal, and environmental sectors
- Support international collaboration on AMR in migratory species
- Invest in wildlife veterinary and laboratory capacity for AMR detection

## Summary

Wildlife AMR sits at the intersection of environmental contamination, microbial ecology, and public health. Understanding how resistant organisms move through wildlife populations, environmental compartments, and into human and domestic animal communities is essential for developing effective AMR mitigation strategies. Wildlife veterinarians, with their unique position at the human-animal-environment interface, are indispensable partners in the global effort to combat antimicrobial resistance.
