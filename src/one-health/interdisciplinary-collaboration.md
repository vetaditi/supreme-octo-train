# Interdisciplinary Collaboration in One Health

## The Imperative for Collaboration

Wildlife health challenges are inherently multifaceted. A disease outbreak in a wild population may simultaneously involve veterinary diagnostics, human epidemiology, ecological analysis, socioeconomic drivers, and policy responses. No single professional or discipline possesses the full toolkit needed to understand and manage these complex problems. Effective interdisciplinary collaboration is therefore not optional but essential.

## Key Partners for Wildlife Veterinarians

### Physicians and Public Health Professionals

Wildlife veterinarians and physicians share more common ground than either group often recognizes:

- **Comparative pathology and medicine**: Many diseases present similarly across species; insights from wildlife pathology can inform human clinical understanding and vice versa
- **Zoonotic disease investigation**: Joint investigation of spillover events requires both human and animal health expertise. Wildlife vets provide source characterization; physicians manage clinical cases
- **Shared diagnostics**: Serological and molecular diagnostic platforms are often transferable between human and animal applications
- **Joint training opportunities**: Programs such as the CDC's Epidemic Intelligence Service (EIS) and veterinary equivalents (e.g., USDA APHIS's National Veterinary Services Laboratories) create shared competencies

**Practical collaboration strategies:**

- Establish joint case conferences for zoonotic disease events
- Co-author risk assessments that integrate wildlife, livestock, and human health data
- Participate in shared continuing education on emerging zoonoses
- Develop standardized case definitions that work across human and animal health systems

### Ecologists and Conservation Biologists

Ecologists provide the environmental and population context essential for interpreting wildlife health data:

- **Population dynamics**: Understanding how disease affects populations requires knowledge of demography, carrying capacity, density dependence, and metapopulation structure
- **Community ecology**: Multi-host pathogen systems require understanding of host community composition, species interactions, and the dilution/amplification effect debate
- **Landscape ecology**: Habitat connectivity, edge effects, and landscape heterogeneity influence disease transmission and wildlife movement
- **Behavioral ecology**: Animal behavior determines contact rates, exposure patterns, and stress levels, all of which modulate disease dynamics

**Practical collaboration strategies:**

- Integrate health sampling into ongoing ecological field studies
- Share GPS telemetry and movement data across health and ecology teams
- Conduct joint habitat assessments that include health risk evaluation
- Co-develop population viability analyses that incorporate disease scenarios

### Epidemiologists

Epidemiologists bring rigorous quantitative frameworks for understanding disease patterns:

- **Study design**: Expertise in designing observational and experimental studies with appropriate controls and statistical power
- **Outbreak investigation**: Systematic approaches to identifying sources, modes of transmission, and risk factors during disease events
- **Modeling**: Mathematical and computational modeling of disease dynamics, forecasting, and intervention assessment
- **Surveillance system design**: Building efficient, sensitive surveillance systems that integrate data from multiple sources

**Practical collaboration strategies:**

- Engage epidemiologists early in study design, not just for data analysis
- Develop shared outbreak investigation protocols that include wildlife sampling
- Participate in joint modeling exercises that incorporate wildlife disease data
- Contribute wildlife health data to multi-sector surveillance dashboards

### Social Scientists

Social scientists illuminate the human dimensions that drive wildlife health outcomes:

- **Medical anthropology**: Understanding cultural practices (bushmeat hunting, traditional medicine use, wildlife trade) that create disease transmission opportunities
- **Health economics**: Quantifying the economic burden of wildlife diseases and the cost-effectiveness of interventions
- **Political science**: Navigating institutional landscapes, understanding policy processes, and advocating for wildlife health in governance structures
- **Risk communication**: Framing wildlife health risks in ways that are accurate, accessible, and actionable for diverse audiences
- **Community-based research**: Engaging local communities as partners rather than subjects in wildlife health research

**Practical collaboration strategies:**

- Include social scientists in study design to address human behavioral dimensions
- Conduct community consultations before implementing wildlife health interventions
- Commission economic analyses to support advocacy for wildlife health funding
- Collaborate on risk communication materials for zoonotic disease events

### Community Health Workers and Local Practitioners

In many settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, community health workers and local practitioners are essential partners:

- They provide early warning of unusual animal mortality events
- They bridge language and cultural barriers between scientists and communities
- They can be trained to collect basic wildlife health data (e.g., mortality reporting)
- They understand local land use, human-wildlife interaction patterns, and community priorities

## Communication Across Disciplines

### Overcoming Jargon Barriers

Each discipline carries its own specialized vocabulary, and what seems like shared terminology can have different meanings:

| Term | Veterinary/Ecological Meaning | Public Health Meaning |
|------|-------------------------------|----------------------|
| Prevalence | Often used loosely; may refer to apparent or true prevalence | Strictly defined as cases/population at a point in time |
| Reservoir | Host species maintaining pathogen | Any source of ongoing infection |
| Surveillance | May emphasize wildlife population monitoring | Systematic collection of health data for action |
| Outbreak | May refer to epizootic in wildlife | Exceeding expected human case counts |
| Incidence | Sometimes conflated with prevalence | New cases per population at risk per time |

**Strategies for clear communication:**

- Define key terms at the start of collaborative projects
- Create shared glossaries for interdisciplinary teams
- Use visual aids (diagrams, maps, flowcharts) that transcend disciplinary language
- Designate "boundary spanners" -- individuals comfortable in multiple disciplinary languages
- Write reports with executive summaries accessible to non-specialists

### Navigating Disciplinary Cultures

Beyond vocabulary, disciplines differ in:

- **Epistemological approaches**: Ecologists may emphasize observational data and natural experiments; epidemiologists may prioritize randomized controlled trials; social scientists may value qualitative and interpretive methods
- **Publication norms**: Authorship conventions, acceptable study designs, and target journals vary widely
- **Temporal frameworks**: Ecological processes may unfold over decades; public health crises demand rapid response
- **Risk tolerance**: Conservation biologists may accept interventions with uncertain outcomes to save endangered species; public health professionals may require higher standards of evidence before acting

## Shared Data Platforms

### Principles of Data Sharing

Effective One Health collaboration requires data to flow across institutional and disciplinary boundaries:

- **Interoperability**: Data systems must use compatible formats and standards
- **Timeliness**: Data shared after the crisis has passed has limited value
- **Quality assurance**: Shared data must meet agreed-upon quality standards
- **Attribution**: Contributors must receive appropriate credit
- **Privacy and ethics**: Sensitive data (location of endangered species, patient health records) must be appropriately protected

### Key Platforms and Databases

| Platform | Focus | Scope |
|----------|-------|-------|
| WAHIS (WOAH) | Animal disease reporting | Global, mandatory for WOAH members |
| ProMED-mail | Emerging disease alerts | Global, open-access surveillance |
| HealthMap | Disease intelligence | Automated aggregation of outbreak reports |
| GBIF | Biodiversity occurrence data | Global, open-access |
| WHISPers (USGS) | Wildlife health event reporting | United States |
| EMPRES-i (FAO) | Animal disease events | Global, transboundary diseases |
| GenBank/GISAID | Pathogen genetic sequences | Global, open or controlled access |
| EID2 | Enhanced infectious disease database | Global, multi-host pathogen data |

### Building Integrated Surveillance Systems

- Agree on standardized case definitions across human, domestic animal, and wildlife health sectors
- Establish data-sharing agreements with clear governance structures
- Invest in information technology infrastructure for real-time data exchange
- Train personnel across sectors in data entry, quality control, and interpretation
- Conduct regular cross-sector data reviews and joint situation analyses

## Joint Outbreak Investigation

### Integrated Investigation Framework

When a disease event involves wildlife, a joint investigation should follow a structured process:

1. **Detection and notification**: Establish clear reporting channels that link wildlife mortality observers, veterinary authorities, and public health agencies
2. **Initial assessment**: Rapid multisector assessment to determine scope, species involved, and potential human/livestock exposure
3. **Joint investigation team**: Assemble a team including wildlife veterinarians, epidemiologists, ecologists, laboratory scientists, and if relevant, social scientists
4. **Coordinated field response**:
   - Wildlife team: Specimen collection, population assessment, environmental sampling
   - Public health team: Human exposure assessment, contact tracing, clinical surveillance
   - Environmental team: Habitat assessment, vector sampling, water/soil testing
5. **Laboratory coordination**: Ensure specimens from all taxa are processed with compatible protocols; share results across teams in real time
6. **Joint analysis**: Integrate human, animal, and environmental data to construct a comprehensive epidemiological picture
7. **Coordinated response**: Develop interventions that address the problem across sectors simultaneously
8. **Joint reporting and publication**: Produce unified reports that reflect contributions from all disciplines

### Common Challenges in Joint Investigations

- **Jurisdictional conflicts**: Different agencies may have overlapping or unclear mandates
- **Resource disparities**: Wildlife health agencies typically have far fewer resources than human health counterparts
- **Specimen sharing**: Regulatory and logistical barriers to sharing biological specimens across agencies and borders
- **Different timelines**: Public health may demand immediate action while wildlife ecology requires longer-term study
- **Publication competition**: Pressure to publish quickly in discipline-specific journals can undermine collaborative analysis

## Building One Health Networks

### Network Architecture

Effective One Health networks operate at multiple levels:

- **Local**: Partnerships between wildlife rehabilitators, local veterinarians, community health clinics, and land managers
- **Regional**: Cross-jurisdictional collaborations linking state/provincial wildlife agencies with health departments
- **National**: Formal agreements between agriculture, health, and environment ministries
- **International**: Multilateral frameworks coordinated through WHO, FAO, WOAH, and UNEP

### Strategies for Network Development

- **Start with shared problems**: Networks form most readily around concrete, shared challenges (e.g., a zoonotic disease at the wildlife-livestock interface)
- **Formalize relationships**: Memoranda of understanding, joint standard operating procedures, and shared budgets provide institutional stability
- **Invest in relationships**: Trust is built through repeated positive interactions; joint trainings, workshops, and field exercises are valuable
- **Create boundary organizations**: Institutions specifically designed to bridge sectors (e.g., national One Health platforms, university-based One Health centers)
- **Leverage technology**: Virtual meeting platforms, shared data dashboards, and communication apps reduce barriers to routine collaboration
- **Engage early-career professionals**: Joint training programs and mentorship across disciplines build the next generation of One Health leaders

### Indicators of Effective Collaboration

Successful interdisciplinary One Health collaborations typically demonstrate:

- Shared research questions developed collaboratively, not imposed by one discipline
- Co-designed studies with input from all relevant sectors
- Joint publications with authors from multiple disciplines
- Shared funding proposals and resource allocation
- Mutual respect for differing expertise and epistemologies
- Tangible outcomes that benefit multiple sectors
- Sustained engagement beyond individual projects or crises

## Case Example: One Health Surveillance Network

A well-functioning One Health surveillance network for a zoonotic pathogen such as *Brucella* might include:

- **Wildlife veterinarians** conducting serological surveys in elk and bison
- **Livestock veterinarians** testing cattle herds near wildlife-livestock interfaces
- **Public health officers** monitoring human brucellosis cases and occupational exposure
- **Ecologists** mapping wildlife movement corridors and livestock grazing areas
- **Epidemiologists** analyzing integrated multi-species datasets
- **Social scientists** studying rancher attitudes toward wildlife and disease management
- **Policy analysts** evaluating regulatory frameworks for livestock-wildlife separation

Data from all partners flows into a shared dashboard, with regular joint reviews that inform management decisions affecting wildlife, livestock, and human health simultaneously.

## Summary

Interdisciplinary collaboration is the engine that powers One Health. For wildlife veterinarians, learning to work effectively across disciplinary boundaries is as important as mastering clinical skills. The investment in building relationships, shared language, integrated data systems, and collaborative investigation protocols pays dividends in more effective disease prevention, more insightful research, and better outcomes for wildlife, human, and ecosystem health alike.
