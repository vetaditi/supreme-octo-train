# Climate Change and Wildlife Health

## Introduction

Climate change is fundamentally reshaping the health landscape for wildlife, domestic animals, and humans. Rising temperatures, altered precipitation patterns, sea level rise, ocean acidification, and increasing frequency of extreme weather events are disrupting ecological systems at every scale. For wildlife veterinarians, understanding the health consequences of climate change is essential for diagnosing emerging problems, anticipating future challenges, and contributing to adaptation strategies.

## Range Shifts of Vectors and Pathogens

### Temperature-Driven Expansion

Many disease vectors and pathogens are constrained by temperature. As isotherms shift poleward and to higher elevations, previously unsuitable habitats become viable:

- **Ticks**: *Ixodes ricinus* in Europe and *Ixodes scapularis* in North America have expanded their ranges northward. The blacklegged tick's range in Canada has expanded dramatically, bringing Lyme disease, anaplasmosis, and babesiosis to new regions.
- **Mosquitoes**: *Aedes albopictus* continues to expand into temperate regions, carrying potential for dengue, chikungunya, and Zika transmission. *Culex* species ranges and activity seasons are lengthening, affecting West Nile virus transmission to wildlife and humans.
- **Biting midges**: *Culicoides* spp. transmitting bluetongue virus have expanded into northern Europe, causing outbreaks in previously unaffected ruminant populations.
- **Parasites**: Lungworm (*Elaphostrongylus rangiferi*) development rates in gastropod intermediate hosts have increased with warming, leading to severe outbreaks in reindeer and muskoxen in the Arctic.

### Altitudinal Shifts

- Avian malaria (*Plasmodium relictum*) and its mosquito vectors have moved to higher elevations in Hawaii, threatening critically endangered honeycreepers that evolved without malaria exposure
- Chytrid fungus (*Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*) dynamics in montane tropical amphibians are linked to temperature patterns, with the thermal-optimum hypothesis suggesting warming at high elevations creates conditions favoring fungal growth
- Highland livestock and wildlife populations in East Africa face expanding tick-borne disease risk as suitable vector habitat moves upslope

### Changes in Seasonality

| Parameter | Change | Health Consequence |
|-----------|--------|-------------------|
| Earlier spring | Extended vector activity season | Longer disease transmission window |
| Warmer winters | Reduced vector winter mortality | Higher vector population densities |
| Extended autumn | Prolonged pathogen development in vectors | Increased transmission efficiency |
| Changed precipitation | Altered breeding habitat availability | Shifted spatial patterns of risk |

## Thermal Stress

### Direct Effects on Wildlife

- **Ectotherms**: Reptiles and amphibians face metabolic stress as temperatures exceed optimal ranges. Thermal performance curves predict reduced immune function, growth, and reproduction at extreme temperatures.
- **Endotherms**: Marine mammals and polar species face thermoregulatory challenges. Mass mortality events in flying foxes (*Pteropus* spp.) in Australia during extreme heat events demonstrate acute thermal mortality.
- **Marine species**: Coral reef fish experience reduced aerobic scope at elevated temperatures, increasing susceptibility to parasites and pathogens.
- **Birds**: Nest failure and chick mortality increase during heat waves, particularly for cavity-nesting and ground-nesting species.

### Indirect Effects Through Immune Function

Thermal stress suppresses immune function across taxa:

- Chronic sub-lethal thermal stress reduces lymphocyte proliferation and antibody production
- Heat stress increases glucocorticoid production, which suppresses both innate and adaptive immunity
- Temperature-dependent immune responses in ectotherms mean that warming can either enhance or suppress immunity depending on the species and the magnitude of change
- Thermal stress may increase susceptibility to opportunistic infections that would otherwise be controlled

## Phenological Mismatches

### Disrupted Ecological Timing

Climate change affects different components of ecological systems at different rates, creating timing mismatches:

- **Predator-prey mismatches**: Migratory birds arriving at breeding grounds after peak insect emergence face reduced food availability, leading to poor body condition and increased disease susceptibility
- **Pollinator-plant mismatches**: Altered flowering phenology relative to pollinator emergence reduces nutrition for both pollinators and the wildlife that depends on plant reproduction
- **Parasite-host mismatches**: Changes in the timing of host migration relative to parasite development can either increase or decrease disease risk depending on the specific system
- **Breeding-resource mismatches**: Reptile nesting phenology may no longer align with optimal incubation temperatures, affecting sex ratios in species with temperature-dependent sex determination

### Consequences for Population Health

- Nutritional stress from phenological mismatches reduces immune competence and reproductive success
- Altered migration timing may expose populations to novel pathogen communities at stopovers
- Desynchronized predator-prey relationships can cascade through food webs, affecting health at multiple trophic levels

## Coral Bleaching and Marine Health

### Mechanisms and Scale

Coral bleaching occurs when thermal stress causes corals to expel their symbiotic zooxanthellae. Mass bleaching events have become more frequent and severe:

- The 2014-2017 global bleaching event affected over 75% of the world's reef-building corals
- Recovery times between bleaching events are shortening, preventing full recovery
- Repeated bleaching shifts coral community composition toward stress-tolerant but less structurally complex species

### Health Consequences for Marine Wildlife

- **Reef fish**: Loss of structural habitat reduces shelter from predators, increases competition, and reduces prey availability. Disease susceptibility increases in nutritionally stressed fish populations.
- **Sea turtles**: Degraded coral reef foraging habitat reduces nutrition for hawksbill turtles; warming sands skew sex ratios toward females in many populations.
- **Marine mammals**: Reef-dependent prey species decline, forcing dietary shifts and increasing energetic costs for coastal cetaceans and pinnipeds.
- **Coral disease**: Warming increases prevalence and virulence of coral diseases (black band disease, white syndrome), further accelerating reef degradation.

## Arctic and Polar Species Impacts

### Warming Amplification

The Arctic is warming approximately four times faster than the global average, creating acute health challenges for polar wildlife:

- **Sea ice loss**: Reduced sea ice affects polar bear body condition, reproduction, and survival. Longer swimming distances between ice floes increase energetic costs and drowning risk, particularly for cubs.
- **Permafrost thaw**: Release of preserved pathogens (e.g., anthrax spores from thawing reindeer burial sites in Siberia caused a 2016 outbreak). Changes in soil hydrology affect burrowing and denning species.
- **Trophic cascades**: Changes in sea ice algae production cascade through copepods, fish, seals, and polar bears, affecting nutritional status at every level.

### Emerging Diseases in Polar Regions

- *Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae* has caused mass mortality in muskoxen in the Canadian Arctic
- *Phocine distemper virus* outbreaks in Arctic seals have been linked to altered ice conditions increasing contact between populations
- Parasites previously restricted to sub-Arctic regions are expanding northward into naive host populations
- *Toxoplasma gondii* has been detected in Arctic marine mammals, likely reflecting northward range expansion of felid definitive hosts

## Modeling Disease Under Climate Scenarios

### Approaches to Climate-Disease Modeling

- **Species distribution models (SDMs)**: Project future range of vectors and hosts under climate scenarios (e.g., MaxEnt, ensemble models)
- **Mechanistic models**: Incorporate physiological and ecological processes (temperature-dependent development rates, host immunity, vector competence)
- **Statistical models**: Use historical climate-disease associations to project future risk
- **Coupled climate-epidemiological models**: Integrate climate model outputs with disease transmission models

### Key Considerations

- **Scenario selection**: Use multiple Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs) or Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) to bracket uncertainty
- **Spatial resolution**: Downscaled climate projections are needed for ecologically meaningful predictions
- **Non-linear responses**: Threshold effects and tipping points may cause abrupt changes not captured by linear projections
- **Biotic interactions**: Climate effects on host communities, competitors, and predators may be as important as direct effects on pathogens and vectors
- **Adaptation potential**: Some species may adapt through behavioral, physiological, or evolutionary responses, modifying projected impacts

### Model Limitations

- Projections are only as good as the underlying data and ecological understanding
- Novel climate conditions ("no-analog climates") may produce ecological configurations with no historical precedent
- Most models do not account for evolutionary adaptation in hosts, pathogens, or vectors
- Social and land-use changes interact with climate but are difficult to project simultaneously

## Adaptation Strategies

### For Wildlife Populations

- **Habitat connectivity**: Maintaining and restoring wildlife corridors to facilitate range shifts in response to changing climate and disease pressure
- **Genetic diversity**: Conserving genetic diversity within populations to maximize adaptive potential; assisted gene flow in extreme cases
- **Managed relocation**: Moving populations to climatically suitable areas when natural dispersal is impossible (controversial but increasingly discussed)
- **Disease management**: Proactive surveillance for climate-sensitive pathogens in expanding risk zones; vaccination of susceptible wildlife populations where feasible

### For Wildlife Health Systems

- **Enhanced surveillance**: Increasing monitoring in climate-sensitive areas and at leading edges of range expansions
- **Early warning systems**: Developing climate-based forecasting tools for disease outbreaks (e.g., Rift Valley fever risk mapping based on ENSO and rainfall anomalies)
- **Capacity building**: Training wildlife veterinarians in climate-health linkages and preparing for novel disease scenarios
- **Research priorities**: Investing in understanding thermal ecology of host-pathogen interactions, climate effects on immune function, and ecosystem-level health responses to climate change

### For Policy and Planning

- **Climate-health vulnerability assessments**: Systematic evaluation of wildlife populations and health systems most at risk from climate change
- **Integration into conservation planning**: Incorporating climate-disease projections into species recovery plans, protected area management, and habitat restoration priorities
- **Cross-sector coordination**: Linking climate adaptation planning across wildlife, livestock, public health, and environmental sectors
- **Mitigation advocacy**: Wildlife health data can powerfully illustrate the biological consequences of climate change, supporting broader mitigation efforts

## Research Priorities

1. Long-term monitoring programs that link climate variables to wildlife health outcomes across multiple taxa and ecosystems
2. Experimental studies on temperature-immune function relationships in wildlife species
3. Improved mechanistic models of climate-driven disease dynamics
4. Assessment of evolutionary adaptation potential in host-pathogen-vector systems
5. Economic valuation of climate change impacts on wildlife health and ecosystem services
6. Development of climate-smart wildlife health surveillance networks

## Summary

Climate change is not a future threat to wildlife health; it is a present reality reshaping disease dynamics, physiological limits, ecological interactions, and conservation challenges worldwide. Wildlife veterinarians must integrate climate literacy into their practice, contribute to climate-health research, and advocate for adaptation strategies that protect wildlife populations in a rapidly changing world.
