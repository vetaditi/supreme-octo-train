# Core Principles of One Health

## The Human-Animal-Environment Interface

The foundational premise of One Health is that human health, animal health, and environmental health are not separate domains but deeply intertwined components of a single, integrated system. Disease emergence, antimicrobial resistance, food safety crises, and environmental degradation all arise at the interfaces where these three domains meet.

### Understanding the Interface

The human-animal-environment interface is not a single boundary but a complex, dynamic zone of interaction:

- **Peridomestic interface** -- Where wildlife, domestic animals, and humans share space around settlements, farms, and villages
- **Sylvatic interface** -- Where humans enter wildlife habitats through logging, mining, ecotourism, or research
- **Agricultural interface** -- Where livestock production systems overlap with wildlife habitats
- **Market interface** -- Where wild-caught or farmed wildlife enters trade chains, including live animal markets
- **Aquatic interface** -- Where human activities, aquatic wildlife, and waterborne pathogens converge

Each interface carries distinct risks and requires tailored One Health interventions.

## Interconnected Health

### Shared Pathogens

Humans, domestic animals, and wildlife share a vast microbial world. Key patterns include:

| Transmission Direction | Examples | Implications |
|----------------------|----------|--------------|
| Wildlife to human (zoonosis) | Ebola, SARS-CoV-2, Nipah, Hantavirus | Pandemic risk, emerging infections |
| Human to wildlife (anthroponosis) | Tuberculosis in great apes, COVID-19 in deer | Conservation threat, reservoir creation |
| Wildlife to livestock | Foot-and-mouth disease, avian influenza | Agricultural loss, economic impact |
| Livestock to wildlife | Bovine tuberculosis, brucellosis | Population decline, spillback risk |
| Environment to all | Anthrax spores in soil, fungal pathogens | Shared environmental exposure |

### Shared Environments

Environmental health directly determines the health of all organisms within an ecosystem:

- **Water quality** affects amphibians, fish, wildlife, livestock, and human communities simultaneously
- **Air quality** and particulate pollution impact respiratory health across species
- **Soil contamination** with heavy metals or persistent organic pollutants enters food webs and affects all trophic levels
- **Habitat degradation** increases contact rates between species, promoting pathogen spillover

### Shared Determinants of Health

Social, economic, and environmental factors drive health outcomes across species:

- Poverty drives bushmeat hunting, increasing zoonotic exposure
- Agricultural intensification reduces biodiversity and increases pathogen amplification
- Urbanization creates novel wildlife-human interfaces
- Climate change alters disease dynamics for humans, animals, and ecosystems simultaneously

## Systems Thinking

### Why Reductionist Approaches Fall Short

Traditional approaches to disease control often focus on a single pathogen in a single host. While valuable, this reductionist lens misses critical dynamics:

- Controlling a wildlife disease reservoir without addressing environmental drivers may simply shift the problem
- Treating livestock disease without understanding wildlife maintenance hosts leads to repeated re-emergence
- Managing human infectious disease without ecological context ignores root causes

### Applying Systems Thinking

Systems thinking in One Health means:

- **Identifying feedback loops** -- How does controlling one component affect others? For example, culling wildlife to reduce disease transmission may disrupt predator-prey dynamics and worsen the problem
- **Recognizing emergent properties** -- Disease emergence is an emergent property of complex social-ecological systems, not simply the result of a pathogen meeting a host
- **Mapping causal networks** -- Using tools such as causal loop diagrams and directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) to understand how drivers interact
- **Embracing uncertainty** -- Complex systems are inherently unpredictable; adaptive management and scenario planning are essential
- **Working across scales** -- From molecular (pathogen evolution) to landscape (habitat connectivity) to global (trade and travel networks)

### Practical Systems Tools

- **Causal loop diagrams** to map interactions between disease, ecology, and human behavior
- **Agent-based models** to simulate disease dynamics across species
- **Network analysis** to understand transmission pathways and contact structures
- **Scenario planning** to prepare for multiple possible futures under environmental change

## The Transdisciplinary Approach

### Beyond Multidisciplinary

One Health requires more than professionals from different fields working in parallel. It demands **transdisciplinary integration**, where:

- Disciplines are not just represented but genuinely merged in problem framing and solution development
- Community and indigenous knowledge is valued alongside scientific data
- Research questions are co-developed with stakeholders, not imposed by academics
- Outputs are actionable across sectors, not confined to disciplinary publications

### Key Disciplines in One Health

- **Veterinary medicine** (domestic and wildlife)
- **Human medicine and public health**
- **Ecology and conservation biology**
- **Environmental science and toxicology**
- **Epidemiology and biostatistics**
- **Social sciences** (anthropology, economics, political science)
- **Geography and spatial science**
- **Microbiology and molecular biology**
- **Agricultural science**

### Building Transdisciplinary Capacity

- Joint training programs for veterinary, medical, and public health students
- Shared field exercises and outbreak simulations
- Cross-sector fellowships and exchanges
- Integrated curricula that teach One Health from undergraduate level

## One Health in Action: Case Studies

### Avian Influenza Surveillance

Highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) exemplifies One Health in practice:

- **Wildlife component**: Surveillance of wild waterbirds (ducks, geese, shorebirds) as natural reservoirs. Migration patterns tracked to predict virus movement. Wild bird mortality events trigger rapid response.
- **Domestic animal component**: Poultry farm biosecurity, rapid depopulation during outbreaks, vaccination strategies in endemic regions.
- **Human component**: Monitoring poultry workers and live market workers for seroconversion, pandemic preparedness planning for potential human-adapted strains.
- **Environmental component**: Environmental sampling of wetlands and waterways, understanding how water temperature and pH affect viral persistence.

The global HPAI surveillance network, coordinated through FAO, WOAH, and WHO, is one of the most mature One Health systems in operation. The 2021-2023 panzootic of H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b demonstrated both the strengths and remaining gaps in this system, with unprecedented spread to marine mammals and new geographic regions revealing the need for expanded wildlife surveillance.

### Ebola Virus Response

The West African Ebola epidemic (2013-2016) laid bare the consequences of failing to apply One Health:

- **Wildlife origins**: Ebola virus circulates in fruit bats and periodically spills over to great apes and humans. Bushmeat hunting and handling is a primary exposure route.
- **Missed signals**: Great ape die-offs preceded human outbreaks in several instances but were not linked to public health early warning systems.
- **One Health response evolution**: Subsequent preparedness efforts have integrated wildlife mortality surveillance into human outbreak early warning. The Democratic Republic of Congo's later outbreaks benefited from more integrated surveillance.
- **Lessons learned**: The need for wildlife health infrastructure in tropical forest regions, community engagement around bushmeat safety, and cross-border One Health coordination.

### Rabies Elimination Programs

Global rabies elimination efforts represent a One Health success story:

- **The problem**: Rabies kills an estimated 59,000 people annually, with 99% of human cases transmitted by domestic dogs, which in turn may acquire infection from wildlife reservoirs.
- **One Health approach**: Mass dog vaccination programs (veterinary sector), post-exposure prophylaxis for bite victims (human health sector), and wildlife reservoir management (wildlife/environmental sector).
- **Integrated strategy**: The "Zero by 30" initiative (WHO, FAO, WOAH, GARC) aims to eliminate human deaths from dog-mediated rabies by 2030 through coordinated action across sectors.
- **Wildlife considerations**: Oral rabies vaccination of wildlife (raccoons, foxes) in North America and Europe, understanding sylvatic cycles that may reintroduce virus to domestic animal populations.

### Lyme Disease and Land Use Change

The ecology of Lyme disease illustrates how environmental change drives One Health outcomes:

- Fragmentation of eastern North American forests reduces predator diversity
- White-footed mice (*Peromyscus leucopus*), highly competent reservoirs for *Borrelia burgdorferi*, thrive in fragmented habitats
- Tick (*Ixodes scapularis*) populations increase with rising deer densities and warming temperatures
- Human Lyme disease incidence increases where suburban development interfaces with forest patches
- Solutions require integrated land-use planning, wildlife management, tick surveillance, and public health education

## Measuring One Health Impact

### Indicators and Metrics

Demonstrating the value of One Health requires metrics that span sectors:

- **Health outcomes**: Reduction in zoonotic disease incidence in both human and animal populations
- **Economic savings**: Costs averted through upstream prevention versus downstream response
- **Ecosystem indicators**: Biodiversity indices, wildlife population health metrics, water and air quality
- **Process indicators**: Number of cross-sector collaborations, shared surveillance systems, joint training events

### Challenges in Evaluation

- Benefits of prevention are inherently difficult to quantify (the pandemic that did not happen)
- Attribution is challenging when multiple sectors contribute to outcomes
- Long time horizons for environmental health improvements
- Need for novel economic evaluation frameworks (e.g., return on investment for pandemic prevention)

## Guiding Principles Summary

1. **Health is one** -- The health of humans, animals, and ecosystems is fundamentally indivisible
2. **Upstream prevention** -- Addressing root causes at the human-animal-environment interface is more effective than downstream response
3. **Collaboration is essential** -- No single discipline or sector can address One Health challenges alone
4. **Equity matters** -- One Health burdens fall disproportionately on marginalized communities; solutions must be equitable
5. **Evidence-driven** -- Decisions should be grounded in the best available science from all relevant disciplines
6. **Adaptive** -- One Health approaches must evolve as systems, threats, and knowledge change
7. **Locally relevant, globally connected** -- Solutions must be tailored to local contexts while recognizing global interconnections
