# One Health Framework

## Introduction

One Health is an integrated, unifying approach that aims to sustainably balance and optimize the health of people, animals, and ecosystems. It recognizes that the health of humans, domestic and wild animals, plants, and the wider environment are closely linked and interdependent. For wildlife veterinarians, One Health is not merely a conceptual framework but a daily operational reality: every wildlife disease investigation sits at the nexus of animal health, environmental integrity, and human well-being.

## Historical Foundations

### From Comparative Medicine to One Medicine

The intellectual roots of One Health stretch back centuries. Rudolf Virchow, the 19th-century pathologist, coined the term "zoonosis" and argued that no dividing line should exist between human and animal medicine. However, the modern articulation of One Health owes its greatest debt to **Calvin Schwabe**, a veterinary epidemiologist who in his 1964 textbook *Veterinary Medicine and Human Health* introduced the concept of **"One Medicine."**

Schwabe observed that:

- Human and veterinary medical sciences share fundamental biological principles
- Many of the most significant infectious diseases circulate between animals and humans
- The artificial separation of human and veterinary medicine weakened both disciplines
- Food safety, zoonotic disease control, and comparative biomedical research demanded integrated approaches

### Evolution to One Health

The transition from "One Medicine" to "One Health" reflected a broadening of scope:

| Era | Concept | Focus |
|-----|---------|-------|
| Pre-2000 | One Medicine | Human-animal disease parallels |
| 2004 | Manhattan Principles | Wildlife-domestic animal-human disease links |
| 2007 | One Health concept formalized | Addition of environmental/ecosystem health |
| 2010s-present | One Health in practice | Institutionalization across global agencies |

The **2004 Manhattan Principles**, drafted at a symposium organized by the Wildlife Conservation Society, explicitly placed wildlife health at the center of the framework. These twelve principles called for an international, interdisciplinary approach to preventing disease that moved across species boundaries and recognized the inseparability of human and animal health from ecosystem function.

## Why Wildlife Veterinarians Are Central to One Health

Wildlife veterinarians occupy a unique and critical position within the One Health framework for several reasons:

### Zoonotic Disease Surveillance

Approximately **60-75% of emerging infectious diseases in humans** are of zoonotic origin, and the majority of these originate in wildlife. Wildlife veterinarians serve as the front line for:

- Detecting novel pathogens before they spill over into human populations
- Conducting active surveillance in wildlife populations at high-risk interfaces
- Characterizing pathogen diversity in wildlife reservoirs
- Assessing spillover risk at wildlife-livestock-human interfaces

### Ecosystem Health Assessment

Wildlife veterinarians are trained to interpret individual animal health findings in the context of population and ecosystem dynamics. They can:

- Identify environmental contaminants through wildlife morbidity and mortality events
- Detect ecosystem degradation through changes in wildlife health parameters
- Link land-use changes to disease emergence patterns
- Evaluate the health consequences of biodiversity loss

### Bridging Disciplines

With training that spans clinical medicine, ecology, epidemiology, and conservation biology, wildlife veterinarians naturally bridge disciplinary gaps that often hinder One Health implementation.

## Global One Health Initiatives

### The Tripartite and Quadripartite

The most prominent institutional expression of One Health is the collaboration among three (now four) major international organizations:

- **World Health Organization (WHO)** -- human health mandate
- **Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO)** -- food security and agricultural health
- **World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH, formerly OIE)** -- animal health standards and disease reporting
- **United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP)** -- added in 2022 to form the Quadripartite, reflecting the essential role of environmental health

Together, these agencies have established the **One Health High-Level Expert Panel (OHHLEP)** and developed a **One Health Joint Plan of Action (2022-2026)** focusing on:

1. Strengthening health systems through One Health approaches
2. Reducing risks from emerging and re-emerging zoonotic epidemics and pandemics
3. Controlling and eliminating endemic zoonotic diseases
4. Strengthening assessment, management, and communication of food safety risks
5. Curbing antimicrobial resistance
6. Integrating environment into One Health

### Regional and National Programs

- **PREDICT Project** (USAID) -- Conducted wildlife surveillance for novel viruses across global hotspots, identifying over 1,200 novel viruses
- **Global Health Security Agenda (GHSA)** -- Multinational initiative strengthening capacities to prevent, detect, and respond to infectious disease threats
- **OFFLU (OIE/FAO Network of Expertise on Animal Influenza)** -- Coordinates animal influenza surveillance and research globally
- **EcoHealth Alliance** -- Research organization working at the intersection of wildlife health, ecology, and global health security

## Core Competencies for One Health Wildlife Veterinarians

To effectively operate within the One Health framework, wildlife veterinarians should develop competencies across several domains:

### Technical Skills
- Wildlife disease diagnostics and pathology
- Epidemiological investigation and outbreak response
- Environmental sampling and contaminant analysis
- Risk assessment and risk communication

### Collaborative Skills
- Interdisciplinary communication and teamwork
- Cultural competency for working in diverse global settings
- Policy engagement and science communication
- Community engagement and participatory approaches

### Systems Thinking
- Understanding of complex adaptive systems
- Ability to work across scales (individual to ecosystem)
- Appreciation for social-ecological system dynamics
- Integration of multiple data streams for holistic assessment

## Challenges and Future Directions

Despite growing recognition of One Health, significant challenges remain:

- **Institutional silos** continue to separate human, animal, and environmental health agencies in most countries
- **Funding imbalances** heavily favor human health over wildlife and environmental health components
- **Workforce gaps** exist in wildlife health capacity, particularly in biodiversity-rich low- and middle-income countries
- **Metrics for success** remain poorly defined, making it difficult to demonstrate the value of One Health investments
- **Data integration** across sectors is hampered by incompatible systems, differing standards, and institutional reluctance to share

The COVID-19 pandemic, widely believed to have a wildlife origin, dramatically underscored the importance of One Health approaches and catalyzed renewed global commitment to integrating wildlife health into pandemic prevention strategies.

## Summary

One Health is the essential operating framework for modern wildlife veterinary medicine. It positions wildlife veterinarians not as specialists working in isolation but as critical nodes in a global network dedicated to protecting human, animal, and environmental health. The chapters that follow explore the principles, collaborations, and specific applications that bring this framework to life in practice.
