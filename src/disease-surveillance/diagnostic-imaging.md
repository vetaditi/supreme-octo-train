# Diagnostic Imaging in Wildlife

## Introduction

Diagnostic imaging is an increasingly valuable tool in wildlife veterinary medicine, providing non-invasive assessment of musculoskeletal, thoracic, abdominal, and soft tissue structures. While the fundamental principles of radiography, ultrasonography, and advanced imaging are the same as in domestic animal practice, wildlife species present unique challenges related to positioning, anatomical variation, size range, and interpretation.

## Radiography

### General Principles

Radiography remains the most widely available and commonly used imaging modality in wildlife medicine. Standard views (ventrodorsal/dorsoventral and lateral) are applicable across taxa, but species-specific modifications are frequently required.

### Positioning Challenges

#### Birds
- **Standard views**: ventrodorsal (VD) with wings extended symmetrically, and right lateral
- **Restraint**: manual restraint during sedation or anesthesia is strongly recommended for diagnostic-quality radiographs; masking tape or radiolucent positioning aids can assist
- **Wing extension**: full VD extension of both wings is essential for evaluating the coelom and respiratory system; asymmetric positioning creates superimposition artifacts
- **Leg positioning**: extend legs caudally for VD view; avoid superimposition over coelomic structures
- **Small birds**: use high-detail cassettes or digital receptor plates; mammography film provides excellent detail for passerines

#### Reptiles
- **Chelonians**: DV view is standard (shell prevents meaningful lateral in most species); horizontal beam lateral or craniocaudal views may supplement; positioning in a radiolucent container or on a raised platform allows inclusion of limbs
- **Snakes**: coil naturally on cassette for survey; targeted views of specific body regions as indicated; use anatomical landmarks (head-to-cloaca distance) to localize findings
- **Lizards**: standard VD and lateral views; restraint in a clear plastic tube or bag may assist; tail should be included for assessment of metabolic bone disease and fractures
- **Crocodilians**: DV and lateral views; heavy sedation or anesthesia required for all but neonates

#### Mammals
- Standard small animal positioning principles apply with species-specific modifications
- **Bats**: extend wings for VD view (tape to cassette surface); lateral view of body for abdominal/thoracic assessment
- **Marine mammals**: large body size requires high-output equipment; positioning on foam pads; lateral decubitus views are standard for pinnipeds
- **Large ungulates**: standing lateral radiography may be necessary when recumbency is not feasible; portable units are essential

#### Amphibians and Fish
- **Amphibians**: DV and lateral views; place in shallow water on cassette surface or use wet paper towels to prevent desiccation during imaging
- **Fish**: lateral view (standard); DV may be obtained by placing fish dorsal side up; keep moist at all times; anesthesia in MS-222 or buffered tricaine maintains immobility

### Technical Considerations

| Factor | Wildlife Application |
|---|---|
| kVp and mAs | Vary enormously with body size (2g hummingbird to 2000kg elephant); tabletop technique for small patients, grid for large |
| Focal-film distance | Standard 100 cm; reduce to 75 cm for portable field units to increase intensity |
| Digital radiography | Preferred for wildlife practice — wide exposure latitude compensates for suboptimal technique in field settings |
| Magnification | Minimize by placing patient close to the detector; important for accurate measurements |
| Scatter reduction | Grid required for patients >10 cm thick; collimation critical for all sizes |

### Common Radiographic Findings in Wildlife

#### Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)
- Extremely common in captive reptiles, primates, and young birds
- Radiographic signs: generalized osteopenia, cortical thinning, pathological fractures, folding fractures (greenstick), bowing deformities
- In wild populations: may indicate nutritional deficiency, lead toxicosis, or renal disease

#### Trauma
- Fractures: common in birds (collision injuries), reptiles (vehicular), mammals (traps, fences)
- Gunshot: lead pellets or bullet fragments visible; associated fractures, soft tissue damage
- Spinal injuries: vertebral fractures, luxations, disc disease (rare in wildlife)

#### Foreign Bodies
- Ingested fishhooks (piscivorous birds, marine mammals)
- Lead shot/fragments (raptors, waterfowl, scavengers)
- Fishing line (external and ingested)
- Anthropogenic debris in gastrointestinal tract

#### Respiratory Disease
- Birds: air sac opacity, pulmonary consolidation, aspergillosis nodules
- Mammals: pulmonary infiltrates, pleural effusion, pneumothorax
- Reptiles: increased pulmonary opacity (pneumonia is common in debilitated reptiles)

#### Coelomic/Abdominal Assessment
- Egg retention/dystocia in birds and reptiles
- Organomegaly (hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, renomegaly)
- Coelomic effusion
- Gastrointestinal obstruction or ileus
- Uroliths (especially in chelonians and iguanids)

## Ultrasonography

### Applications

Ultrasonography provides real-time assessment of soft tissue structures without ionizing radiation. It is particularly valuable for:

- Cardiac evaluation (echocardiography)
- Reproductive assessment (follicular development, pregnancy diagnosis, egg abnormalities)
- Abdominal/coelomic organ evaluation
- Guided sampling (fine-needle aspirates, biopsies, cystocentesis)
- Fluid detection (effusions, abscesses)
- Musculoskeletal soft tissue assessment

### Species-Specific Approaches

#### Birds
- **Acoustic windows**: ventral midline approach (through thin body wall musculature), interclavicular approach
- **Transducer selection**: high-frequency linear or microconvex probes (10-18 MHz for small birds, 7.5-12 MHz for larger species)
- **Limitations**: air sacs create acoustic barriers; imaging is limited to structures immediately adjacent to the body wall
- **Key applications**: liver assessment, reproductive status, cardiac evaluation, guided aspiration of coelomic effusion

#### Reptiles
- **Chelonians**: prefemoral acoustic window (cranial to hindlimb, ventral to carapace bridge) provides access to coelomic organs; cervical approach for thyroid
- **Lizards and snakes**: ventral approach through body wall; scales may attenuate ultrasound — apply generous coupling gel or use standoff pad
- **Key applications**: follicular development, egg assessment, liver and kidney evaluation, abscess characterization, cardiac assessment

#### Mammals
- Standard small/large animal ultrasonographic approaches apply with anatomical modifications
- **Marine mammals**: thick blubber layer attenuates ultrasound — use lower frequency probes (2-5 MHz); waterproof probes allow in-water examination
- **Bats**: high-frequency probes (15-20 MHz) required for small body size
- **Key applications**: pregnancy diagnosis, thoracic assessment in marine mammals, abdominal organ evaluation

#### Amphibians
- High-frequency probes in a water bath provide excellent imaging
- Assess reproductive status, coelomic organs, and cardiac function

### Field Ultrasonography

Portable, battery-powered ultrasound units have made field ultrasonography increasingly practical:

- Handheld and tablet-based units are lightweight and durable
- Useful for pregnancy determination in captured large mammals
- Field-guided aspirates and biopsies
- Assessment of capture-related complications (pneumothorax, hemorrhage)
- Limitations: screen visibility in bright sunlight, limited probe selection, image storage capacity

## Computed Tomography (CT)

### Availability and Indications

CT provides cross-sectional imaging with excellent bony and soft tissue detail. While primarily available at referral centers and veterinary teaching hospitals, CT is increasingly used for wildlife patients:

- **Skull and head assessment**: fractures, sinusitis, otitis, dental disease, orbital masses
- **Spinal evaluation**: vertebral fractures, disc disease, spinal cord compression
- **Thoracic imaging**: pulmonary pathology in birds and mammals (superior to radiography for detail)
- **Orthopedic planning**: complex fracture assessment before surgical repair
- **Vascular assessment**: contrast-enhanced CT angiography

### Wildlife-Specific Considerations

- General anesthesia required for all wildlife CT examinations
- Size range presents challenges: scanners may accommodate a hummingbird or a seal but not an elephant
- Motion artifact must be controlled through adequate anesthetic depth
- Respiratory gating may improve thoracic image quality in mammals
- IV contrast administration requires reliable venous access
- Hounsfield unit reference values for soft tissues are generally transferable across species; bone density values may vary

### Avian CT

CT is particularly valuable in avian medicine:
- Air sacs provide natural contrast, allowing excellent delineation of coelomic structures
- Superior to radiography for detecting aspergillosis lesions
- Sinus and middle ear disease assessment
- Evaluation of the proventriculus and ventriculus

## Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

### Applications

MRI provides superior soft tissue contrast compared to CT and is the modality of choice for:

- Central nervous system evaluation (brain, spinal cord)
- Soft tissue tumor characterization
- Joint and ligament assessment
- Ocular pathology

### Practical Limitations in Wildlife

- Requires prolonged anesthesia (30-60+ minutes)
- Limited availability (academic and referral institutions)
- Metallic implants and foreign bodies (shot, microchips) create artifacts
- Species-specific coil selection challenging (size range, body shape)
- Cost considerations often limit use to high-value conservation cases or research

### When MRI Is Justified

- Endangered species with neurological signs where diagnosis will alter management
- Research imaging for anatomical studies
- Cases where CT findings are equivocal and soft tissue detail would change treatment decisions
- Rehabilitation patients with complex soft tissue injuries where surgical planning requires MRI guidance

## Field-Portable Imaging Units

### Digital Radiography in the Field

- Portable X-ray generators (battery-powered or vehicle-powered) with digital receptors enable field radiography
- Applications: capture-related injury assessment, pre-release screening of rehabilitated animals, field necropsy documentation
- Radiation safety: establish controlled area, use lead aprons and thyroid shields, maximize distance during exposure, use remote triggers

### Portable Ultrasound

- Handheld and laptop-based units suitable for field conditions
- Withstand temperature and humidity extremes of field environments
- Useful for rapid assessment during capture operations
- Image quality has improved dramatically in recent years

### Endoscopy

While not strictly diagnostic imaging, rigid and flexible endoscopy provides direct visualization of internal structures:

- **Avian endoscopy**: air sac examination (aspergillosis), gender determination, laparoscopy
- **Gastrointestinal endoscopy**: foreign body retrieval, biopsy
- **Respiratory endoscopy**: tracheal examination, bronchoalveolar lavage
- Portable endoscopy units are available for field use

## Interpretation Differences from Domestic Species

### Anatomical Variations

Wildlife veterinarians must be familiar with normal anatomical variations:

- **Birds**: air sacs, pneumatized bones, proventriculus-ventriculus complex, absence of diaphragm, renal morphology
- **Reptiles**: spectacle over eyes, lack of diaphragm, variable lung morphology (unicameral to multicameral), paired or single functional organs (depending on species)
- **Amphibians**: absence of thoracic-abdominal compartmentalization, variable ossification
- **Marine mammals**: hyperstotic (thickened) ribs in manatees, cervical vertebral fusion in cetaceans, relative organ positions altered by body plan
- **Bats**: elongated forelimb bones, thin wing membrane, compact thorax

### Normal vs. Pathological

- Physiological egg production in birds and reptiles may mimic pathological organomegaly on imaging
- Seasonal fat deposition (pre-hibernation, pre-migration) alters organ visualization and body condition assessment
- Parasitic granulomas are common incidental findings in wildlife — correlation with clinical significance is essential
- Pneumatized bones may be confused with lytic lesions; compare bilaterally
- Crop contents in birds may mimic soft tissue masses on lateral radiographs

### Reference Resources

- Comparative radiographic atlases are invaluable: multiple published atlases exist for raptors, psittacines, waterfowl, reptiles, and small exotic mammals
- Institutional image archives serve as practical references for species frequently encountered
- Contralateral limb comparison is useful for subtle orthopedic abnormalities
- Consultation with radiologists experienced in non-domestic species improves diagnostic accuracy
