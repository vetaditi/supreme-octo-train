# Wildlife Disease Monitoring and Surveillance Program Design

## Introduction

Systematic disease surveillance in wildlife populations is essential for early detection of emerging threats, monitoring endemic disease dynamics, and informing management decisions. Unlike livestock or companion animal medicine, wildlife disease surveillance must contend with free-ranging populations, limited access, unknown denominators, and logistical challenges that demand creative and adaptive program design.

## Surveillance Types

### Passive Surveillance

Passive surveillance relies on the detection and investigation of sick or dead animals reported opportunistically. It is the backbone of most wildlife disease monitoring programs.

**Advantages:**
- Low cost relative to active programs
- Broad geographic and taxonomic coverage
- Can detect unexpected diseases or novel pathogens
- Provides baseline mortality data over time

**Limitations:**
- Detection bias — conspicuous species and accessible areas are overrepresented
- Low sensitivity — subclinical infections and diseases of cryptic species go undetected
- Reporting depends on public awareness and willingness to report
- Specimens may be degraded before collection

**Components of effective passive surveillance:**
- Public reporting hotlines and online submission portals
- Training for wildlife agency field staff, hunters, and conservation officers
- Established relationships with wildlife rehabilitation centers as sentinel sites
- Rapid response protocols for mortality event investigation
- Necropsy and diagnostic capacity (in-house or through diagnostic laboratory partnerships)

### Active Surveillance

Active surveillance involves systematic, targeted sampling of wildlife populations to detect specific pathogens or assess health status.

**Advantages:**
- Defined sampling frame allows estimation of prevalence and incidence
- Can detect subclinical infections and carriers
- Consistent methodology enables temporal and spatial comparisons
- Targeted to specific diseases, species, or risk areas

**Limitations:**
- Resource-intensive (personnel, equipment, laboratory costs)
- Requires animal capture or hunter-harvest sampling infrastructure
- May cause disturbance or stress to wildlife populations
- Results apply only to the disease and population targeted

**Common active surveillance approaches:**

| Approach | Method | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Hunter-harvest sampling | Collection of samples from hunter-killed animals | CWD surveillance in cervids, AI in waterfowl |
| Trap-and-release surveys | Capture, sample, and release live animals | Hantavirus serosurveys in rodents |
| Serological surveys | Blood collection for antibody detection | Brucellosis in bison and elk |
| Environmental sampling | Collection of feces, water, soil, or carcass swabs | Environmental DNA for pathogen detection |
| Remote sampling | Hair snares, fecal collection, camera traps | Tuberculosis in badgers, stress hormone analysis |

### Targeted Surveillance

Targeted surveillance focuses on specific diseases in defined populations or geographic areas based on risk assessment:

- Border areas adjacent to regions with known outbreaks
- Populations at the wildlife-livestock interface
- Species known to be reservoir hosts for pathogens of concern
- Areas with historical disease occurrence
- Populations undergoing translocation or reintroduction

### Syndromic Surveillance

Syndromic surveillance monitors clinical syndromes or mortality patterns rather than specific pathogens:

- Mortality event triggers (e.g., >5 animals of same species found dead within defined area/timeframe)
- Neurological syndrome monitoring in multiple taxa
- Respiratory syndrome tracking in waterfowl and shorebirds
- Reproductive failure monitoring in colonial species

## Sentinel Species Programs

Certain wildlife species serve as effective sentinels for environmental or disease threats due to their ecological characteristics:

### Characteristics of Good Sentinel Species
- Sensitive to the agent or contaminant of interest
- Widespread and abundant enough to provide adequate samples
- Ecologically positioned to reflect exposure (e.g., top predators for bioaccumulation)
- Biologically well-characterized with available baseline health data
- Accessible for sampling (e.g., nesting colonies, hunter-harvested species)

### Examples

| Sentinel Species | Monitored Threat | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Bald eagles | Lead, rodenticides, environmental contaminants | Apex predator, scavenger, bioaccumulation |
| House finches | *Mycoplasma gallisepticum* | Susceptible, visible at feeders, nationwide range |
| Prairie dogs | Plague (*Yersinia pestis*) | Highly susceptible, colony die-offs are conspicuous |
| Honey bees | Pesticide exposure, environmental quality | Foraging exposes them to diverse environmental contaminants |
| Mussels/bivalves | Water quality, pathogens, contaminants | Filter feeders that concentrate water-borne agents |
| Corvids (crows, jays) | West Nile virus | Highly susceptible, die-offs precede human cases |
| Marine mammals | Ocean health, biotoxins, emerging pathogens | Long-lived, apex predators, strand on beaches |

## Mortality Event Monitoring

### Defining a Mortality Event

A wildlife mortality event is an unusual increase in deaths above expected baseline levels. Triggers for investigation typically include:

- Multiple dead animals of the same species in a localized area
- Deaths in species not commonly found dead (e.g., raptors, large mammals)
- Unusual clinical signs observed before death
- Concurrent mortality in multiple species (suggesting a shared exposure)
- Mortality in species of conservation concern regardless of numbers

### Response Protocol

1. **Initial report and assessment**
   - Record species, number, location (GPS), date, environmental context
   - Assess whether the event requires immediate response or can be investigated on the next available day
   - Identify potential human or domestic animal health risks

2. **Field investigation**
   - Collect fresh carcasses for necropsy (prioritize recently dead animals)
   - Document environmental conditions (weather, water levels, algal blooms, land use)
   - Record signs of trauma, predation, or other non-disease mortality factors
   - Photograph the site and carcasses in situ
   - Collect water and substrate samples if environmental cause suspected

3. **Necropsy and sampling**
   - Conduct systematic gross examination
   - Collect a comprehensive sample set based on differential diagnoses
   - Preserve tissues in formalin for histopathology and fresh/frozen for microbiology, virology, and toxicology

4. **Laboratory diagnostics**
   - Submit to qualified wildlife disease diagnostic laboratory
   - Communicate suspected differentials and field observations to guide testing
   - Request archiving of remaining tissues for future analysis

5. **Reporting and communication**
   - Report findings to wildlife management agencies
   - Notify public health authorities if zoonotic agent confirmed or suspected
   - Report WOAH-listed diseases to national veterinary authority
   - Issue public advisories if warranted

## National and International Reporting Systems

### WOAH (World Organisation for Animal Health)

WOAH maintains a list of notifiable diseases that member countries are obligated to report, including diseases that occur in wildlife. Reporting through the World Animal Health Information System (WAHIS) includes:

- Immediate notification of first occurrence of a listed disease
- Follow-up reports on ongoing outbreaks
- Six-monthly reports on disease presence/absence
- Annual reports on wildlife disease status

### National Wildlife Health Databases

- **United States**: USGS National Wildlife Health Center (NWHC) maintains the Wildlife Health Information Sharing Partnership (WHISPers) database for wildlife mortality events
- **Canada**: Canadian Wildlife Health Cooperative (CWHC) provides regional diagnostic services and national disease reporting
- **Europe**: European Wildlife Disease Association network; individual country systems vary
- **Australia**: Wildlife Health Australia coordinates national surveillance and reporting

### Regional and State/Provincial Systems

Many jurisdictions maintain their own wildlife disease databases and reporting requirements:

- State wildlife agencies may require mandatory reporting of certain conditions (e.g., CWD in cervids)
- Fish and wildlife health programs conduct regional surveillance
- Tribal and indigenous wildlife management programs contribute local data

## Citizen Science Contributions

### Value of Public Participation

Citizen science programs dramatically expand the geographic and temporal reach of surveillance:

- **eBird** and **iNaturalist** — platforms that can detect unusual mortality or morbidity when observers report sick or dead wildlife
- **Project FeederWatch** — long-term monitoring that can detect disease emergence in bird populations (e.g., *Mycoplasma* conjunctivitis in finches)
- **Bat monitoring programs** — acoustic surveys and roost counts that detect population declines indicative of disease impact
- **Amphibian monitoring** — volunteer surveys that detect mass mortality events or population declines from chytridiomycosis

### Limitations

- Identification accuracy varies among participants
- Reporting bias toward charismatic, conspicuous species and accessible habitats
- Data quality requires validation and standardization
- Training and engagement require sustained investment

### Best Practices for Integrating Citizen Science

- Provide clear reporting criteria and simple submission tools (mobile apps, web portals)
- Offer training in species identification, recognizing signs of disease, and safe handling
- Validate reported events through expert follow-up
- Provide feedback to reporters to maintain engagement
- Integrate citizen science data with professional surveillance databases

## Designing a Surveillance Program

### Key Steps

1. **Define objectives** — what disease(s) or health parameter(s) will be monitored and why
2. **Identify target population** — species, geographic area, demographic segments
3. **Select surveillance approach** — passive, active, targeted, or combination
4. **Determine sample size** — based on expected prevalence, desired confidence level, and population size (standard epidemiological calculations)
5. **Establish sampling protocol** — collection methods, preservation, transport, laboratory submission
6. **Secure resources** — funding, personnel, laboratory agreements, equipment
7. **Implement quality assurance** — standard operating procedures, training, data management
8. **Analyze and report** — statistical analysis, mapping, regular reporting to stakeholders
9. **Evaluate and adapt** — periodic review of program effectiveness, adjustment based on findings

### Sample Size Considerations

For surveys to detect disease presence at a given prevalence with a specified confidence:

| Target Prevalence | 95% Confidence (infinite pop.) | 99% Confidence (infinite pop.) |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | 29 | 44 |
| 5% | 59 | 90 |
| 2% | 149 | 228 |
| 1% | 299 | 459 |

These assume perfect diagnostic test sensitivity and specificity. Adjust upward for imperfect tests and finite populations.

## Data Management and Analysis

### Essential Data Fields

- Species (common and scientific name)
- Date and location (GPS coordinates)
- Sample type and condition
- Demographic data (sex, age class, body condition)
- Clinical observations or cause of death
- Diagnostic test results
- Environmental and ecological context

### Geospatial Analysis

Geographic information systems (GIS) are indispensable for wildlife disease surveillance:

- Mapping disease occurrence and spread over time
- Identifying spatial clusters and hotspots
- Modeling habitat and environmental risk factors
- Guiding targeted surveillance efforts
- Communicating findings to managers and the public

### Long-term Data Value

Sustained surveillance programs generate data of increasing value over time:

- Establishing baseline prevalence and geographic distribution
- Detecting trends and shifts in disease dynamics
- Identifying associations between disease and environmental variables
- Evaluating the effectiveness of management interventions
- Providing historical context for novel disease events
