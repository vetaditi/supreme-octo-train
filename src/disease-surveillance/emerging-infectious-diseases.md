# Emerging and Re-emerging Infectious Diseases

## Introduction

Emerging infectious diseases (EIDs) are those that have recently appeared in a population for the first time, or that have existed previously but are rapidly increasing in incidence or geographic range. Re-emerging diseases are those that were once controlled but are resurging. Wildlife veterinarians are positioned at the front line of EID detection because wildlife reservoirs are implicated in the majority of novel human pathogen emergence events.

## The Wildlife-Livestock-Human Interface

### Spillover Events

Pathogen spillover occurs when an infectious agent crosses from a reservoir host species into a novel host population. Spillover is not a single event but a multi-step process:

1. **Pathogen prevalence in the reservoir** — sufficient circulation to create exposure opportunities
2. **Inter-species contact** — direct, indirect, or vector-mediated exposure at the interface
3. **Compatibility** — the pathogen must be able to infect cells of the new host (receptor binding, immune evasion)
4. **Within-host establishment** — replication sufficient to cause infection
5. **Onward transmission** — sustained transmission in the new host population (not required for individual spillover but necessary for epidemic emergence)

### Interface Zones

The wildlife-livestock-human interface is particularly intense in certain settings:

- **Peri-urban and agricultural frontiers** — where habitat conversion brings wildlife, livestock, and people into close proximity
- **Live animal markets and wildlife trade** — concentration of diverse species under stress, facilitating cross-species transmission
- **Shared grazing and water resources** — where wildlife and livestock comingle
- **Bat roosts near human settlements** — tropical regions where fruit bats overlap with agricultural and residential areas
- **Aquaculture and coastal zones** — where marine wildlife, farmed fish, and human activity converge

## Drivers of Disease Emergence

### Habitat Encroachment and Land Use Change

Deforestation, agricultural expansion, and urbanization fragment wildlife habitats and increase contact rates at interfaces. Studies have demonstrated that areas of recent deforestation have elevated risk of zoonotic spillover, particularly for bat-borne viruses.

- Forest fragmentation concentrates wildlife into smaller patches, increasing density and stress
- Edge habitats create novel ecological communities where species that would not normally interact come into contact
- Reduced biodiversity (the "dilution effect" hypothesis) may increase transmission of some pathogens by concentrating competent reservoir hosts

### Climate Change

Climate change alters disease dynamics through multiple mechanisms:

- **Range shifts of vectors** — ticks, mosquitoes, and midges expanding into previously unsuitable latitudes and altitudes
- **Altered phenology** — changes in migration timing, breeding seasons, and hibernation affecting host-pathogen dynamics
- **Extreme weather events** — flooding, drought, and storms that concentrate animals, contaminate water sources, and cause nutritional stress
- **Arctic thaw** — release of preserved pathogens and increased access to previously ice-covered regions
- **Ocean warming** — expansion of marine pathogens and harmful algal blooms

### Wildlife Trade

The legal and illegal wildlife trade moves billions of animals across international borders annually:

- Live animals from diverse geographic origins are concentrated in markets, creating opportunities for cross-species pathogen exchange
- Stress of capture, transport, and captivity increases susceptibility and shedding
- Inadequate biosecurity in trade chains facilitates pathogen amplification
- The exotic pet trade introduces novel host-pathogen combinations into new environments

### Other Drivers

- **Antimicrobial resistance** — environmental contamination with antimicrobial compounds selects for resistant organisms in wildlife
- **Intensive animal agriculture** — high-density livestock operations adjacent to wildlife habitats amplify certain pathogens
- **Global travel and transport** — rapid movement of people, animals, and goods links distant disease systems
- **Human demographic changes** — population growth, immunocompromised populations, and aging demographics alter susceptibility

## Major Examples of Emerging Diseases with Wildlife Origins

### Ebola Virus Disease

- **Agent**: Ebola virus (family *Filoviridae*, genus *Ebolavirus*), five recognized species
- **Suspected reservoir**: Fruit bats (Pteropodidae), though virus has not been consistently isolated from bats
- **Spillover pathway**: likely bat-to-human or bat-to-great ape-to-human via contact with bodily fluids or consumption of bushmeat
- **Wildlife impact**: devastating mortality in western lowland gorillas and chimpanzees; estimated >90% mortality in some gorilla populations in Central Africa
- **Key features**: hemorrhagic fever with case fatality rates of 25-90% depending on species/strain; repeated emergence in Central and West Africa
- **Surveillance role**: monitoring great ape mortality as a sentinel for Ebola circulation; bat sampling programs to define reservoir ecology

### Nipah Virus

- **Agent**: Nipah virus (family *Paramyxoviridae*, genus *Henipavirus*)
- **Reservoir**: Pteropid fruit bats (*Pteropus* spp.)
- **Spillover pathway**: bat urine/saliva contaminating date palm sap (Bangladesh) or fruit; pig amplification (Malaysia, 1998-99)
- **Wildlife considerations**: subclinical infection in bats; seasonal shedding patterns correlated with breeding and birthing
- **Key features**: encephalitis and respiratory disease in humans, case fatality rate 40-75%; limited person-to-person transmission documented
- **Prevention**: excluding bats from date palm sap collection sites; surveillance of bat populations for viral shedding

### SARS-CoV Origins and SARS-CoV-2

- **SARS-CoV (2002-2003)**: originated in horseshoe bats (*Rhinolophus* spp.), with palm civets (*Paguma larvata*) and raccoon dogs (*Nyctereutes procyonoides*) as probable intermediate hosts in live animal markets
- **SARS-CoV-2 (2019-present)**: closest known relatives in horseshoe bats; intermediate host debated; live animal market in Wuhan implicated epidemiologically
- **Reverse zoonosis**: SARS-CoV-2 has spilled back into wildlife (white-tailed deer, mink, several zoo species), creating new reservoirs with potential for viral evolution and re-emergence
- **Lessons**: wildlife-origin coronaviruses pose ongoing pandemic risk; surveillance of bat coronaviruses, market biosecurity, and monitoring of spillback into wildlife are critical

### White-Nose Syndrome

- **Agent**: *Pseudogymnoascus destructans* — psychrophilic (cold-loving) fungus
- **Hosts**: hibernating insectivorous bats in North America; European bats carry the fungus with limited mortality (suggesting co-evolution)
- **Origin**: introduced from Europe to North America, first detected in New York in 2006
- **Impact**: killed an estimated 6-7 million bats in North America; some species (e.g., little brown bat, northern long-eared bat, tricolored bat) have experienced >90% population declines
- **Pathology**: fungal invasion of wing membrane during hibernation causing disruption of torpor, electrolyte imbalance, dehydration, and death
- **Surveillance**: visual surveys of hibernacula, wing scoring (UV fluorescence), qPCR of skin swabs for fungal DNA, environmental sampling of cave substrates
- **Management challenges**: no proven population-level treatment; decontamination protocols for researchers; cave closures; research into probiotics and antifungal treatments

### Chronic Wasting Disease

- **Agent**: misfolded prion protein (PrP^CWD^) — a transmissible spongiform encephalopathy (TSE)
- **Hosts**: cervids — mule deer, white-tailed deer, elk, moose, reindeer/caribou
- **Distribution**: expanding across North America, detected in Scandinavia and South Korea
- **Transmission**: direct contact, environmental contamination (prions bind to soil particles and remain infectious for years), possibly via plants
- **Clinical signs**: progressive weight loss (wasting), behavioral changes, excessive salivation, ataxia, polydipsia, polyuria; invariably fatal
- **Diagnostic approach**:
  - **Live animal**: rectal mucosal biopsy or tonsil biopsy with IHC
  - **Post-mortem**: IHC or ELISA of retropharyngeal lymph nodes and obex (brainstem)
  - **Emerging**: RT-QuIC (real-time quaking-induced conversion) assay on lymphoid tissue, blood, feces, or environmental samples
- **Management**: hunter-harvest surveillance programs, carcass disposal regulations, movement restrictions, population reduction in endemic areas
- **Zoonotic concern**: no confirmed human cases, but experimental evidence of cross-species barrier concern; prudent to avoid consumption of CWD-positive animals

## Preparedness and Response

### Surveillance Strategies for EIDs

- **Syndromic surveillance**: monitoring for unusual morbidity or mortality patterns without targeting a specific pathogen
- **Pathogen discovery**: metagenomic sequencing of wildlife samples to characterize viral diversity in high-risk taxa (bats, rodents, primates)
- **Interface surveillance**: targeted sampling at wildlife-livestock-human interface zones
- **Sentinel monitoring**: tracking health indicators in sentinel species that are early indicators of environmental change

### Response Frameworks

When a novel disease event is detected in wildlife:

1. **Confirm and characterize** — verify the event, identify the pathogen, determine geographic scope
2. **Assess risk** — evaluate potential for spread, wildlife conservation impact, livestock implications, zoonotic potential
3. **Communicate** — notify relevant authorities (WOAH, national veterinary services, public health agencies), issue wildlife health alerts
4. **Investigate** — conduct epidemiological investigation including case definition, active surveillance, environmental sampling
5. **Manage** — implement control measures appropriate to the pathogen and context (biosecurity, movement restrictions, population management, treatment or vaccination if available)
6. **Monitor** — ongoing surveillance to track disease trajectory and evaluate management effectiveness

### Biosecurity for Wildlife Professionals

Wildlife veterinarians working at the EID interface should maintain:

- Current risk awareness for their geographic and taxonomic areas of practice
- Appropriate PPE for the level of risk (minimum: gloves and eye protection; upgraded for aerosol-generating procedures or BSL-3 pathogens)
- Decontamination protocols for equipment between sites to prevent pathogen translocation
- Pre-exposure vaccinations where available (rabies, influenza) and awareness of post-exposure protocols
- Mental health support for responders to large-scale mortality events

## The Future of EID Surveillance

Advances in sequencing technology, environmental DNA/RNA detection, remote sensing, and computational modeling are transforming wildlife disease surveillance. However, the fundamental requirement remains the same: trained wildlife veterinarians in the field, collecting quality samples, conducting thorough investigations, and collaborating across disciplines to detect and respond to emerging threats before they become pandemics.
