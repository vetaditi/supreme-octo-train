# Diagnostic Laboratory Techniques for Wildlife

## Introduction

Laboratory diagnostics in wildlife medicine present unique challenges. Reference ranges are unavailable for many species, validated assays may not exist for the taxon of interest, and sample quality from field collection is often suboptimal. Despite these limitations, modern laboratory techniques provide powerful tools for disease diagnosis, pathogen surveillance, and health assessment in wildlife populations. This chapter reviews the major categories of diagnostic laboratory methods and their application to wildlife medicine.

## Serology

Serological methods detect antibodies (or antigens) in serum or plasma and are widely used for surveillance and retrospective diagnosis. Key considerations for wildlife include the limited availability of species-specific conjugates and the need to validate assays for each new species.

### Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

- **Indirect ELISA**: detects antibodies in the test serum against a known antigen bound to a plate; requires species-specific or cross-reactive secondary antibody conjugate
- **Competitive (blocking) ELISA**: does not require species-specific conjugate; the test serum competes with a labeled antibody for antigen binding; applicable across many species
- **Capture ELISA**: detects antigen in a sample using paired antibodies; useful for pathogen detection (e.g., F1 antigen of *Yersinia pestis*)

**Wildlife considerations:**
- Competitive ELISAs are preferred for multi-species surveillance programs because they are species-independent
- Protein A and Protein G conjugates can substitute for species-specific anti-immunoglobulin conjugates and bind IgG from a range of mammalian species (limited cross-reactivity in birds, reptiles, and amphibians)
- Cutoff values established for domestic species may not be appropriate for wildlife; validation in the target species is strongly recommended

### Virus Neutralization (VN) Test

- Measures functional antibodies that can neutralize viral infectivity in cell culture
- Considered the gold standard for many viral serology applications
- Highly specific but labor-intensive and requires viable virus and cell culture facilities
- Useful for confirming ELISA results and determining antibody titers
- Species-independent (measures functional activity, not antibody species)

### Hemagglutination Inhibition (HI)

- Standard serological assay for influenza and paramyxoviruses
- Detects antibodies that inhibit viral hemagglutination of red blood cells
- Species-independent; widely used in avian influenza surveillance
- Subtype-specific (uses known viral antigens)
- Requires fresh red blood cells (typically chicken or turkey for avian influenza)

### Other Serological Methods

- **Fluorescent antibody (FA/IFA)**: detects antibodies using fluorescently labeled conjugates; used for hantavirus, Toxoplasma, and many other agents
- **Western blot**: confirmatory test with high specificity; identifies antibodies to specific protein bands
- **Complement fixation (CF)**: historical method still used for some diseases; detects complement-fixing antibodies
- **Fluorescence polarization assay (FPA)**: rapid, species-independent; used for brucellosis surveillance
- **Lateral flow assays**: rapid point-of-care tests; available for some wildlife diseases (e.g., canine distemper, parvovirus)

## Molecular Diagnostics

### Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

Conventional PCR amplifies specific DNA sequences for detection by gel electrophoresis:
- Highly sensitive and specific when primers are well-designed
- Can detect pathogen DNA in tissue, blood, swabs, and environmental samples
- Product can be sequenced for pathogen characterization and phylogenetics
- Used for bacteria, fungi, and DNA viruses

### Reverse Transcription PCR (RT-PCR)

- Converts RNA to cDNA before amplification
- Essential for RNA virus detection (influenza, coronaviruses, rabies, hantaviruses)
- Real-time RT-PCR (RT-qPCR) allows quantification of viral load
- Standard method for avian influenza screening (matrix gene RT-qPCR)

### Real-Time (Quantitative) PCR (qPCR)

- Detects amplification in real time using fluorescent probes or dyes
- Provides quantitative data (pathogen load, gene copy number)
- Faster than conventional PCR (no post-amplification gel step)
- Reduced contamination risk (closed-tube system)
- Multiplex assays can detect multiple targets simultaneously

### Sequencing

#### Sanger Sequencing
- Targeted sequencing of PCR products
- Standard for pathogen identification and strain characterization
- Reliable, cost-effective for single gene targets
- Used for phylogenetic analysis and molecular epidemiology

#### Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS)
- High-throughput sequencing of millions of DNA/RNA fragments simultaneously
- Applications in wildlife disease diagnostics:
  - **Whole genome sequencing (WGS)**: complete pathogen genome for detailed characterization, transmission tracking, and virulence/resistance gene identification
  - **Amplicon sequencing**: targeted deep sequencing of specific genes (e.g., 16S rRNA for bacterial communities, ITS for fungi)
  - **Metagenomic sequencing**: unbiased sequencing of all nucleic acids in a sample — can identify unknown or unexpected pathogens

### Metagenomics

Metagenomic approaches are transforming wildlife pathogen discovery:

- **Viral metagenomics**: identifies novel viruses without prior knowledge of the pathogen; critical for emerging disease investigation
- **Bacterial metagenomics (16S/shotgun)**: characterizes entire microbial communities; useful for gut microbiome studies, environmental sampling
- **Challenges**: requires significant bioinformatic expertise, large reference databases, and careful controls to distinguish true positives from contamination or commensal organisms
- **Applications**: pathogen discovery in bat populations, characterizing viral diversity in wildlife reservoirs, investigating mortality events of unknown etiology

### Other Molecular Methods

- **LAMP (Loop-mediated isothermal amplification)**: isothermal amplification that does not require a thermocycler; field-deployable for point-of-care diagnostics
- **RT-QuIC (Real-time quaking-induced conversion)**: ultrasensitive detection of prions; emerging application for CWD surveillance
- **CRISPR-based diagnostics**: emerging rapid detection platforms (e.g., SHERLOCK, DETECTR) with potential for field use
- **Digital PCR**: absolute quantification without standard curves; high sensitivity for low-copy-number targets

## Bacteriology

### Culture and Identification

- Standard aerobic and anaerobic culture on appropriate media
- Selective and differential media for specific pathogens (e.g., MacConkey, blood agar, Lowenstein-Jensen for mycobacteria)
- Biochemical identification (API strips, Vitek systems)
- MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry for rapid species identification from colonies
- Incubation requirements vary: standard (24-48 h), mycobacteria (weeks to months), fastidious organisms (specialized media and conditions)

### Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

- Disk diffusion (Kirby-Bauer) or broth microdilution (MIC determination)
- CLSI breakpoints established for domestic species may not directly apply to wildlife
- Culture and sensitivity testing guides rational antimicrobial selection and contributes to AMR surveillance in wildlife populations

### Special Bacteriology

- **Mycobacteriology**: acid-fast staining (Ziehl-Neelsen), specialized culture (BACTEC MGIT, Lowenstein-Jensen), PCR for *Mycobacterium* species identification
- **Chlamydiology**: cell culture or PCR for *Chlamydia psittaci* and related species
- **Mycoplasma**: specialized media (Frey's or SP4), PCR preferred for routine detection

## Mycology

- **Direct examination**: KOH preparation, calcofluor white staining for fungi in clinical samples
- **Culture**: Sabouraud dextrose agar, dermatophyte test medium, specialized media for dimorphic fungi
- **Histopathology**: PAS and GMS staining for fungal elements in tissue sections
- **Molecular**: ITS sequencing for definitive species identification; qPCR for *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis* and *B. salamandrivorans*, *Pseudogymnoascus destructans*
- **Antifungal susceptibility**: broth microdilution (CLSI M38/M27) for clinically relevant isolates

## Histopathology

### Tissue Processing
- Fixed tissues are trimmed, processed through graded alcohols and xylene, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4-5 micrometers, and stained
- Standard stain: hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)
- Processing time: typically 24-48 hours after adequate fixation

### Special Stains

| Stain | Target | Application |
|---|---|---|
| Gram | Bacteria (gram-positive vs gram-negative) | Bacterial infection characterization |
| Ziehl-Neelsen (acid-fast) | Mycobacteria | Tuberculosis, mycobacteriosis |
| PAS (Periodic acid-Schiff) | Fungal elements, glycogen | Fungal infections, storage diseases |
| GMS (Grocott methenamine silver) | Fungal elements | Enhanced fungal detection |
| Warthin-Starry | Spirochetes, *Helicobacter* | Leptospirosis |
| Prussian blue | Hemosiderin (iron) | Iron storage, hemolysis |
| Congo red | Amyloid | Amyloidosis (with polarized light) |
| Von Kossa | Calcium deposits | Metastatic/dystrophic calcite |
| Masson trichrome | Collagen, muscle | Fibrosis assessment |

### Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

- Uses antibodies to detect specific antigens in fixed tissue sections
- Available for many pathogens: rabies virus, CWD prion (PrP^CWD^), West Nile virus, avian influenza, *Chlamydia*, *Toxoplasma*, and many others
- Also used for tissue characterization (cytokeratin, vimentin, CD markers for neoplasia classification)
- Requires validated primary antibodies; cross-reactivity across species should be confirmed

### Wildlife-Specific Considerations

- Limited histological reference material for many species — comparative atlases and experienced pathologists are invaluable
- Normal anatomical variations (e.g., melanin deposition, lymphoid tissue distribution) can be misinterpreted as pathology
- Autolytic changes are common in wildlife carcasses found in the field — assess tissue quality before over-interpreting findings
- Parasitic lesions are extremely common in wild animals and may be incidental rather than clinically significant

## Cytology

### Applications
- Impression smears from tissues and lesions (rapid field assessment)
- Fine-needle aspirates of masses, lymph nodes, effusions
- Body cavity fluid analysis (peritoneal, pleural, pericardial)
- Tracheal and bronchoalveolar washes
- Crop cytology in birds (yeast, *Trichomonas*, bacterial flora)
- Buffy coat smears for blood parasites

### Staining
- Diff-Quik (modified Romanowsky): rapid, field-appropriate
- Wright-Giemsa: standard hematology and cytology stain
- New methylene blue: wet-mount for reticulocyte counts and rapid assessment
- Gram stain: bacterial characterization in cytological preparations

### Interpretation
- Cytology provides rapid preliminary information but should be confirmed with histopathology when possible
- Inflammatory patterns (heterophilic/granulocytic vs lymphocytic vs granulomatous) guide differential diagnosis
- Intracellular organisms, neoplastic cells, and parasites may be identified on cytology
- Species-specific hematological features (e.g., nucleated red blood cells in birds, reptiles, and amphibians; heterophils vs neutrophils) require familiarity with comparative hematology

## Interpretation Considerations for Wildlife

### General Challenges

- **Reference ranges**: unavailable for most wild species; published ranges often based on small sample sizes from captive animals, which may differ from free-ranging populations
- **Capture effects**: catecholamine and cortisol responses alter hematology (stress leukogram, hyperglycemia, lymphopenia) and chemistry values within minutes of capture
- **Seasonal variation**: hibernation, migration, breeding, and nutritional cycles affect blood parameters
- **Age and sex effects**: juveniles, pregnant/lactating females, and aged animals may have different baseline values
- **Anesthetic effects**: some anesthetics alter hematology and chemistry parameters
- **Sample quality**: hemolysis, lipemia, and delayed processing are common in field-collected wildlife samples

### Practical Recommendations

- Compare results to the most relevant available reference data (same species, same context preferred)
- Interpret trends and patterns rather than absolute values when reference ranges are unavailable
- Always consider capture method and time-from-capture when interpreting hematology and chemistry
- Maintain a laboratory sample archive for future comparative analysis
- Contribute published reference data to the literature whenever adequate sample sizes are achieved
