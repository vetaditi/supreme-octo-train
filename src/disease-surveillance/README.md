# Disease Surveillance and Diagnostics

## Introduction

Disease surveillance in wildlife populations represents one of the most critical functions of the wildlife veterinarian. Unlike domestic animal medicine, where individual patient health is the primary concern, wildlife disease surveillance operates at the interface of population health, ecosystem integrity, and public health. The detection, characterization, and monitoring of diseases in free-ranging wildlife populations provide early warning systems for threats to biodiversity, livestock industries, and human communities.

## Importance of Wildlife Disease Surveillance

### Conservation Impact

Infectious and non-infectious diseases are increasingly recognized as significant drivers of wildlife population declines. Several species have been pushed toward extinction by disease events:

- **Chytrid fungus** (*Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis* and *B. salamandrivorans*) has caused catastrophic declines in amphibian populations worldwide
- **White-nose syndrome** (*Pseudogymnoascus destructans*) has killed millions of bats across North America
- **Devil facial tumor disease** has reduced Tasmanian devil populations by more than 80%
- **Canine distemper virus** has repeatedly devastated endangered African wild dog and black-footed ferret populations

Without systematic surveillance, these threats may go undetected until population-level impacts become irreversible.

### Public Health and One Health

The One Health framework recognizes the interconnection of human, animal, and environmental health. Approximately 60-75% of emerging infectious diseases in humans originate from animal reservoirs, and wildlife plays a central role in the maintenance and transmission of many zoonotic pathogens. Wildlife disease surveillance serves as a sentinel system, detecting pathogen circulation before spillover into human or livestock populations occurs.

Key One Health intersections include:

- **Zoonotic disease detection** — identifying pathogens such as avian influenza, rabies, hantaviruses, and coronaviruses in wildlife reservoirs
- **Antimicrobial resistance monitoring** — tracking resistance genes in wildlife that may reflect environmental contamination or serve as reservoirs
- **Environmental contamination** — documenting toxicological impacts that may indicate broader ecosystem and human health risks
- **Vector-borne disease ecology** — understanding wildlife hosts and arthropod vectors that drive disease transmission cycles

### Agricultural and Economic Significance

Wildlife populations serve as reservoirs for diseases that threaten livestock and poultry industries. Bovine tuberculosis in white-tailed deer and European badgers, brucellosis in bison and elk, and highly pathogenic avian influenza in wild waterfowl are examples where wildlife-livestock disease interfaces demand ongoing surveillance and management.

## The Role of the Wildlife Veterinarian

Wildlife veterinarians occupy a unique position in disease surveillance systems. Their responsibilities span multiple domains:

### Field Investigation

- Responding to wildlife mortality events and conducting field necropsies
- Collecting diagnostic samples under challenging field conditions
- Assessing population-level health indicators during capture operations
- Collaborating with wildlife biologists on health monitoring during research projects

### Diagnostic Interpretation

- Integrating clinical, pathological, and laboratory findings in species with limited reference data
- Recognizing that normal physiological parameters vary enormously across taxa
- Distinguishing disease-related findings from capture-related artifacts (capture myopathy, stress leukograms)
- Applying diagnostic results at the population rather than individual level

### Coordination and Communication

- Reporting notifiable diseases to appropriate authorities (WOAH, national veterinary services)
- Communicating disease risks to wildlife managers, public health officials, and the public
- Contributing data to national and international wildlife health databases
- Training field personnel in sample collection and biosafety protocols

### Research and Policy

- Designing and implementing surveillance programs appropriate to the disease and species of concern
- Contributing to risk assessments for wildlife translocations and reintroductions
- Advising on disease management strategies that balance conservation and public health objectives
- Publishing findings to advance the field of wildlife disease ecology

## Guiding Principles

Effective wildlife disease surveillance rests on several foundational principles:

- **Baseline data are essential** — understanding what is normal for a population before disease events occur
- **Surveillance must be sustained** — sporadic sampling cannot detect trends or emerging threats
- **Interdisciplinary collaboration is non-negotiable** — effective surveillance requires partnerships among veterinarians, ecologists, epidemiologists, and public health professionals
- **Diagnostic limitations must be acknowledged** — validated assays for domestic species may not perform equivalently in wildlife, and reference ranges are often unavailable
- **Ethical considerations apply** — sampling and handling must minimize stress, injury, and disturbance to wild animals and their habitats

The chapters that follow provide the practical and theoretical foundation for wildlife disease surveillance, equipping the veterinary practitioner to detect, diagnose, and respond to disease threats in wild animal populations.
