# Parasitology in Wildlife

## Introduction

Parasitism is the most common lifestyle in the animal kingdom, and wild animals harbor rich parasite communities that reflect their evolutionary history, ecology, and environment. Wildlife parasitology differs fundamentally from clinical parasitology in domestic species: in wildlife, the goal is rarely to eliminate parasites but rather to understand host-parasite dynamics, assess population health, and identify cases where parasitism becomes pathological due to environmental stressors, immunosuppression, or novel host-parasite encounters.

## Endoparasites

### Helminths

#### Nematodes (Roundworms)

Common nematode parasites of wildlife and their significance:

| Parasite | Hosts | Location | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Baylisascaris procyonis* | Raccoons (definitive), many aberrant hosts | Small intestine (raccoon), CNS (aberrant) | Zoonotic; fatal neural larva migrans in humans and wildlife |
| *Elaphostrongylus cervi* | Cervids | CNS, skeletal muscle | Neurological disease; climate-driven range expansion |
| *Parelaphostrongylus tenuis* (meningeal worm) | White-tailed deer (definitive), moose, caribou (aberrant) | Meninges | Subclinical in deer; fatal neurological disease in moose and caribou |
| *Anisakis* spp. | Marine mammals (definitive), fish (intermediate) | Stomach | Zoonotic from raw fish consumption |
| *Dirofilaria immitis* | Canids, mustelids, pinnipeds | Heart, pulmonary arteries | Heartworm; mosquito-transmitted |
| *Trichinella* spp. | Carnivores, omnivores (bears, wild boar) | Skeletal muscle (encysted larvae) | Zoonotic; public health significance in game meat |
| *Haemonchus contortus* | Ruminants including wild ungulates | Abomasum | Anemia, weight loss; resistance issues in livestock |
| Strongylid nematodes | Virtually all vertebrates | GI tract | Ubiquitous; pathological at high burdens |

#### Cestodes (Tapeworms)

- *Echinococcus multilocularis*: foxes (definitive host), rodents (intermediate); zoonotic — causes alveolar echinococcosis in humans; expanding range in North America and Europe
- *Echinococcus granulosus*: wolves/canids (definitive), ungulates (intermediate); hydatid cyst disease
- *Taenia* spp.: carnivore-prey life cycles; common in predator-prey systems
- *Diphyllobothrium* spp.: fish-eating mammals and birds; zoonotic from raw fish
- *Mesocestoides* spp.: carnivores and raptors; peritoneal larval cestodiasis

#### Trematodes (Flukes)

- *Fasciola hepatica*: liver fluke of ungulates; shared with livestock at wildlife-livestock interface
- *Schistosoma* spp.: blood flukes with aquatic snail intermediate hosts; wildlife reservoirs for human schistosomiasis
- *Alaria* spp.: intestinal fluke of canids; mesocercariae in paratenic hosts (frogs, snakes)
- Trematodes of waterfowl and wading birds: diverse species with complex aquatic life cycles
- *Ribeiroia ondatrae*: causes limb malformations in amphibians; linked to eutrophication

#### Acanthocephalans (Thorny-headed Worms)

- Common in fish, waterfowl, and marine mammals
- *Macracanthorhynchus* spp.: intestinal parasites of wild pigs and carnivores
- *Polymorphus* spp.: common in diving ducks; arthropod intermediate hosts
- Can cause intestinal perforation at high intensities

### Protozoa

#### Apicomplexans

- **Toxoplasma gondii**: felids (definitive hosts); virtually all warm-blooded vertebrates as intermediate hosts; significant cause of mortality in marine mammals (sea otters, Hawaiian monk seals), Australian marsupials, and endangered Hawaiian birds; zoonotic
- **Sarcocystis** spp.: predator-prey life cycles; generally subclinical but can cause fatal disease in some species (e.g., marine mammals)
- **Cryptosporidium** spp.: diverse species infecting the GI tract of reptiles, birds, and mammals; oocysts resistant to chlorination; zoonotic potential
- **Haemoparasites** (*Plasmodium*, *Haemoproteus*, *Leucocytozoon*): blood parasites of birds transmitted by arthropod vectors; avian malaria (*Plasmodium relictum*) has devastated Hawaiian honeycreepers
- **Babesia/Theileria**: tick-transmitted hemoparasites of mammals; emerging in wildlife at wildlife-livestock interfaces
- **Hepatozoon** spp.: tick-transmitted; common in reptiles and some mammals

#### Flagellates

- **Trypanosoma cruzi**: agent of Chagas disease; sylvatic cycle involves raccoons, opossums, woodrats, and triatomine bug vectors; zoonotic significance in the Americas
- **Trichomonas gallinae**: causes avian trichomonosis (frounce) in pigeons, doves, raptors; fatal upper GI tract necrosis in many raptor species; recent epidemic in European finches
- **Giardia** spp.: diverse assemblages in wildlife; zoonotic potential debated (assemblage A most concerning)
- **Leishmania** spp.: sand fly-transmitted; wildlife reservoirs in some regions (hyraxes, rodents)

#### Amoebae

- *Entamoeba invadens*: major pathogen of captive reptiles; causes fatal amoebic dysentery; relatively uncommon in free-ranging populations

## Ectoparasites

### Ticks

Ticks are among the most significant ectoparasites of wildlife due to their role as disease vectors:

- **Hard ticks (Ixodidae)**: *Ixodes* (Lyme disease, anaplasmosis), *Dermacentor* (Rocky Mountain spotted fever, tularemia), *Amblyomma* (ehrlichiosis, heartwater), *Haemaphysalis* (diverse pathogens)
- **Soft ticks (Argasidae)**: *Ornithodoros* (African swine fever, relapsing fever)
- Wildlife serve as maintenance hosts for tick populations and as reservoirs for tick-borne pathogens
- Tick surveillance on wildlife provides data on pathogen circulation and vector distribution
- Climate change is altering tick distributions, expanding ranges northward and to higher elevations

### Mites

- **Sarcoptic mange** (*Sarcoptes scabiei*): causes severe dermatitis and mortality in foxes, wolves, wombats, mountain gorillas, and many other species; can cause population-level declines
- **Demodex** spp.: common commensals in mammals; disease when immunosuppressed
- **Knemidokoptes** spp.: scaly leg and face mites of birds (parrots, passerines, raptors)
- **Chiggers** (Trombiculidae): larval stages parasitic on diverse wildlife; vectors for scrub typhus
- **Feather mites**: generally commensal on birds; diverse species assemblages; ecological indicators

### Lice

- **Chewing lice (Mallophaga)**: common on birds and some mammals; generally low pathogenicity except at high infestations
- **Sucking lice (Anoplura)**: mammalian ectoparasites; blood-feeding; can cause anemia and debilitation
- Host-specific; useful for understanding host evolutionary relationships and contact patterns

### Fleas

- **Siphonaptera**: parasitic on mammals and birds
- Vectors for plague (*Yersinia pestis*), murine typhus, myxomatosis (in rabbits)
- *Pulex irritans*, *Ctenocephalides* spp., *Oropsylla* spp. (prairie dog flea — plague vector)
- Flea surveys on wildlife contribute to plague surveillance in endemic areas

### Other Ectoparasites

- **Hippoboscid flies** (louse flies): parasitic on birds and mammals; vectors for blood parasites
- **Bot flies** (*Cuterebra*, *Oestridae*): larval stages cause subcutaneous or nasopharyngeal myiasis in rodents, lagomorphs, ungulates
- **Blow flies** (*Calliphoridae*): myiasis in debilitated wildlife; important in forensic entomology
- **Leeches**: ectoparasites of aquatic and semi-aquatic wildlife

## Parasite Surveys

### Objectives

Parasite surveys in wildlife serve multiple purposes:

- Establishing baseline parasite community composition for a host population
- Monitoring changes in parasite prevalence and intensity over time
- Identifying potential pathogenic parasites in species of conservation concern
- Assessing parasite transmission risk at wildlife-livestock interfaces
- Documenting parasite biodiversity (many wildlife parasites remain undescribed)
- Using parasites as biological indicators of environmental quality or host population connectivity

### Survey Design

- **Sample size**: adequate numbers to estimate prevalence with acceptable confidence intervals; minimum 30 animals recommended for presence/absence surveys
- **Seasonal timing**: parasite burdens vary seasonally; standardize collection timing or sample across seasons
- **Host demographics**: sample across age classes and sexes, as parasite burdens often vary with host age and sex
- **Quantitative methods**: use standardized methods that allow estimation of intensity (e.g., worm counts, eggs per gram) rather than simple presence/absence

## Fecal Examination Techniques

### Flotation

- **Simple flotation**: feces mixed with flotation solution (sodium chloride, zinc sulfate, sugar solution); parasite eggs and oocysts float to surface and are collected on a coverslip
- **Centrifugal flotation**: improved recovery through centrifugation; higher sensitivity than simple flotation
- **Flotation solutions**:
  - Sodium chloride (sp. gr. 1.18-1.20): inexpensive but distorts eggs rapidly
  - Zinc sulfate (sp. gr. 1.18): preserves morphology better; good for *Giardia* cysts
  - Sheather's sugar solution (sp. gr. 1.27): excellent for coccidian oocysts; does not distort eggs
  - Sodium nitrate (sp. gr. 1.18-1.20): commercial Fecasol; convenient but crystallizes

### Sedimentation

- Used for heavy eggs that do not float well (trematode eggs, acanthocephalan eggs)
- Formalin-ethyl acetate concentration: standard technique for diverse parasite recovery
- Allows recovery of both light and heavy parasite stages

### Quantitative Methods

- **McMaster technique**: standard quantitative flotation; estimates eggs per gram (EPG); useful for monitoring treatment efficacy and comparing infection intensity
- **Modified Wisconsin**: double centrifugation flotation; higher sensitivity than McMaster
- **Mini-FLOTAC**: newer quantitative method with good sensitivity and standardization

### Special Techniques

- **Baermann technique**: extraction of nematode larvae from feces using their active migration away from feces into warm water; essential for lungworm larvae
- **Fecal culture**: larvae hatched from eggs for identification to genus/species (L3 identification)
- **Direct smear**: rapid examination of fresh feces for motile protozoa (*Trichomonas*, *Giardia* trophozoites)
- **Acid-fast staining**: detection of *Cryptosporidium* oocysts in fecal smears
- **PCR**: molecular detection and speciation of parasites from fecal material; increasingly used for survey work

## Host-Parasite Dynamics in Wild Populations

### Aggregated Distribution

Parasite distributions in wildlife populations are characteristically aggregated (overdispersed): most hosts harbor few parasites, while a small proportion harbor very heavy burdens. This pattern has important implications:

- Mean parasite burden may not reflect the experience of most individuals
- Heavily parasitized individuals may be disproportionately important for transmission
- Aggregation is described by the negative binomial distribution, parameterized by mean and *k* (inverse measure of aggregation)

### Density-Dependent Effects

- Parasite transmission often increases with host density, creating feedback loops between host population dynamics and parasite burden
- Density-dependent parasite regulation may contribute to host population cycling (e.g., grouse-nematode cycles in the UK)
- Translocation and reintroduction programs must consider whether moving animals into naive populations introduces novel parasites

### Environmental Mediation

- Habitat quality influences parasite transmission: degraded habitats may increase or decrease parasite burden depending on the parasite's life cycle
- Climate change alters parasite development rates, vector distributions, and host susceptibility
- Pollution and contaminants can modulate immune function, altering host-parasite outcomes

## Parasite-Mediated Selection

### Evolutionary Significance

Parasites are a major selective force in wildlife evolution:

- **Red Queen hypothesis**: hosts and parasites engage in coevolutionary arms races, maintaining genetic diversity
- **MHC diversity**: major histocompatibility complex polymorphism is maintained in part by parasite-driven selection; populations with reduced MHC diversity (e.g., bottlenecked species) may be more susceptible to parasitism
- **Sexual selection**: Hamilton-Zuk hypothesis proposes that parasite resistance underlies mate choice signals (bright plumage, elaborate displays)
- **Behavioral ecology**: sickness behavior, parasite avoidance, and grooming allocations are shaped by parasite pressure

### Conservation Implications

- Captive breeding programs may inadvertently relax parasite-mediated selection, producing animals poorly adapted to parasites upon release
- Island populations and those that have lost parasites through bottlenecks may be hypersusceptible to reintroduced or novel parasites
- Managing parasites in conservation contexts requires balancing individual welfare with population-level evolutionary processes
- Complete deworming before release may not be beneficial if parasites contribute to immune priming and community resistance
