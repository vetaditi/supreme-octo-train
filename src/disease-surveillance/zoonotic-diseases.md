# Zoonotic Diseases in Wildlife

## Overview

Zoonotic diseases — those transmissible between animals and humans — represent a critical intersection of wildlife veterinary medicine and public health. Wildlife species serve as primary reservoirs for numerous pathogens of global significance. The wildlife veterinarian must be able to recognize clinical presentations, collect appropriate diagnostic samples, and communicate risks effectively to public health authorities and the general public.

## Rabies

### Etiology
Rabies virus (RABV), a negative-sense single-stranded RNA virus in the family *Rhabdoviridae*, genus *Lyssavirus*.

### Wildlife Reservoirs
- **North America**: raccoons, skunks, foxes, bats (multiple species)
- **Africa and Asia**: jackals, mongooses, bats
- **Europe**: red foxes, raccoon dogs, bats
- **Latin America**: vampire bats (*Desmodus rotundus*), dogs (feral)

### Transmission
Primarily through saliva via bite wounds. Aerosol transmission documented in bat caves. Vertical transmission is rare.

### Clinical Signs in Wildlife
- **Furious form**: aggression, loss of fear of humans, unprovoked attacks, excessive salivation, self-mutilation
- **Paralytic (dumb) form**: progressive paralysis, jaw drop, inability to swallow, ataxia
- Abnormal behavior is the most common initial sign — nocturnal animals active during daylight, unusual tameness

### Diagnostic Approach
- **Gold standard**: Direct fluorescent antibody (DFA) test on fresh brain tissue (hippocampus, brainstem, cerebellum)
- **Confirmatory**: RT-PCR for viral RNA, direct rapid immunohistochemistry test (dRIT)
- **Post-mortem**: Negri bodies on histopathology (pathognomonic but sensitivity ~50-80%)
- Ante-mortem diagnosis in live wildlife is not practical for management purposes

### Public Health Significance
Rabies causes approximately 59,000 human deaths annually worldwide. Bat-associated rabies variants are the leading cause of human rabies deaths in the Americas. Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is nearly 100% effective if administered promptly.

## Leptospirosis

### Etiology
*Leptospira interrogans* sensu lato — a spirochete bacterium with over 300 serovars organized into serogroups.

### Wildlife Reservoirs
- Rodents (primary maintenance hosts for many serovars)
- Raccoons, opossums, deer, wild boar, sea lions, bats

### Transmission
Contact with urine-contaminated water or soil, through mucous membranes or skin abrasions. Chronic renal shedding by carrier animals maintains environmental contamination.

### Clinical Signs in Wildlife
- Often subclinical in maintenance hosts
- Acute disease in incidental hosts: renal failure, hepatitis, hemolytic anemia, pulmonary hemorrhage
- California sea lions: reproductive failure, neonatal mortality, renal disease during outbreaks

### Diagnostic Approach
- **Serology**: microscopic agglutination test (MAT) — paired titers recommended; cross-reactivity between serogroups complicates interpretation
- **Molecular**: PCR on blood (acute phase) or urine (carrier detection)
- **Culture**: possible from blood or urine but slow (weeks to months) and requires specialized media (EMJH)
- **Histopathology**: silver staining (Warthin-Starry) of kidney sections

### Public Health Significance
Occupational risk for wildlife workers handling animals or working in contaminated aquatic environments. Outbreaks follow flooding events. Personal protective equipment (PPE), especially waterproof gloves, is essential during field work.

## Brucellosis

### Etiology
*Brucella abortus* (elk, bison), *B. suis* (feral swine, caribou, reindeer), *B. melitensis* (rarely in wildlife), *B. ceti/pinnipedialis* (marine mammals).

### Wildlife Reservoirs
- Greater Yellowstone Ecosystem: elk and bison (*B. abortus*)
- Feral swine: *B. suis* across range states
- Marine mammals: cetaceans and pinnipeds

### Transmission
Contact with reproductive fluids, aborted fetuses, placental material. Venereal transmission in some species. Ingestion of contaminated material.

### Clinical Signs in Wildlife
- Abortion (typically late gestation), retained placenta, metritis
- Orchitis, epididymitis in males
- Arthritis, hygroma (bison — particularly carpal hygromas)
- Subclinical carriage is common

### Diagnostic Approach
- **Serology**: card test, standard plate/tube agglutination, complement fixation, fluorescence polarization assay (FPA), competitive ELISA
- **Culture**: blood, reproductive tissues, lymph nodes — **BSL-3 pathogen**, handle accordingly
- **Molecular**: PCR on tissues and fluids
- Note: vaccination with RB51 or S19 in wildlife complicates serological interpretation

### Public Health Significance
Highly infectious via aerosol, mucous membrane, or skin contact. Significant occupational hazard during necropsy of infected animals. Undulant fever in humans — chronic debilitating illness.

## Hantavirus

### Etiology
Hantaviruses (family *Bunyavirales*, genus *Orthohantavirus*). Multiple species including Sin Nombre virus (SNV), Seoul virus, Andes virus.

### Wildlife Reservoirs
Specific rodent species serve as primary hosts:
- **Deer mouse** (*Peromyscus maniculatus*): Sin Nombre virus (North America)
- **Cotton rat** (*Sigmodon hispidus*): Black Creek Canal virus
- **Norway rat** (*Rattus norvegicus*): Seoul virus (worldwide)
- Each hantavirus is typically associated with a single rodent host species

### Transmission
Inhalation of aerosolized virus from rodent urine, feces, or saliva. Direct bite transmission. Person-to-person transmission rare (documented with Andes virus).

### Clinical Signs in Wildlife
Rodent reservoir hosts are generally asymptomatic with chronic infection and prolonged viral shedding. Experimental infections may show mild pathology.

### Diagnostic Approach
- **Serology**: ELISA, IFA for antibody detection in rodent populations
- **Molecular**: RT-PCR on lung tissue or blood for viral RNA
- **Surveillance**: trap-and-release serosurveys of rodent populations to assess prevalence

### Public Health Significance
Hantavirus pulmonary syndrome (HPS) has a case fatality rate of approximately 36% in North America. Wildlife workers entering rodent-contaminated structures should use N95 respirators and wet-decontamination protocols.

## Avian Influenza

### Etiology
Influenza A viruses (family *Orthomyxoviridae*). Classified by hemagglutinin (H1-H18) and neuraminidase (N1-N11) surface proteins. Pathotyped as low pathogenicity (LPAI) or highly pathogenic (HPAI) based on molecular characteristics and virulence in chickens.

### Wildlife Reservoirs
- **Primary reservoir**: Anseriformes (ducks, geese, swans) and Charadriiformes (shorebirds, gulls)
- Wild waterfowl maintain diverse LPAI subtypes, typically with subclinical infection
- HPAI H5 clade 2.3.4.4b has caused unprecedented mortality in wild birds globally since 2021

### Transmission
Fecal-oral route predominant in waterfowl (virus shed in high titers in feces). Respiratory transmission also occurs. Contaminated water bodies serve as environmental reservoirs.

### Clinical Signs in Wildlife
- **LPAI**: generally subclinical in reservoir species; mild respiratory signs possible
- **HPAI**: neurological signs (torticollis, circling, seizures), respiratory distress, sudden death, hemorrhagic lesions, significant mortality in colonial nesting seabirds, raptors, scavengers, and marine mammals

### Diagnostic Approach
- **Sample collection**: oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs in viral transport media
- **Screening**: real-time RT-PCR (matrix gene, then H5/H7 subtyping)
- **Confirmation**: virus isolation in embryonated eggs, sequencing for pathotype determination
- **Serology**: HI, ELISA (useful for surveillance, not acute diagnosis)

### Public Health Significance
Pandemic potential. Sporadic human infections with H5N1, H7N9, and other subtypes. Enhanced biosecurity and PPE required during HPAI outbreak investigations. Reportable to WOAH.

## Coronaviruses

### Etiology
Large family *Coronaviridae*, positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses. Genera include *Alphacoronavirus* and *Betacoronavirus* (mammalian hosts) and *Gammacoronavirus* and *Deltacoronavirus* (primarily avian hosts).

### Wildlife Reservoirs
- **Bats**: harbor the greatest diversity of coronaviruses; ancestral hosts for SARS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2 progenitors, and MERS-CoV progenitors
- **Rodents**: reservoirs for alphacoronaviruses
- **Wild birds**: gammacoronaviruses and deltacoronaviruses

### Transmission
Respiratory droplets, fecal-oral route, and potentially through intermediate hosts (civets for SARS-CoV, dromedary camels for MERS-CoV, debated for SARS-CoV-2).

### Clinical Signs in Wildlife
Generally subclinical in reservoir bat species. Pathology may occur in spillover hosts (e.g., respiratory and enteric disease in mink, white-tailed deer with SARS-CoV-2).

### Diagnostic Approach
- **Molecular**: pan-coronavirus RT-PCR targeting conserved RdRp gene, followed by sequencing
- **Serology**: surrogate virus neutralization tests, species-adapted ELISA platforms
- **Surveillance**: fecal and oral swab sampling of bat roosts, longitudinal serosurveys

### Public Health Significance
Three coronavirus pandemics/epidemics of animal origin in 20 years (SARS, MERS, COVID-19) underscore the importance of surveillance at wildlife-human interfaces.

## Plague

### Etiology
*Yersinia pestis* — gram-negative coccobacillus, family *Yersiniaceae*.

### Wildlife Reservoirs
- Rodents (prairie dogs, ground squirrels, wood rats) are amplifying hosts
- Rabbits and hares can serve as sources of human exposure
- Wild felids and canids may be infected through predation

### Transmission
Flea bite (primary), direct contact with infected tissues, respiratory droplets (pneumonic plague).

### Clinical Signs in Wildlife
- **Prairie dogs**: acute septicemia with high mortality; colony die-offs are sentinel events
- **Felids**: bubonic plague (submandibular lymphadenopathy), pneumonic plague, septicemia
- **Canids**: generally more resistant; may seroconvert without clinical disease

### Diagnostic Approach
- **Culture**: blood, lymph node aspirates, tissues — requires BSL-3 handling
- **Serology**: passive hemagglutination (PHA), ELISA — useful for surveillance in carnivores
- **Molecular**: PCR for *pla* and *caf1* genes
- **Rapid antigen test**: F1 antigen detection (field-deployable)

### Public Health Significance
Reportable disease. Wildlife workers in endemic areas should use flea repellent and avoid handling sick rodents and lagomorphs without PPE. Antibiotic prophylaxis may be indicated after high-risk exposure.

## Tularemia

### Etiology
*Francisella tularensis* — small gram-negative coccobacillus. Two clinically relevant subspecies: *F. t. tularensis* (Type A, more virulent, North America) and *F. t. holarctica* (Type B, Holarctic distribution).

### Wildlife Reservoirs
- Lagomorphs (rabbits, hares) — primary amplifying hosts in North America
- Rodents (voles, muskrats, beavers)
- Aquatic environments serve as environmental reservoirs

### Transmission
Arthropod bite (ticks, deer flies), direct contact with infected animals, ingestion of contaminated water, inhalation of aerosols.

### Clinical Signs in Wildlife
- **Lagomorphs**: acute septicemia, lethargy, hepatosplenomegaly with multifocal necrotic foci, sudden death
- **Rodents**: similar acute presentation with high mortality
- **Beaver**: die-offs associated with contaminated waterways

### Diagnostic Approach
- **Culture**: blood, liver, spleen — highly infectious, requires BSL-3
- **Molecular**: PCR targeting *fopA*, *tul4*, or ISFtu2 genes
- **Serology**: microagglutination, ELISA (useful for retrospective surveys)
- **Histopathology**: multifocal necrotizing hepatitis and splenitis with granulomatous inflammation

### Public Health Significance
Extremely low infectious dose (~10 organisms by aerosol). CDC Category A bioterrorism agent. Ulceroglandular form most common in natural infections. Wildlife workers should use gloves and respiratory protection when handling sick or dead lagomorphs.

## General Principles for the Wildlife Veterinarian

### Biosafety
- Always apply standard precautions (gloves, eye protection) when handling wildlife
- Upgrade to BSL-3 equivalent precautions for suspect high-consequence pathogens
- Establish SOPs for specimen packaging and transport of dangerous goods
- Maintain awareness of endemic zoonoses in your geographic area of practice

### Communication
- Report notifiable diseases promptly to relevant authorities
- Provide clear, science-based risk communication to the public
- Coordinate with public health agencies during outbreak investigations

### Sample Collection Priorities
For any suspected zoonotic disease investigation, collect:

| Sample Type | Container | Priority Pathogens |
|---|---|---|
| Brain (fresh and fixed) | Sealed container, 10% formalin | Rabies, encephalitic viruses |
| Liver, spleen, kidney | Sterile container on ice | Bacterial zoonoses, leptospirosis |
| Oropharyngeal swab | VTM | Influenza, coronaviruses |
| Cloacal/rectal swab | VTM or Cary-Blair | Influenza, enteric pathogens |
| Serum (clotted blood) | Red-top or SST tube | Serology for all agents |
| Whole blood (EDTA) | Purple-top tube | PCR, hematology |
| Lung tissue | Sterile, on ice | Hantavirus, plague, tularemia |
