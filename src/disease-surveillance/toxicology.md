# Toxicology in Wildlife

## Introduction

Environmental toxicology is a major concern in wildlife medicine. Anthropogenic contaminants enter ecosystems through diverse pathways and affect wildlife at individual and population levels. Wildlife veterinarians must recognize toxicological syndromes, collect appropriate diagnostic samples, and interpret results in the context of species-specific susceptibility and environmental exposure. Wildlife also serve as sentinels for environmental contamination that may affect human and livestock health.

## Lead Poisoning

### Sources

#### Ammunition
- **Spent lead shot**: waterfowl ingest lead pellets from the sediment of wetlands; upland game birds ingest shot from fields
- **Bullet fragments**: scavenging raptors (bald eagles, golden eagles, California condors, vultures) ingest lead fragments from gut piles and unrecovered carcasses; fragmentation of lead bullets produces hundreds of small particles distributed throughout tissue
- Lead ammunition remains the single largest source of lead exposure in raptors and condors

#### Fishing Tackle
- Lead sinkers and jig heads are ingested by waterfowl, loons, and swans
- Common loons (*Gavia immer*) are particularly affected; lead tackle ingestion is a leading cause of adult loon mortality in some regions
- Trumpeter and mute swans regularly ingest lead sinkers while feeding

#### Other Sources
- Lead-based paint (older structures used as nesting sites)
- Mining waste and contaminated soils
- Lead batteries in the environment

### Clinical Signs
- **Acute**: depression, weakness, green diarrhea (waterfowl), ataxia, head tilt, wing droop, inability to fly, seizures
- **Chronic**: weight loss, muscle wasting, pale mucous membranes, decreased reproductive success
- **Sublethal effects**: immunosuppression, impaired neurological function, reduced flight performance, behavioral changes

### Diagnosis

| Test | Sample | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Blood lead level | Whole blood (EDTA or heparin) | >20 ug/dL: subclinical exposure; >60 ug/dL: clinical poisoning; >100 ug/dL: severe/lethal |
| Liver lead | Fresh or frozen liver tissue | >6 ppm wet weight: diagnostic of lead poisoning |
| Kidney lead | Fresh or frozen kidney tissue | Supports diagnosis when liver unavailable |
| Radiography | Whole body | Metallic densities in GI tract (shot, sinkers, fragments) |
| Blood smear | EDTA blood | Basophilic stippling of erythrocytes (inconsistent) |
| ALAD activity | Whole blood | Delta-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase inhibition; sensitive biomarker |
| Free erythrocyte protoporphyrin (FEP) | Whole blood | Elevated with chronic exposure |

### Management
- Chelation therapy: CaEDTA (calcium disodium EDTA), succimer (DMSA), D-penicillamine
- Remove lead source (gastric lavage, endoscopic retrieval, proventriculotomy)
- Supportive care: fluids, nutritional support, seizure control
- Population-level: non-lead ammunition regulations, fishing tackle bans, lead abatement programs

## Rodenticide Exposure

### Anticoagulant Rodenticides (ARs)

#### First-Generation (FGAR)
- Warfarin, chlorophacinone, diphacinone
- Require multiple feedings to achieve lethal dose in target rodents
- Lower bioaccumulation potential but still cause secondary poisoning in wildlife

#### Second-Generation (SGAR)
- Brodifacoum, bromadiolone, difethialone, difenacoum
- Lethal after a single feeding; persist in liver tissue for months
- High bioaccumulation and secondary/tertiary poisoning risk
- Detected in the majority of tested raptors, foxes, bobcats, and other predators in multiple studies

### Clinical Signs
- Hemorrhage: subcutaneous, intracoelomic, pulmonary, from body orifices
- Weakness, pallor, dyspnea
- Sudden death without premonitory signs in acute cases
- Chronic sublethal exposure may impair immune function and predator avoidance

### Diagnosis
- **Coagulation testing**: prolonged PT, APTT, PIVKA (proteins induced by vitamin K absence)
- **Liver residue testing**: LC-MS/MS quantification of specific AR compounds in liver tissue
- **Post-mortem**: evidence of hemorrhage at necropsy; confirm with toxicology
- Note: AR residues in liver indicate exposure but not necessarily cause of death — integrate with clinical and pathological findings

### Treatment
- Vitamin K1 (phytonadione): 2.5-5 mg/kg IM or PO, then 1-2.5 mg/kg PO BID for 4-6 weeks for SGAR exposure
- Supportive care: fluid therapy, blood transfusion if available and species-appropriate, thermal support

## Pesticides

### Organophosphates and Carbamates
- Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitors
- Sources: agricultural application, intentional poisoning (illegal but occurs)
- Clinical signs: salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation (SLUD), muscle tremors, miosis, bradycardia, seizures, respiratory failure
- Diagnosis: brain or plasma AChE activity (compared to species-specific baseline); <50% of normal is diagnostic
- Treatment: atropine (0.2-0.5 mg/kg IV, IM; titrate to effect); pralidoxime for organophosphates (10-20 mg/kg IM); decontamination; supportive care

### Neonicotinoids
- Systemic insecticides widely used in agriculture: imidacloprid, clothianidin, thiamethoxam
- Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor agonists
- Primary concern: sublethal effects on pollinators (bees) and insectivorous birds
- Wildlife effects: reduced foraging efficiency, impaired navigation, reproductive effects, immunosuppression
- Diagnosis: residue analysis in tissues, crop contents, or environmental samples by LC-MS/MS
- Acute toxicity less common in vertebrates than in invertebrates; sublethal population-level effects are the principal concern

### Other Pesticides of Concern
- **Strychnine**: illegal predator control; CNS stimulant causing tetanic seizures
- **Sodium monofluoroacetate (1080)**: used legally in some countries for predator control; blocks the citric acid cycle; no specific antidote
- **Paraquat**: herbicide; causes pulmonary fibrosis; occasionally detected in wildlife

## Oil Contamination

### Sources
- Marine oil spills (tanker accidents, platform blowouts, pipeline leaks)
- Chronic low-level discharge from vessels and land-based sources
- Natural seeps

### Affected Species
- Seabirds (most visibly affected — feather contamination destroys waterproofing)
- Marine mammals (fur-bearing species such as sea otters most vulnerable; cetaceans and pinnipeds affected by ingestion and inhalation)
- Sea turtles
- Coastal and aquatic birds (shorebirds, wading birds, waterfowl)

### Pathophysiology
- **External oiling**: loss of feather/fur waterproofing and insulation leading to hypothermia
- **Ingestion**: gastrointestinal irritation, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, hemolytic anemia, immunosuppression
- **Inhalation**: respiratory irritation, pneumonia
- **Reproductive effects**: eggshell thinning, embryotoxicity, reduced hatching success

### Diagnosis and Response
- Visual assessment of oiling extent and severity (standardized scoring systems)
- Blood chemistry: heterophil:lymphocyte ratio, Heinz bodies, liver enzymes, packed cell volume
- Petroleum hydrocarbon residues in blood, tissues, or feathers
- Response protocol: stabilization (warmth, fluids, nutrition) before washing; detergent wash (Dawn dish soap is the standard); rinse; waterproofing recovery in clean water pools; rehabilitation to pre-release conditioning

## Harmful Algal Blooms (HABs)

### Major Toxins

| Toxin | Source | Affected Wildlife | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brevetoxin | *Karenia brevis* (marine dinoflagellate) | Sea turtles, manatees, seabirds, dolphins, fish | Sodium channel activation; neurotoxic |
| Domoic acid | *Pseudo-nitzschia* spp. (marine diatom) | Sea lions, sea otters, seabirds, fish | Excitatory amino acid; neurotoxic (seizures, hippocampal necrosis) |
| Microcystin | *Microcystis* spp. (freshwater cyanobacteria) | Waterfowl, shorebirds, mammals | Hepatotoxic; phosphatase inhibition |
| Anatoxin-a | *Anabaena*, *Oscillatoria* (cyanobacteria) | Dogs (common), birds, mammals | Nicotinic agonist; rapid neuromuscular paralysis |
| Saxitoxin | *Alexandrium* spp. (marine dinoflagellate) | Marine mammals, seabirds | Sodium channel blocker; paralytic shellfish poisoning |

### Diagnosis
- **Clinical presentation**: mass mortality event coinciding with algal bloom; neurological signs (domoic acid, brevetoxin), acute hepatic failure (microcystin)
- **Toxin detection**: ELISA, LC-MS/MS in serum, urine, feces, stomach contents, or tissues
- **Histopathology**: hippocampal necrosis (domoic acid), hepatocellular necrosis (microcystin)
- **Environmental sampling**: water sampling for algal cell counts and toxin concentrations

## Heavy Metals

### Mercury
- Sources: coal combustion, gold mining, natural deposits; methylated by aquatic microorganisms to methylmercury (the bioavailable form)
- Bioaccumulation through aquatic food webs; highest concentrations in top predators (loons, eagles, mink, river otters)
- Effects: neurological impairment, reproductive failure, immunosuppression, behavioral changes
- Diagnosis: total mercury in blood, feathers (non-lethal), or liver/kidney (post-mortem); hair/fur in mammals; feathers are useful for non-invasive monitoring (reflect exposure during feather growth)

### Cadmium
- Sources: mining, smelting, phosphate fertilizers, batteries
- Accumulates in kidney and liver
- Effects: renal tubular damage, osteoporosis, reproductive impairment
- Diagnosis: kidney cadmium concentration; >10 ppm wet weight associated with renal damage in many species

### Selenium
- Both essential nutrient and toxicant (narrow therapeutic window)
- Sources: agricultural irrigation drainage (Kesterson Reservoir disaster), mining, coal combustion
- Bioaccumulates in aquatic food chains
- Effects: teratogenesis (deformed embryos in birds), reproductive failure, hepatotoxicity
- Diagnosis: liver selenium; >10 ppm wet weight: potentially toxic; egg selenium >3 ppm: associated with embryonic deformity in waterbirds

## Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances (PFAS)

### Overview
PFAS are a group of thousands of synthetic fluorinated compounds used in firefighting foams (AFFF), non-stick coatings, water-resistant textiles, food packaging, and industrial applications. They are referred to as "forever chemicals" due to environmental persistence.

### Wildlife Exposure
- Ubiquitous in the environment; detected in wildlife globally, including remote Arctic regions
- Bioaccumulate and biomagnify through food webs
- Aquatic and piscivorous species are most heavily exposed
- PFOS and PFOA are the most studied compounds, but many other PFAS are emerging concerns

### Effects
- Hepatotoxicity and altered lipid metabolism
- Immunosuppression (reduced antibody responses, increased susceptibility to disease)
- Endocrine disruption (thyroid, reproductive hormones)
- Reproductive impairment
- Developmental toxicity

### Diagnosis
- Serum or plasma PFAS concentrations by LC-MS/MS
- Liver concentrations post-mortem
- Egg concentrations for avian reproductive risk assessment
- Interpretation complicated by the large number of PFAS compounds and evolving toxicity thresholds

### Current Research Priorities
- Establishing effects thresholds for wildlife species
- Characterizing exposure in diverse taxa and ecosystems
- Understanding the contribution of precursor PFAS compounds
- Evaluating population-level impacts

## Sample Collection for Toxicological Screening

### Recommended Samples by Suspected Toxicant

| Suspected Agent | Priority Samples | Container | Storage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lead | Whole blood (EDTA), liver, kidney | Lead-free tubes and containers | Blood: refrigerate; tissue: freeze |
| Anticoagulant rodenticides | Liver (>10 g preferred) | Chemically clean container | Freeze at -20C |
| Organophosphates/carbamates | Brain (half), plasma | Sealed containers | Freeze immediately at -80C if possible |
| Mercury | Blood, liver, kidney, feathers/hair | Acid-washed containers (tissue); paper envelope (feathers) | Freeze tissue; feathers at ambient |
| PFAS | Serum/plasma, liver | Polypropylene containers (not glass) | Freeze at -20C |
| Petroleum hydrocarbons | Blood, liver, GI contents, feather/skin swabs | Glass containers with PTFE-lined caps | Freeze; protect from UV |
| Algal toxins | Serum, stomach contents, liver, feces | Sealed containers | Freeze; protect from light |
| Pesticides (general) | GI contents, liver, fat, brain | Glass or chemically clean containers | Freeze at -20C or colder |

### General Principles
- Collect samples before any treatment (chelation, activated charcoal, decontamination)
- Use chemically clean (pesticide-grade) containers; avoid cross-contamination
- Collect adequate sample volume (consult with the analytical laboratory for minimum requirements)
- Record all potential sources of exposure observed at the site
- Document the animal's location, species, clinical signs, and environmental context
- Submit a complete history with the samples — laboratory interpretation depends on clinical context
- Maintain a frozen tissue archive for retrospective analysis when new contaminants or methods emerge

### Laboratory Selection
- Use laboratories accredited for environmental toxicology testing (ISO 17025 or equivalent)
- Confirm the laboratory can analyze the compounds of interest at relevant detection limits
- Inquire about species-specific interpretation guidance when available
- Turnaround times for toxicology testing range from days to weeks depending on the analyte and methodology
