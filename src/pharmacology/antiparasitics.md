# Antiparasitic Agents in Wildlife Medicine

## Introduction

Parasitism is ubiquitous in wild animals, and the decision to treat is not always straightforward. In free-ranging wildlife, parasites are a natural component of host ecology; treatment is typically indicated only when parasite burdens contribute to morbidity, compromise rehabilitation outcomes, or threaten population health. In captive wildlife (zoos, aquaria, rehabilitation), regular parasite surveillance and strategic deworming are standard practice. This chapter covers the major antiparasitic drug classes, dosing by taxon, safety considerations, and clinical guidance for their use in wildlife species.

## General Principles

- **Identify before treating**: Fecal examination (direct wet mount, flotation, sedimentation) and species identification should guide drug selection when feasible
- **Treat the environment**: Reinfection is inevitable if environmental contamination is not addressed; cleaning, substrate changes, and intermediate host control are essential adjuncts
- **Resistance monitoring**: Fecal egg count reduction tests (FECRT) are the standard for detecting anthelmintic resistance; increasingly relevant in captive wildlife collections
- **Allometric considerations**: Smaller species have higher metabolic rates and may require higher mg/kg doses; see the Allometric Scaling chapter for principles
- **Ectotherm metabolism**: Drug metabolism in reptiles and amphibians is temperature-dependent; ensure patients are within their preferred optimal temperature zone (POTZ) during treatment

## Macrocyclic Lactones

### Ivermectin

A broad-spectrum avermectin active against nematodes, ectoparasites (mites, lice, ticks, myiasis larvae), and some microfilariae. Enhances GABA-mediated chloride channel opening, causing neuromuscular paralysis in invertebrates.

#### Dosing by Taxon

| Taxon | Dose | Route | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mammals (general) | 0.2–0.4 mg/kg | PO, SC | Single dose; repeat in 14 days | Standard dose for most nematodes and ectoparasites |
| Primates | 0.2 mg/kg | PO, SC | Repeat in 14 days | Generally well-tolerated |
| Small mammals (rodents, insectivores) | 0.2–0.5 mg/kg | PO, SC | Repeat in 10–14 days | Higher end for mange mites |
| Birds | 0.2 mg/kg | PO, IM | Repeat in 14 days | Effective against *Capillaria*, *Syngamus*, mites |
| Lizards and snakes | 0.2 mg/kg | PO, SC, IM | Repeat in 14 days | Generally safe in squamates |
| **Chelonians** | **CONTRAINDICATED** | — | — | **See safety warning below** |
| Crocodilians | 0.2 mg/kg | PO, SC | Repeat in 14 days | Use with caution; limited safety data |
| Amphibians | Not recommended | — | — | Toxicity risk; prefer fenbendazole |

#### Safety Warnings

> **Ivermectin toxicity in chelonians**: Ivermectin is potentially lethal in chelonians (turtles and tortoises). Clinical signs include flaccid paralysis, blindness, coma, and death. The mechanism appears to involve ivermectin crossing the blood-brain barrier more readily in chelonians. **Do not use ivermectin in any chelonian species.** Use fenbendazole or other alternatives.

> **Macrocyclic lactone sensitivity**: Certain species and breeds are known to have increased sensitivity to macrocyclic lactones due to P-glycoprotein (ABCB1/MDR1) deficiency or blood-brain barrier differences. Exercise caution in:
> - Chelonians (all species — contraindicated)
> - Skinks (reports of toxicity in some species)
> - Indigo snakes (*Drymarchon* spp. — anecdotal concerns)
> - Young or debilitated animals of any species (reduced P-glycoprotein function)

### Moxidectin

A milbemycin with a broader safety margin than ivermectin in some species, longer duration of action, and efficacy against ivermectin-resistant parasites.

| Taxon | Dose | Route | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mammals | 0.2–0.5 mg/kg | PO, SC, topical | Single; repeat in 30 days | Topical (spot-on) formulations available |
| Birds | 0.2 mg/kg | PO, topical | Single; repeat in 14–30 days | Topical application to skin between scapulae |
| Reptiles (non-chelonians) | 0.2 mg/kg | PO | Single; repeat in 14 days | Limited data; use with caution |
| Chelonians | **Avoid** | — | — | Insufficient safety data; same class as ivermectin |

### Selamectin

- Topical (spot-on) avermectin; systemic absorption provides ectoparasite and some endoparasite coverage
- Mammals: 6–12 mg/kg topical (between scapulae)
- Birds: 6–12 mg/kg topical (on skin of dorsal neck)
- Reptiles: 6–10 mg/kg topical; limited data; avoid in chelonians
- Particularly useful for mite treatment in snakes (*Ophionyssus natricis*): 6 mg/kg topical, repeat in 30 days

## Benzimidazoles

### Fenbendazole

Broad-spectrum benzimidazole active against nematodes (adults and larvae), some cestodes, and *Giardia*. Inhibits tubulin polymerization, disrupting microtubule function in parasites.

| Taxon | Dose | Route | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mammals (general) | 5–10 mg/kg | PO | SID for 3–5 days | Effective against most GI nematodes and lungworms |
| Primates | 50 mg/kg | PO | SID for 3 days | Higher doses needed for some primates |
| Birds | 20–50 mg/kg | PO | SID for 3–5 days | Effective against *Capillaria*, *Ascaridia*, *Syngamus* |
| Reptiles | 50–100 mg/kg | PO | Single; repeat in 14 days | Higher doses due to ectotherm metabolism |
| Amphibians | 50–100 mg/kg | PO | Single; repeat in 14 days | Preferred anthelmintic for amphibians |
| Fish | 2–25 mg/kg in food | PO | SID for 3 days | Mix with food; palatability issues |

#### Safety Notes

> **Bone marrow toxicity**: Prolonged or high-dose fenbendazole administration has been associated with fatal bone marrow suppression (pancytopenia) in some species, particularly birds (storks, cranes, vultures, flamingos, doves) and some reptiles. Risk increases with doses >50 mg/kg for >5 consecutive days. **Monitor CBC during extended treatment courses.** Avoid concurrent use with other myelosuppressive drugs.

### Albendazole

- Similar spectrum to fenbendazole but with greater teratogenic potential
- 25–50 mg/kg PO (mammals); generally avoided in birds and reptiles due to toxicity risk
- **Contraindicated in pregnant animals** (teratogenic across taxa)
- Use fenbendazole preferentially unless albendazole-specific efficacy is required (e.g., certain larval cestodes, *Echinococcus*)

### Oxfendazole

- Metabolized to fenbendazole sulfoxide (the active metabolite of fenbendazole)
- 10 mg/kg PO in mammals; less commonly used in wildlife than fenbendazole
- Available in injectable and pour-on formulations convenient for large wildlife

## Praziquantel

Isoquinoline derivative; the drug of choice for cestodes (tapeworms) and trematodes (flukes). Causes calcium influx, tegumental damage, and spastic paralysis of susceptible helminths.

| Taxon | Dose | Route | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mammals | 5–10 mg/kg | PO, SC, IM | Single; repeat in 14–21 days for trematodes | Effective against all cestode genera |
| Birds | 10–20 mg/kg | PO, IM | Single; repeat in 10–14 days | Higher doses for trematodes |
| Reptiles | 5–8 mg/kg | PO, IM, ICe | Repeat in 14 days | Effective for spirorchid trematodes in sea turtles |
| Amphibians | 8–24 mg/kg | PO, bath (10 mg/L for 3 h) | Repeat in 14 days | Bath administration useful for small species |
| Fish (teleosts) | 2–10 mg/kg PO or 2 mg/L bath for 24 h | PO, bath | Repeat in 7 days | Monogeneans, cestodes |
| Elasmobranchs | 5–10 mg/kg | PO (in food) | Repeat in 14 days | Monogeneans, cestodes |

**Notes**: Injectable formulation causes pain at IM injection sites; dilute or use PO route when possible. Oral formulation has a bitter taste; hide in food or administer directly.

## Pyrantel Pamoate

Depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agent effective against ascarids and hookworms. Poor efficacy against whipworms and ineffective against larval stages.

| Taxon | Dose | Route | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mammals | 5–10 mg/kg | PO | Single; repeat in 14 days | Safe; low systemic absorption |
| Birds | 5–10 mg/kg | PO | Single; repeat in 14 days | Limited efficacy against *Capillaria* |
| Reptiles | 5–10 mg/kg | PO | Single; repeat in 14 days | Safe in chelonians (unlike ivermectin) |

**Advantage**: Very wide safety margin due to minimal systemic absorption; useful in debilitated animals and species where ivermectin is contraindicated.

## Metronidazole

Nitroimidazole antiprotozoal and anaerobic antibiotic. Active against *Giardia*, *Entamoeba*, *Trichomonas*, *Hexamita*, *Balantidium*, and anaerobic bacteria.

| Taxon | Dose | Route | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mammals | 15–25 mg/kg | PO | BID for 5–7 days | Standard for giardiasis and amebiasis |
| Primates | 25–50 mg/kg | PO | BID for 5–7 days | Higher doses for *Entamoeba histolytica*; hepatotoxicity risk |
| Birds | 25–50 mg/kg | PO | BID for 5–7 days | Effective against *Trichomonas gallinae* (avian trichomoniasis) |
| Reptiles | 25–50 mg/kg | PO | Every 48 h for 3–5 treatments | Adjust interval for ectotherm metabolism |
| Amphibians | 50 mg/kg | PO, bath | Every 48 h for 3 treatments | Limited data; use cautiously |

#### Safety Notes

- **Neurotoxicity**: Dose-dependent CNS toxicity (vestibular signs, ataxia, seizures) reported in multiple species; risk increases with prolonged courses (>14 days) or high doses
- **Hepatotoxicity**: Reported in primates and carnivores at high doses; monitor with extended treatment
- **Bitter taste**: Causes hypersalivation and food refusal; compound in flavored vehicle or administer directly

## Toltrazuril and Ponazuril

Triazinone antiprotozoals; highly effective against coccidia (*Eimeria*, *Isospora*, *Cystoisospora*) and some other Apicomplexa. Act on all intracellular stages (schizonts, gamonts) by inhibiting mitochondrial respiration.

### Toltrazuril

| Taxon | Dose | Route | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mammals | 10–25 mg/kg | PO | SID for 2–3 days or single dose | Effective for coccidiosis treatment and metaphylaxis |
| Birds | 7–25 mg/kg | PO or in water (25 mg/L) | SID for 2 days; repeat in 5–7 days | Standard treatment for avian coccidiosis |
| Reptiles | 15–25 mg/kg | PO | Single; repeat in 7–14 days | Limited PK data in reptiles |

### Ponazuril

The active metabolite of toltrazuril; available as an oral paste (equine formulation) convenient for dosing wildlife.

| Taxon | Dose | Route | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mammals | 20–30 mg/kg | PO | SID for 3 days or single 50 mg/kg dose | Effective against *Toxoplasma*, *Sarcocystis*, *Neospora* |
| Birds | 20–30 mg/kg | PO | SID for 3 days | Increasingly used for refractory coccidiosis |
| Reptiles | 20–30 mg/kg | PO | Every 48 h for 3 treatments | Growing evidence base |
| Marine mammals | 10–20 mg/kg | PO | SID for 28 days | Used for *Sarcocystis neurona* (protozoal encephalitis) in sea otters |

## Levamisole

Cholinergic agonist; effective against nematodes (especially lungworms). Also has immunostimulatory properties.

| Taxon | Dose | Route | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mammals | 5–10 mg/kg | PO, SC | Single; repeat in 14 days | Narrow therapeutic index in some species |
| Birds | 20–40 mg/kg | PO | Single; repeat in 14 days | Effective for *Syngamus trachea* (gapeworm) |
| Reptiles | 5–10 mg/kg | PO, SC, ICe | Repeat in 14 days | Use cautiously; toxicity reported at higher doses |
| Fish | 2 mg/L bath for 24 h | Bath | Repeat in 7 days | Nematode infestations |

**Caution**: Narrow therapeutic index; toxicity manifests as cholinergic crisis (salivation, tremors, respiratory distress). Treat overdose with atropine (0.02–0.04 mg/kg IV).

## Fipronil

Phenylpyrazole ectoparasiticide; blocks GABA-gated chloride channels in arthropods. Applied topically for fleas, ticks, and mites.

- **Mammals**: Topical spray or spot-on at label doses (typically 7.5 mg/kg)
- **Birds**: **Use with extreme caution**; toxicity and death reported in small birds; avoid in birds <1 kg; 0.25 mL of 0.29% spray per 500 g applied to skin under feathers, avoiding inhalation
- **Reptiles**: 7.5 mg/kg topical spray; effective for mites and ticks; allow to dry before returning to enclosure
- **Amphibians**: **CONTRAINDICATED** (absorbed through permeable skin; toxicity expected)

## Antiparasitic Drug Selection Guide by Parasite Group

| Parasite Group | First-Line Drug | Alternatives | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ascarids (roundworms) | Fenbendazole | Pyrantel, ivermectin (non-chelonian) | Repeat treatment to catch larval migration |
| Strongyles | Fenbendazole, ivermectin | Moxidectin, oxfendazole | Resistance monitoring recommended in captive populations |
| Lungworms | Fenbendazole | Ivermectin, levamisole | Prolonged courses may be needed (5–10 days FBZ) |
| Cestodes (tapeworms) | Praziquantel | Fenbendazole (high dose) | Praziquantel is definitive treatment; FBZ for larval stages |
| Trematodes (flukes) | Praziquantel | Albendazole (liver flukes) | Higher praziquantel doses and repeat treatments for trematodes |
| Coccidia | Toltrazuril / ponazuril | Sulfadimethoxine, amprolium | Toltrazuril acts on all intracellular stages |
| *Giardia* / flagellates | Metronidazole | Fenbendazole (3–5 days) | Metronidazole preferred for clinical giardiasis |
| *Trichomonas* | Metronidazole | Ronidazole (birds) | Ronidazole 10 mg/kg PO BID for 7 days for avian trichomoniasis |
| Ectoparasites (mites, lice) | Ivermectin (non-chelonian) | Selamectin, fipronil, moxidectin | Treat environment concurrently |
| Myiasis (fly strike) | Manual removal + ivermectin | Topical wound management | Debride maggots; treat wounds; systemic ivermectin to prevent reinfestation |

## Monitoring and Follow-Up

- Perform fecal examination 10–14 days post-treatment to assess efficacy
- Fecal egg count reduction of >95% indicates adequate drug efficacy; <90% suggests resistance
- Repeat treatment courses based on parasite life cycle (most nematodes: 14-day retreatment interval to catch maturing larvae)
- In captive collections: implement strategic deworming programs based on regular fecal surveillance rather than blanket prophylactic dosing
- Environmental decontamination: remove feces daily; steam-clean enclosures; freeze substrate for parasite-susceptible species; control intermediate hosts (snails for trematodes, arthropods for cestodes)
