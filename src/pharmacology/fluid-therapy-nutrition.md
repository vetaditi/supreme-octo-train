# Fluid Therapy and Nutritional Support

## Introduction

Dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, and malnutrition are among the most common and immediately life-threatening problems encountered in wildlife patients. Free-ranging animals presented for rehabilitation are frequently debilitated from days to weeks of illness, injury, or environmental stress before they are captured. Appropriate fluid resuscitation and nutritional support are foundational to survival in critical wildlife patients.

## Assessment of Hydration Status

### Clinical Estimation of Dehydration

| Estimated Dehydration (%) | Clinical Signs |
|---|---|
| < 5% | Not clinically detectable; subclinical dehydration is common in wildlife |
| 5–6% | Subtle loss of skin turgor; slightly tacky mucous membranes |
| 7–8% | Decreased skin turgor (skin tent); dry mucous membranes; mild enophthalmos |
| 9–10% | Marked skin tenting; sunken eyes; tacky to dry mucous membranes; prolonged CRT (>2 seconds) |
| 11–12% | Severe enophthalmos; collapsed veins; altered mentation; weak pulses |
| >12% | Hypovolemic shock; moribund; death imminent without aggressive resuscitation |

### Species-Specific Considerations

- **Birds**: Skin turgor is difficult to assess due to thin skin and feathers; evaluate mucous membranes (oral, conjunctival), eye position (enophthalmos), wrinkled skin over the feet (anurans), urate consistency (dry, chalky urates indicate dehydration)
- **Reptiles**: Skin turgor variable; spectacled snakes and geckos have limited useful assessment sites. Sunken eyes, wrinkled skin, dry mucous membranes, and decreased urate output are useful indicators. Reptiles tolerate higher levels of dehydration than mammals before clinical signs become apparent.
- **Amphibians**: Body weight changes are the most reliable indicator (weigh daily); edema or hydrocoelom may mask dehydration
- **Marine mammals**: Standard clinical assessment; serum chemistry (BUN:creatinine ratio, hematocrit, total protein) supplements clinical evaluation

### Laboratory Assessment

| Parameter | Dehydration Indicators | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| PCV / Hematocrit | Elevated (hemoconcentration) | Interpret with caution in anemic patients |
| Total protein | Elevated (hemoconcentration) | May be confounded by hypoproteinemia |
| BUN / Uric acid | Elevated | Uric acid in birds and reptiles; BUN in mammals |
| Urine specific gravity | Elevated (>1.030 mammals) | Species-dependent normal ranges |
| Lactate | Elevated (>2 mmol/L) | Indicator of tissue hypoperfusion |
| Electrolytes | Variable; often hypernatremia or hyponatremia depending on losses | Essential for guiding fluid selection |

## Fluid Therapy Routes

### Intravenous (IV)

- **Indications**: Moderate to severe dehydration (>7–8%), hypovolemic shock, rapid drug delivery
- **Access sites by taxon**:
  - Mammals: cephalic, saphenous, jugular, auricular (rabbits, ungulates) veins
  - Birds: basilic (cutaneous ulnar), medial metatarsal, right jugular veins
  - Reptiles: jugular (chelonians, crocodilians), cephalic (lizards), ventral coccygeal (snakes, lizards), dorsal venous sinus (chelonian carapace—advanced)
- **Advantages**: Rapid restoration of intravascular volume; precise rate control with infusion pumps
- **Challenges in wildlife**: Venous access difficult in small or fractious patients; catheter maintenance during recovery; risk of dislodgement

### Intraosseous (IO)

- **Indications**: Vascular collapse, inability to access peripheral veins, emergent resuscitation
- **Access sites**:
  - Birds: distal ulna (preferred), proximal tibiotarsus; **avoid pneumatized bones** (humerus and femur in most species are pneumatized and communicate with the respiratory system)
  - Mammals: proximal tibia, trochanteric fossa of femur, proximal humerus
  - Reptiles: proximal tibia (lizards), bridge between carapace and plastron (chelonians, controversial)
- **Technique**: Use spinal needles or dedicated IO needles; insert with rotary motion until decreased resistance and ability to flush without subcutaneous swelling
- **Flow rates**: Equivalent to IV; can be used for all fluids, blood products, and most medications
- **Complications**: Osteomyelitis (rare with aseptic technique), fracture (fragile bones), extravasation

### Subcutaneous (SC)

- **Indications**: Mild to moderate dehydration (5–8%); maintenance fluid support; outpatient management
- **Sites**: Lateral body wall, interscapular region (mammals), inguinal or axillary web (birds), lateral body wall or dorsal subcutaneous space (reptiles)
- **Absorption**: Slower than IV/IO; dependent on peripheral perfusion. **Do not rely on SC fluids in shocked, severely dehydrated, or hypothermic patients** (poor peripheral perfusion impairs absorption)
- **Maximum volumes per site**: 10–20 mL/kg per injection site in mammals and birds; adjust for patient size
- **Frequency**: Can be administered every 6–24 hours as needed
- **Fluid selection**: Isotonic crystalloids only (LRS, 0.9% NaCl, Normosol-R); **never administer hypertonic, dextrose-containing (>2.5%), or colloidal fluids SC**

### Intracoelomic (ICe)

- **Indications**: Reptiles and amphibians when other routes are impractical; mild to moderate dehydration
- **Technique**: Insert needle in the ventral midline (reptiles) or lateral coelomic region (amphibians); avoid organs by directing the needle away from midline viscera
- **Advantages**: Rapid absorption from the coelomic cavity in ectotherms; practical for field use
- **Risks**: Organ laceration; peritonitis (if sterile technique is not maintained)
- **Volume**: Up to 20–30 mL/kg per administration in reptiles; 10–20 mL/kg in amphibians

### Oral / Enteral

- **Indications**: Mild dehydration in stable, non-vomiting patients; maintenance therapy following parenteral rehydration
- **Methods**: Syringe feeding, gavage tube (crop tube in birds, stomach tube in mammals and reptiles)
- **Advantages**: Most physiologic route; supports GI mucosal integrity
- **Contraindications**: Ileus, GI obstruction, severe regurgitation, unconscious patients, suspected GI perforation

## Fluid Types

### Crystalloids

| Fluid | Na+ (mEq/L) | K+ (mEq/L) | Ca2+ (mEq/L) | Buffer | Osmolarity (mOsm/L) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lactated Ringer's (LRS) | 130 | 4 | 2.7 | Lactate (28) | 273 | Most commonly used; balanced, isotonic |
| 0.9% NaCl (Normal Saline) | 154 | 0 | 0 | None | 308 | Slightly hypertonic; hyperchloremic acidosis with large volumes |
| Normosol-R / Plasma-Lyte A | 140 | 5 | 0 | Acetate/gluconate | 294 | Balanced; no calcium (compatible with blood products) |
| Half-strength (0.45%) NaCl + 2.5% dextrose | 77 | 0 | 0 | None | 280 | Maintenance fluid; hypotonic after dextrose metabolism |
| 5% Dextrose (D5W) | 0 | 0 | 0 | None | 252 | Free water replacement; avoid SC; avoid in head trauma |
| Amphibian Ringer's | 111 | 2 | 1.8 | — | ~230 | Dilute; appropriate for amphibian osmolarity |

**LRS** is the default resuscitation and replacement fluid for most wildlife species. For amphibians, use dilute (half-strength) crystalloids or amphibian-specific Ringer's solution to avoid hyperosmolar stress.

### Colloids

| Fluid | Type | Dose Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hetastarch (6% HES) | Synthetic | 5–20 mL/kg IV bolus; up to 20 mL/kg/day | Oncotic support; controversial due to renal injury risk in humans; use judiciously |
| VetStarch | Synthetic | 5–20 mL/kg IV | Lower molecular weight HES; potentially safer |
| Fresh frozen plasma | Natural | 10–20 mL/kg IV | Provides clotting factors, albumin, globulins; species-matched preferred |
| Whole blood | Natural | 10–20 mL/kg IV | Hemorrhagic shock; crossmatch when possible |

**Indications for colloids**: Hypoproteinemia (total protein <3.5 g/dL in mammals), severe hemorrhage, inadequate response to crystalloid boluses, maintenance of oncotic pressure during prolonged resuscitation.

## Fluid Rates

### Replacement Phase

- **Deficit volume (mL)** = Body weight (kg) x Estimated dehydration (%) x 1000
- Replace 50% of the deficit in the first 6–12 hours; remainder over the next 12–24 hours
- **Shock bolus (mammals)**: 60–90 mL/kg IV over 15–30 minutes (dogs); 40–60 mL/kg (cats, small mammals); administer in 10–20 mL/kg increments, reassessing between boluses
- **Shock bolus (birds)**: 10–25 mL/kg IV or IO over 5–10 minutes; reassess and repeat as needed
- **Reptiles**: 10–25 mL/kg IV or IO; boluses should be warmed to the patient's POTZ

### Maintenance Rates

| Taxon | Maintenance Rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mammals (most) | 40–60 mL/kg/day | Adjust for species size (allometric scaling) |
| Small mammals (<1 kg) | 80–100 mL/kg/day | Higher metabolic rate |
| Birds | 50–100 mL/kg/day | Higher metabolic rate; insensible losses through respiration |
| Reptiles | 10–25 mL/kg/day | Lower metabolic rate; adjust for temperature |
| Amphibians | Via immersion or 10–20 mL/kg/day ICe | Maintain appropriate water osmolality |

### Ongoing Losses

Add estimated ongoing losses (vomiting, diarrhea, wound drainage, third-space losses) to the maintenance rate. Reassess hydration status every 4–12 hours during active resuscitation.

## Electrolyte Correction

### Hyponatremia

- Common in freshwater drowning/aspiration, hypotonic fluid overload, adrenal insufficiency
- Mild (130–135 mEq/L): restrict free water; administer LRS or 0.9% NaCl
- Severe (<120 mEq/L): 0.9% NaCl; correct at a rate no faster than 0.5–1.0 mEq/L/hour (8–12 mEq/L/day maximum) to prevent osmotic demyelination syndrome
- Monitor serum sodium every 4–6 hours during correction

### Hypernatremia

- Common in dehydrated wildlife (insensible losses without water intake), seabird ingestion of seawater, salt toxicity
- Correct slowly: reduce serum sodium by no more than 0.5–1.0 mEq/L/hour (10–12 mEq/L/day maximum) to avoid cerebral edema
- Use hypotonic fluids (0.45% NaCl, D5W) or gradual dilution of enteral intake

### Hypokalemia

- Common in anorexic patients, those with GI losses, or after fluid therapy with potassium-poor solutions
- Supplement IV fluids with KCl:

| Serum K+ (mEq/L) | KCl to Add (mEq/L of IV fluid) | Maximum IV Rate (mEq/kg/hr) |
|---|---|---|
| 3.5–5.0 (normal) | 0–10 | — |
| 3.0–3.5 | 20 | 0.25 |
| 2.5–3.0 | 30 | 0.25 |
| 2.0–2.5 | 40 | 0.25 |
| < 2.0 | 60 | 0.5 (with ECG monitoring) |

- **Never exceed 0.5 mEq/kg/hour** without continuous ECG monitoring; fatal cardiac arrhythmias can result

### Hypocalcemia

- Common in egg-laying reptiles and birds (hypocalcemic tetany), nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism, eclampsia
- Acute: calcium gluconate 10% solution, 50–100 mg/kg IV slowly over 10–15 minutes with cardiac monitoring; stop if bradycardia develops
- Chronic: dietary calcium supplementation, vitamin D3, UVB exposure (reptiles and birds)

## Acid-Base Disturbances

| Condition | Common Causes in Wildlife | Treatment Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Metabolic acidosis | Shock, renal failure, toxin exposure, severe diarrhea, lactic acidosis | Address underlying cause; fluid resuscitation with balanced crystalloids; sodium bicarbonate only for pH <7.1 (1–2 mEq/kg IV slowly) |
| Metabolic alkalosis | Prolonged vomiting, hypokalemia | 0.9% NaCl; potassium supplementation |
| Respiratory acidosis | Hypoventilation (anesthesia, head trauma, pneumonia) | Improve ventilation; assisted ventilation if needed |
| Respiratory alkalosis | Hyperventilation (pain, anxiety, heat stress) | Address underlying cause; minimize stress |

## Nutritional Support

### Triage Nutritional Assessment

- **Body condition scoring**: Use species-appropriate scales (1–5 or 1–9); palpate pectoral muscle mass (birds), epaxial musculature (mammals), and fat stores
- **Serum albumin**: <2.0 g/dL in mammals indicates significant protein depletion
- **Duration of anorexia**: Animals anorexic for >3–5 days require nutritional intervention (earlier in small species with high metabolic rates)
- **Prealbumin / retinol-binding protein**: More sensitive early indicators of protein-calorie malnutrition (limited availability in wildlife species)

### Resting Energy Requirement (RER) and Metabolic Energy Requirement (MER)

The resting energy requirement is estimated using the allometric formula:

**RER (kcal/day) = 70 x (body weight in kg)^0.75**

For most hospitalized wildlife patients, start at RER and increase gradually. Illness factors (1.2–1.5 x RER) are applied as the patient stabilizes.

| Patient Category | Energy Target |
|---|---|
| Initial stabilization (day 1–2) | 50–75% of RER |
| Stabilized, recovering | 100% RER |
| Recovering, increased demand | 1.2–1.5 x RER (MER) |
| Growth, lactation, severe trauma | 1.5–2.0 x RER |

### Enteral Nutrition

The preferred route whenever the GI tract is functional. Enteral nutrition maintains intestinal mucosal integrity, reduces bacterial translocation, and supports immune function.

#### Delivery Methods

| Method | Description | Indications |
|---|---|---|
| Assisted feeding / syringe feeding | Small volumes delivered by syringe to the oral cavity | Alert patients with intact swallow reflex; supplemental feeding |
| Gavage / crop tube (birds) | Tube passed into the crop for bolus delivery | Standard method for debilitated birds; 2–5% BW per feeding |
| Orogastric / nasogastric tube | Tube passed into the stomach | Mammals; allows continuous or bolus feeding |
| Esophagostomy tube | Surgically placed tube in the cervical esophagus | Prolonged feeding in mammals; avoids repeated orogastric intubation |
| Pharyngostomy tube | Less preferred than esophagostomy | Risk of laryngeal obstruction |

#### Enteral Diet Selection

| Taxon | Suitable Diets | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Carnivorous mammals | Critical care liquid diets (e.g., Emeraid Carnivore, Hills a/d) | High protein and fat; can be diluted for tube feeding |
| Herbivorous mammals | Critical care herbivore formulas (Emeraid Herbivore, Oxbow Critical Care) | Fiber content supports GI function |
| Birds (carnivorous) | Whole prey slurry; Emeraid Carnivore; Hills a/d | Transition to whole prey as soon as possible |
| Birds (granivorous/frugivorous) | Emeraid Omnivore; hand-feeding formula diluted | Ensure crop empties between feedings (palpate crop) |
| Reptiles | Species-appropriate whole prey slurry; Emeraid Carnivore or Herbivore | Warm to POTZ before feeding; slow GI transit in ectotherms |
| Fish | Finely ground species-appropriate diet in gelatin | Gavage via stomach tube for debilitated individuals |

### Parenteral Nutrition

Reserved for patients in which enteral nutrition is not possible or insufficient (GI obstruction, severe ileus, pancreatitis, major GI surgery).

#### Peripheral Parenteral Nutrition (PPN)

- Osmolarity <900 mOsm/L to avoid phlebitis; administered via peripheral veins
- Provides partial caloric support (30–70% of RER)
- Typical formulation: amino acids (3%) + dextrose (5%) + lipid emulsion (20%); mixed under sterile conditions
- Duration: short-term (3–7 days) bridge until enteral feeding can be established

#### Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN)

- Osmolarity >900 mOsm/L; requires central venous access (jugular catheter)
- Provides complete caloric and protein support
- Requires careful formulation, sterile compounding, and close metabolic monitoring
- Complications: hyperglycemia, sepsis (line infection), hepatic lipidosis, electrolyte imbalances

### Refeeding Syndrome

A potentially fatal metabolic complication that occurs when nutrition is reintroduced to severely malnourished or chronically anorexic animals.

#### Pathophysiology

During prolonged starvation, intracellular electrolytes (phosphorus, potassium, magnesium) become depleted as they are mobilized for energy. When carbohydrate intake resumes, insulin secretion drives glucose and electrolytes intracellularly, causing precipitous drops in serum phosphorus (hypophosphatemia), potassium (hypokalemia), and magnesium (hypomagnesemia). This can result in cardiac arrhythmias, respiratory failure, hemolytic anemia, seizures, and death.

#### Risk Factors

- Starvation for >5 days (shorter in small species with high metabolic rates)
- Body condition score 1/5 or 1/9
- Serum phosphorus <3.0 mg/dL prior to refeeding
- Species commonly affected: raptors, seabirds, marine mammals, emaciated large mammals

#### Prevention Protocol

1. **Baseline bloodwork**: Measure phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, glucose, and electrolytes before initiating nutrition
2. **Start low**: Begin at 25–50% of calculated RER on day 1
3. **Increase gradually**: Increase by 25% of RER every 24 hours if electrolytes remain stable
4. **Supplement electrolytes prophylactically**:
   - Phosphorus: potassium phosphate 0.01–0.06 mmol/kg/hr IV
   - Potassium: KCl supplementation in IV fluids (see table above)
   - Magnesium: magnesium sulfate 0.5–1.0 mEq/kg/day IV CRI
   - Thiamine (vitamin B1): 1–2 mg/kg IM SID (thiamine is rapidly depleted during refeeding)
5. **Monitor closely**: Serum phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium every 6–12 hours for the first 72 hours; glucose every 4–6 hours

| Monitoring Parameter | Frequency (First 72 Hours) | Action Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Serum phosphorus | Every 6–12 h | <2.0 mg/dL: supplement aggressively |
| Serum potassium | Every 6–12 h | <3.0 mEq/L: supplement; ECG monitoring |
| Serum magnesium | Every 12–24 h | <1.5 mg/dL: supplement |
| Blood glucose | Every 4–8 h | >300 mg/dL: reduce dextrose, consider insulin |
| ECG | Continuous if K+ or PO4 abnormal | Arrhythmias: stop feeding, correct electrolytes |

#### Species-Specific Refeeding Notes

- **Raptors**: Particularly susceptible; emaciated raptors should receive warm fluids and electrolyte correction before any caloric intake. Begin with small amounts of lean prey tissue (low fat to reduce hepatic stress).
- **Seabirds**: Oiled or emaciated seabirds require gastric intubation with an electrolyte solution first, then gradual introduction of fish slurry. Monitor for aspergillosis (immunocompromised birds are susceptible).
- **Marine mammals**: Emaciated pinnipeds should receive oral electrolyte solutions before introducing fish. Monitor for hypoglycemia (particularly in neonates).
- **Reptiles**: Slow metabolic rate provides some protection against acute refeeding syndrome; however, anorexic chelonians and lizards should still have nutrition reintroduced gradually at POTZ temperatures.
