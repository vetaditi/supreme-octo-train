# Analgesics and Anti-inflammatory Drugs

## Introduction

Pain management in wildlife is both an ethical imperative and a clinical necessity. Pain causes physiological stress, immunosuppression, delayed healing, and behavioral changes that reduce survival probability. However, pain assessment in wild animals is challenging: species may mask signs of pain as a survival strategy, species-specific pain indicators are poorly characterized for most taxa, and validated pain scoring systems are available for only a handful of species.

## Pain Assessment in Wildlife

### Challenges

- Wild animals instinctively conceal signs of pain and vulnerability
- Behavioral baselines are often unknown for individual patients
- Stress and fear responses from capture and handling confound pain assessment
- Species-specific behavioral repertoires require familiarity with normal behavior
- Observer bias and anthropomorphism can lead to both under- and over-recognition of pain

### General Indicators of Pain Across Taxa

| Indicator | Description |
|---|---|
| Altered posture | Guarding, hunched posture, reluctance to bear weight |
| Reduced activity | Lethargy, decreased movement, reluctance to fly or swim |
| Appetite changes | Anorexia, reduced food intake |
| Vocalization | Abnormal calls, increased or decreased vocalization |
| Aggression or withdrawal | Changes in response to handling |
| Physiological markers | Tachycardia, tachypnea, hypertension, elevated cortisol |
| Facial expressions | Grimace scales developed for some mammalian species (mice, rats, rabbits, horses, cats) |
| Self-mutilation | Picking at wounds, feather/fur removal over painful areas |

### Species-Specific Pain Assessment

- **Birds**: fluffed feathers, closed eyes, reluctance to perch, wing droop, decreased preening, altered body posture, lameness; avian grimace scales are being developed for some species
- **Reptiles**: color changes, anorexia, reduced tongue flicking, altered body posture, eyes closed, reluctance to move; pain assessment in reptiles remains poorly studied
- **Marine mammals**: altered swimming pattern, listing, increased respiratory rate, avoidance of contact, changes in social interaction
- **Small mammals**: orbital tightening, ear position changes, whisker position, cheek flattening (grimace scale components where validated)

## Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs)

### Mechanism of Action
NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, reducing prostaglandin synthesis. COX-1 is constitutive (protective functions in GI tract, kidneys, platelets); COX-2 is primarily induced during inflammation. Preferential COX-2 inhibitors offer anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects with reduced GI and renal side effects.

### Meloxicam

The most widely used NSAID in wildlife medicine due to its preferential COX-2 selectivity and broad safety profile across many species.

- **Mammals**: 0.1-0.2 mg/kg PO, SC, IM SID
- **Birds (most species)**: 0.5-1.0 mg/kg PO, IM SID-BID
- **Reptiles**: 0.1-0.5 mg/kg PO, IM SID-every 48h (metabolism slower in ectotherms)
- **Amphibians**: limited data; 0.1-0.2 mg/kg ICe (intracoelomic) or topical bath
- Generally well-tolerated with short-term use; monitor renal function during extended courses
- Liquid oral formulation (1.5 mg/mL) convenient for dosing small patients

### Carprofen

- **Mammals**: 2-4 mg/kg PO, SC SID-BID
- **Birds**: 1-2 mg/kg PO, IM BID
- **Reptiles**: 1-4 mg/kg PO, SC, IM SID-every 48-72h
- Generally safe in most species for short-term use

### NSAID Toxicity in Vultures and Gyps Species

**This is a critical safety concern in wildlife medicine.**

- **Diclofenac** is lethal to Old World vultures (*Gyps* spp.) at therapeutic doses intended for livestock
- Vultures are exposed through consumption of carcasses of livestock treated with diclofenac
- Causes fatal visceral gout and acute renal failure
- Responsible for catastrophic population declines (>95%) in three *Gyps* species in South Asia
- **Diclofenac must never be used in vultures or any closely related scavenging raptor**
- **Ketoprofen, aceclofenac, flunixin, and nimesulide** have also been shown to be toxic to vultures
- **Meloxicam** has been demonstrated safe for vultures and is the recommended NSAID for livestock in vulture range countries
- Tolfenamic acid has also shown safety in *Gyps* vultures in limited studies

### Other NSAIDs

- **Ketoprofen**: 1-2 mg/kg IM, SC SID (mammals); limited use in wildlife due to vulture toxicity concerns and availability of safer alternatives
- **Flunixin meglumine**: 1-2 mg/kg IV, IM SID (large mammals); risk of GI ulceration with repeated doses; toxic to vultures
- **Piroxicam**: used primarily for neoplasia management (anti-tumor properties); 0.3 mg/kg PO SID (mammals)

### NSAID Safety Precautions

- Always ensure adequate hydration before administering NSAIDs (dehydration increases nephrotoxicity risk)
- Avoid concurrent use of multiple NSAIDs or NSAIDs with corticosteroids
- Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration
- Monitor renal function (uric acid in birds, BUN/creatinine in mammals) during extended courses
- Species-specific sensitivity varies; exercise caution with any NSAID in an unstudied species

## Opioids

### Mechanism of Action
Opioids bind to mu, kappa, and delta receptors in the CNS and peripheral tissues to produce analgesia, sedation, and euphoria. Response varies dramatically across species due to differences in receptor distribution and affinity.

### Butorphanol

A mixed agonist-antagonist (kappa agonist, mu antagonist) widely used in wildlife for its analgesic and sedative properties:

- **Mammals**: 0.1-0.4 mg/kg IM, SC, IV every 2-4 hours
- **Birds**: 1-4 mg/kg IM every 2-4 hours (higher doses required than in mammals due to different receptor pharmacology)
- **Reptiles**: 0.5-2 mg/kg IM, SC every 12-24 hours (variable efficacy reported across species)
- Short duration of action limits utility for sustained analgesia
- Minimal respiratory depression compared to pure mu agonists
- Commonly combined with alpha-2 agonists or sedatives for chemical immobilization (see Capture and Anesthesia section)

### Buprenorphine

Partial mu agonist with long duration of action:

- **Mammals**: 0.01-0.05 mg/kg IM, SC, IV every 6-12 hours
- **Birds**: 0.1-0.6 mg/kg IM every 6-12 hours (variable reports of efficacy; some studies suggest limited analgesic effect in birds)
- **Reptiles**: 0.02-0.1 mg/kg IM, SC every 24-72 hours
- Sustained-release formulation (Simbadol) provides 24-72 hours of analgesia from a single injection in some species
- Ceiling effect reduces risk of severe respiratory depression
- Long onset time (20-30 minutes) compared to butorphanol

### Tramadol

Atypical opioid with mu-opioid activity and serotonin/norepinephrine reuptake inhibition:

- **Mammals**: 2-5 mg/kg PO BID-TID
- **Birds**: 5-30 mg/kg PO BID-QID (significant species variation; red-tailed hawks: 11 mg/kg PO BID; Hispaniolan Amazon parrots: 30 mg/kg PO QID)
- **Reptiles**: 5-11 mg/kg PO SID-BID (red-eared sliders, ball pythons — limited studies)
- Oral bioavailability varies dramatically across species
- Useful for outpatient pain management when injectable dosing is not feasible
- Fewer regulatory restrictions than Schedule II opioids in many jurisdictions

### Hydromorphone

Pure mu agonist with potent analgesic properties:

- **Mammals**: 0.05-0.2 mg/kg IM, SC, IV every 4-6 hours
- **Birds**: 0.3-0.6 mg/kg IM every 3-6 hours
- Greater analgesic efficacy than butorphanol for moderate to severe pain
- Respiratory depression and GI ileus are potential side effects

### Methadone

Full mu agonist with NMDA receptor antagonism:

- **Mammals**: 0.1-0.5 mg/kg IM, IV every 4-8 hours
- Useful for multimodal pain management due to dual mechanism
- Limited data in non-mammalian wildlife

### Opioid Considerations in Wildlife

- **Birds**: appear to have different opioid receptor distribution than mammals; kappa-opioid agonists (butorphanol) may be more effective than mu agonists in some avian species, though this is debated in recent literature
- **Reptiles**: opioid analgesia is inconsistent and species-variable; thermal nociceptive assays may not accurately predict clinical analgesic efficacy
- **Amphibians**: mu-opioid receptors present; morphine and butorphanol have demonstrated analgesic effects in some studies
- **Reversal**: naloxone (0.01-0.04 mg/kg IV, IM) reverses mu agonists; naltrexone for sustained reversal

## Local Anesthetics

### Lidocaine

- **Infiltration**: 1-2 mg/kg maximum (mammals, birds); dilute to 0.5-1% for small patients
- **Regional blocks**: splash blocks for wound management, ring blocks for digit amputation, brachial plexus blocks (birds)
- **Epidural**: 0.2 mg/kg (large mammals) for hindquarter procedures
- **Intravenous regional anesthesia (IVRA)**: limb surgery under tourniquet
- **Constant rate infusion (CRI)**: 25-50 mcg/kg/min IV as systemic analgesic adjunct in mammals
- Rapid onset (5-10 minutes), duration 60-90 minutes

### Bupivacaine

- Longer duration than lidocaine (4-6 hours)
- 1-2 mg/kg maximum (mammals, birds) for local infiltration and nerve blocks
- **Liposomal bupivacaine** (Nocita): provides up to 72 hours of local analgesia from a single infiltration; explored for use in wildlife surgical wound management
- **Caution**: more cardiotoxic than lidocaine if inadvertently administered IV

### Applications in Wildlife

- Local blocks are underutilized in wildlife medicine and should be incorporated routinely
- Wing blocks for fracture stabilization in birds
- Splash blocks and incisional blocks for surgical wounds
- Dental blocks for oral procedures in mammals
- Digital nerve blocks for foot and talon procedures
- Intercostal blocks for rib fractures

## Multimodal Analgesia

### Principles

Multimodal analgesia combines drugs from different classes to target multiple points in the pain pathway, achieving superior analgesia with lower doses of individual agents and fewer side effects.

### Recommended Multimodal Protocols

#### Mild Pain (minor procedures, minor trauma)
- Meloxicam (NSAID) alone, or
- Meloxicam + local anesthetic infiltration

#### Moderate Pain (fracture stabilization, soft tissue surgery)
- Meloxicam (NSAID) + opioid (butorphanol or buprenorphine) + local/regional anesthesia

#### Severe Pain (major surgery, severe trauma, burns)
- NSAID + full mu agonist (hydromorphone or methadone) + local/regional anesthesia + adjunctive agents
- Consider ketamine CRI (0.1-0.5 mg/kg/h) as an NMDA antagonist adjunct for wind-up pain in mammals
- Gabapentin (5-10 mg/kg PO BID-TID in mammals) for neuropathic or chronic pain

### Adjunctive Agents

| Drug | Mechanism | Dose (mammal) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gabapentin | Calcium channel modulation | 5-10 mg/kg PO BID-TID | Neuropathic pain; sedation possible |
| Ketamine (sub-anesthetic) | NMDA antagonism | 0.1-0.5 mg/kg/h CRI | Prevents central sensitization |
| Maropitant | NK-1 receptor antagonist | 1 mg/kg SC SID | Antiemetic with possible visceral analgesic properties |
| Acetaminophen | Central COX inhibition (debated) | 10-15 mg/kg PO BID (mammals only) | **Toxic to cats, snakes, and potentially other species — use with extreme caution** |

## Corticosteroids

### Role in Wildlife

Corticosteroids are powerful anti-inflammatory agents but their use in wildlife is generally limited due to:

- Immunosuppression (increased susceptibility to aspergillosis in birds, latent infections)
- Hyperglycemia and diabetogenic potential
- GI ulceration risk, especially when combined with NSAIDs
- Potential to worsen active infections

### Indications Where Corticosteroids May Be Appropriate

- Cerebral edema/spinal cord trauma: dexamethasone 0.5-2 mg/kg IV (controversial but still used acutely)
- Severe allergic reactions/anaphylaxis: dexamethasone 0.5-1 mg/kg IV, IM
- Immune-mediated disease (uncommon diagnosis in wildlife)
- Anti-inflammatory therapy when NSAIDs are contraindicated

### General Recommendation

Avoid corticosteroids in wildlife patients unless specifically indicated. NSAIDs are preferred for anti-inflammatory therapy in most situations. If corticosteroids are used, limit duration and monitor for complications.
