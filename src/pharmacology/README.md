# Pharmacology and Therapeutics

## Introduction

Pharmacology in wildlife medicine operates under constraints that distinguish it sharply from domestic animal practice. The wildlife veterinarian must make therapeutic decisions with limited pharmacokinetic data, enormous species variation, restricted drug access in field settings, and the additional challenge of minimizing handling time and stress. This section provides practical guidance on drug selection, dosing, and administration across the taxonomic breadth encountered in wildlife practice.

## Fundamental Challenges

### Limited Pharmacokinetic Data

Formal pharmacokinetic studies require controlled dosing, serial blood sampling, and adequate sample sizes — conditions that are difficult to achieve in wildlife species. As a result:

- Pharmacokinetic data exist for only a small fraction of wildlife species
- Published doses are often extrapolated from domestic species, closely related taxa, or allometric scaling
- Drug metabolism rates can vary by orders of magnitude between species due to differences in hepatic enzyme activity, body composition, and physiology
- Therapeutic drug monitoring is rarely available in field settings

### Species Variation

The taxonomic diversity encountered in wildlife practice is extraordinary. A single practitioner may treat mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, and fish — each with fundamentally different physiology:

- **Metabolic rate**: varies with body size, thermoregulatory strategy (endotherm vs ectotherm), and activity level
- **Hepatic metabolism**: phase I and phase II enzyme systems differ qualitatively and quantitatively across taxa; some species lack specific metabolic pathways entirely
- **Renal function**: glomerular filtration rate, tubular secretion, and renal portal systems (present in birds, reptiles, amphibians, and fish) affect drug elimination
- **Protein binding**: plasma protein composition and binding affinity vary, affecting drug distribution and free drug concentration
- **Body composition**: percentage of body fat, water, and muscle varies enormously, affecting volume of distribution
- **Gastrointestinal physiology**: hindgut fermenters, foregut fermenters, ruminants, carnivores, and granivores process oral drugs differently

### Allometric Scaling

When species-specific data are unavailable, allometric scaling provides a principled method for estimating drug doses based on the relationship between body size and metabolic rate. However, allometric scaling has important limitations:

- It accounts for metabolic rate differences but not species-specific differences in drug handling
- It is most appropriate for drugs eliminated primarily by hepatic metabolism
- It should not replace species-specific data when available
- It may be unreliable for drugs with narrow therapeutic indices

Allometric scaling principles and practical application are covered in detail in the dedicated chapter.

### Drug Availability in Field Settings

Wildlife veterinarians frequently work in remote locations far from veterinary pharmacies:

- Drug stability under field conditions (temperature extremes, humidity) must be considered
- Portable drug kits must be carefully curated for the most likely clinical scenarios
- Controlled substance regulations may limit which drugs can be carried into the field
- Reconstituted drugs may have limited shelf life once prepared
- Intramuscular and subcutaneous routes are often preferred over IV in field settings due to ease of administration

## Therapeutic Decision-Making Framework

When approaching pharmacotherapy in a wildlife patient, consider the following framework:

### 1. Is Treatment Appropriate?

Not every wild animal should receive medical treatment. Consider:

- **Conservation value**: is the individual from a threatened population where every animal matters?
- **Prognosis**: can the animal be returned to the wild with full function?
- **Welfare**: will treatment cause more suffering than benefit?
- **Population perspective**: does treating one animal create risks for the population (e.g., antimicrobial resistance, pathogen shedding)?
- **Legal framework**: are there regulatory or permit requirements for treating this species?

### 2. What Is the Diagnosis?

Empirical treatment is often necessary in wildlife medicine, but effort should be made to establish a diagnosis:

- Prioritize diagnostic sample collection before initiating treatment
- Broad-spectrum empirical therapy may be started while awaiting results
- Adjust therapy based on culture and sensitivity, molecular diagnostics, or treatment response

### 3. Drug Selection

Select drugs based on:

- Known efficacy against the target pathogen or condition
- Published dose for the species or closest related taxon
- Known safety profile in the taxon (avoid documented toxic combinations, e.g., NSAIDs in vultures)
- Route of administration feasible for the species and clinical setting
- Duration of action compatible with handling limitations
- Withdrawal considerations for animals that may re-enter the food chain (scavengers consuming treated carcasses)

### 4. Dose Determination

Dose selection priority:

1. Species-specific pharmacokinetic data (highest confidence)
2. Published clinical dose for the species (clinical experience, case reports)
3. Dose for closely related species in the same order or family
4. Allometric scaling from a well-studied reference species
5. Domestic animal dose (lowest confidence for distant taxa)

### 5. Monitoring and Adjustment

- Monitor treatment response clinically and, where possible, with diagnostic testing
- Adjust dose and duration based on response
- Be alert for adverse effects, particularly with drugs that have limited safety data in the species
- Document doses and outcomes to contribute to the collective knowledge base

## Key Resources

Wildlife drug dosing references that should be available in every wildlife veterinary practice:

- Exotic Animal Formulary (Carpenter and Marion)
- Zoo Animal and Wildlife Immobilization and Anesthesia (West et al.)
- Fowler's Zoo and Wild Animal Medicine (Miller and Fowler)
- IUCN/SSC Guidelines for reintroduction and translocation health protocols
- Regional wildlife rehabilitation pharmacology guides

These references are supplemented by primary literature, institutional formularies, and the clinical experience shared through professional networks such as the Wildlife Disease Association, American Association of Zoo Veterinarians, and European Association of Zoo and Wildlife Veterinarians.
