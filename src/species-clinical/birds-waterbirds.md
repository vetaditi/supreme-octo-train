# Waterbirds and Shorebirds

## Overview

Waterbirds and shorebirds encompass a diverse assemblage of avian species that depend on aquatic habitats for feeding, breeding, or both. Their association with water creates specific clinical challenges, including waterproofing maintenance, susceptibility to aquatic toxins and pollutants, and exposure to waterborne pathogens. This chapter covers waterfowl, herons and egrets, pelicans, shorebirds, and seabirds, with emphasis on oil spill response, avian influenza surveillance, and habitat-associated diseases.

## Waterfowl (Anseriformes: Ducks, Geese, Swans)

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- **Waterproofing**: Feathers are waterproofed through a combination of uropygial gland secretions, feather microstructure (barbule interlocking), and preening behavior. Loss of waterproofing is immediately life-threatening—water penetrates to the skin, destroying insulation and causing hypothermia.
- **Lamellate bill**: Comb-like structures along bill edges filter food from water; straining behavior concentrates ingested contaminants and toxins.
- **Well-developed cecae**: Important for microbial fermentation; waterfowl have relatively complex GI tracts compared to many avian orders.
- **Countercurrent heat exchange in legs**: Arteriovenous rete in legs prevents heat loss through feet in cold water.
- **Salt glands (supraorbital)**: Excrete excess sodium chloride in marine and brackish water species; produce a concentrated brine that drips from the nares.
- **Keel musculature**: Powerful flight muscles support long-distance migration in many species.

### Common Diseases and Clinical Concerns

#### Lead Shot Ingestion

- Waterfowl ingest spent lead shot from sediment while feeding, mistaking it for grit or seeds
- Lead shot has been banned for waterfowl hunting in many jurisdictions (US federal ban since 1991) but persists in the environment for decades
- Clinical signs: weight loss, green diarrhea, wing droop, weakness, seizures
- Radiographic confirmation of metallic densities in the ventriculus
- Treatment: CaEDTA chelation (35 mg/kg IM BID); surgical or endoscopic removal of shot from ventriculus
- Gizzard (ventriculus) grinding action fragments lead shot, accelerating absorption compared to lead ingestion in other taxa

#### Avian Botulism

- **Clostridium botulinum** type C and type E toxins cause flaccid paralysis in waterfowl
- Outbreaks occur in warm, shallow, nutrient-rich wetlands with decaying organic matter
- Maggot cycle: botulinum toxin concentrates in invertebrates feeding on carcasses; waterfowl ingest invertebrates and are poisoned; their carcasses produce more toxin
- Clinical signs: progressive flaccid paralysis starting with legs and wings ("limberneck"), inability to fly, drowning
- Treatment: supportive care (fluids, nutritional support, shade); antitoxin if available but supply is extremely limited
- Prevention: carcass removal to break the maggot cycle; water level management

#### Avian Cholera

- *Pasteurella multocida* causes peracute to acute septicemia with massive die-offs
- Mortality can be very rapid (found dead with no premonitory signs)
- Survivors may develop chronic infections (joint, sinus, or CNS involvement)
- Treatment: antibiotics (enrofloxacin, penicillin); however, treatment of wild birds during die-offs focuses on population management rather than individual treatment
- Important differential in any mass mortality event involving waterfowl

#### Aspergillosis

- *Aspergillus fumigatus* infection of the respiratory system (air sacs, lungs)
- Waterfowl are moderately susceptible; stress, immunosuppression, and poor air quality increase risk
- Chronic granulomatous disease; poor prognosis once advanced
- Diagnosis: radiography (air sac opacity), endoscopy (visible plaques), culture, serology (galactomannan antigen)
- Treatment: voriconazole or itraconazole; nebulization with amphotericin B or terbinafine

### Handling Considerations

- Restrain by controlling head/neck (to prevent biting) and wings (to prevent battering)
- Swan and goose wing strikes can cause significant injury to handlers
- Avoid compressing the keel/sternum during restraint (respiratory compromise)
- Handle with clean gloves to avoid transferring oils or contaminants to feathers
- Assess waterproofing before release: float test in clean water, observing for water penetration

## Herons, Egrets, and Bitterns (Ardeidae)

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Long neck with specialized S-curved vertebral configuration allowing rapid strike for fish capture
- Dagger-like bill used for spearing prey; can be directed at handler's eyes during restraint
- Powder down feathers (specialized feathers that continuously disintegrate into fine powder) used in plumage maintenance
- Long legs with partial webbing; wading specialists

### Common Clinical Presentations

- **Fishing line and hook entanglement**: Among the most common presentations; hooks embedded in esophagus, proventriculus, or externally. Monofilament line causes constriction injuries to wings, legs, and neck.
- **Botulism**: Herons and egrets are susceptible to type C and E botulism
- **West Nile virus**: Some ardeid species are susceptible; encephalitis with neurological signs
- **Traumatic injuries**: Vehicle strikes, predator attacks (especially on ground-nesting species)
- **Mycobacteriosis**: *Mycobacterium avium* complex; chronic wasting disease with granulomatous lesions

### Handling Considerations

- **Primary danger**: The bill. Herons and egrets will strike at handlers' faces and eyes with remarkable speed and accuracy. Always control the head first and wear eye protection.
- Wrap the body and wings in a towel; control the neck behind the head
- Long legs are fragile; support feet and legs during restraint to prevent fractures

## Pelicans (Pelecanidae)

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Large, expandable gular (throat) pouch used for prey capture; pouch injuries are common clinical presentations
- Pneumatic skeleton with extensive subcutaneous air sac system
- Large body size (brown pelican 3–5 kg; white pelican 5–9 kg)
- Obligate plunge-divers (brown pelican) or surface-feeding (white pelican)

### Common Clinical Presentations

- **Fishing hook and line entanglement**: Extremely common; hooks in pouch, esophagus, or externally
- **Pouch lacerations and infections**: Torn pouch membrane impairs feeding; bacterial or fungal infection of pouch tissue
- **West Nile virus encephalitis**: Brown pelicans experienced significant mortality events
- **Parasitism**: Air sac nematodes (*Contracaecum*), trematodes
- **Oil contamination**: Brown pelicans are frequently affected during coastal oil spills

### Handling Considerations

- Control the bill by grasping the upper and lower mandible together; avoid pinching the pouch
- Wrap wings against body to prevent flapping and injury
- Pelicans can regurgitate fish and stomach contents when stressed; position to allow drainage

## Shorebirds (Charadriiformes: Plovers, Sandpipers, etc.)

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Generally small-bodied with high metabolic rates
- Extremely long-distance migrants; some species (bar-tailed godwit) fly over 11,000 km non-stop
- Pre-migratory fattening: body mass may double before migration; physiological organ remodeling (gut shrinkage, pectoral muscle hypertrophy)
- Variable bill morphology adapted to specific feeding niches (probing, pecking, sweeping)
- Precocial chicks (many species); young are mobile shortly after hatching

### Common Clinical Concerns

- **Habitat loss**: Staging and stopover site degradation is the primary conservation threat; affects body condition and survival
- **Contaminant exposure**: Heavy metals (mercury, selenium) in coastal sediments; bioaccumulation through invertebrate prey
- **Avian influenza**: Shorebirds, particularly in the families Scolopacidae and Charadriidae, are important reservoir and sentinel species for low-pathogenicity avian influenza (LPAI) viruses
- **Botulism**: Susceptible to type C botulism at inland wetlands
- **Predation**: Nest predation is a major population-limiting factor; predator management is a conservation tool in some contexts

### Handling Considerations

- Small, fragile birds; gentle handling essential
- Stress susceptibility is very high; minimize handling time
- Leg banding must be correctly sized to avoid injury; improper bands cause constriction and leg necrosis
- Long-billed species: control the bill to prevent eye injury to handlers

## Seabirds

### Species Groups

Includes albatrosses, petrels, and shearwaters (Procellariiformes); gulls and terns (Laridae); auks and puffins (Alcidae); gannets and boobies (Sulidae); frigatebirds (Fregatidae).

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- **Salt gland excretion**: Well-developed supraorbital salt glands excrete hypertonic NaCl solution; functional assessment is part of clinical evaluation in debilitated seabirds
- **Procellariform stomach oil**: Petrels and shearwaters can eject a foul-smelling stomach oil as a defense mechanism; also used to feed chicks. This oil can damage waterproofing of other birds in group housing.
- **Specialized flight adaptations**: Albatrosses use dynamic soaring (minimal flapping); auks have wing-propelled diving. These functional requirements influence fracture prognosis.
- **Colonial nesting**: Facilitates disease transmission; colonies are important monitoring sites for disease surveillance

### Common Diseases and Clinical Concerns

- **Plastic ingestion**: Procellariiform seabirds are particularly affected; ingest floating plastic debris mistaken for prey. Causes GI obstruction, perforation, and toxin leaching. Bolus counts at necropsy are used as environmental monitoring indicators.
- **Bycatch**: Incidental capture in fishing gear (longlines, gillnets, trawl nets) is the primary threat to many seabird species worldwide
- **Oil contamination**: Seabirds are highly vulnerable to oil spills; even small amounts of oil can destroy waterproofing
- **Avian pox**: Causes proliferative skin lesions, particularly in gulls and terns; may affect feet, legs, face, and eyes
- **Aspergillosis**: Common in debilitated, immunocompromised seabirds during rehabilitation
- **Newcastle disease (paramyxovirus)**: Cormorants and other seabirds can harbor virulent strains; regulatory concern due to potential transmission to poultry

## Avian Influenza Surveillance

### Importance of Waterbird Surveillance

Wild waterbirds (particularly Anseriformes and Charadriiformes) are the natural reservoir for avian influenza A viruses. Surveillance in wild bird populations is essential for:

- Early detection of highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) emergence
- Understanding viral ecology, diversity, and evolution
- Predicting risk of spillover to poultry and, rarely, humans
- Monitoring geographic spread along migratory flyways

### Surveillance Methods

| Method | Application | Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cloacal swabs | Live-captured birds | Direct viral detection; live bird sampling | Requires capture; single time point |
| Oropharyngeal swabs | Live-captured birds | May have higher sensitivity for some subtypes | Same as above |
| Fresh fecal samples | Environmental sampling | Non-invasive; no bird capture needed | Cannot link to individual; degradation |
| Serology (blood) | Population exposure assessment | Indicates past exposure; population-level data | Requires capture; does not detect active shedding |
| Mortality surveillance | Dead bird collection | Detects HPAI die-offs; pathogen isolation | Delayed detection; carcass scavenging |
| Hunter-harvested sampling | Waterfowl hunting seasons | Large sample sizes; broad geographic coverage | Seasonal; biased toward game species |

### HPAI H5N1 (Clade 2.3.4.4b) Considerations

The global spread of HPAI H5N1 since 2020 has caused unprecedented mortality in wild bird populations:

- Mass die-offs in colonial seabirds (terns, gannets, pelicans, skuas) with population-level impacts
- Spillover to marine mammals (seals, sea lions) with mammalian-adapted mutations
- Barn owls and other raptors highly susceptible through predation of infected birds
- Scavenging species (vultures, crows, gulls) at high risk
- Biosafety precautions essential when handling sick or dead wild birds during active HPAI outbreaks: N95 mask, gloves, eye protection, hand hygiene

## Oil Spill Response for Waterbirds

### Immediate Assessment

- Quantify oiling extent: percentage of body surface affected, location of oiling
- Assess overall condition: body weight, hydration, temperature, injuries
- Triage: categorize as described in the marine mammal oil spill chapter; same principles apply

### Stabilization Before Washing

Washing oiled birds before medical stabilization increases mortality. Stabilization priorities:

1. **Warmth**: Maintain ambient temperature at 27–30°C
2. **Hydration**: Oral or subcutaneous fluids (Lactated Ringer's solution or equivalent); address dehydration before washing
3. **Nutrition**: Tube-feed calorie-dense formula once hydrated; activated charcoal if petroleum ingestion is suspected
4. **GI protection**: Bismuth subsalicylate or sucralfate for GI irritation from petroleum ingestion
5. **Stabilization period**: Typically 24–48 hours before washing, depending on condition

### Washing Protocol

- 1–2% Dawn dishwashing detergent in 40–41°C water
- Systematic washing: work detergent into feathers against the grain, then with the grain
- Multiple wash cycles may be required until water beads off cleanly
- Thorough rinse with clean 40°C water using high-pressure nozzles
- Drying in warm environment (heat lamps, warm room)

### Waterproofing Assessment and Release

- Pool time: birds must demonstrate waterproofing by floating without water penetrating feathers
- Preening behavior: normal preening indicates returning waterproofing
- Flight capability: birds must be flight-tested before release
- Release site selection: uncontaminated habitat with appropriate food resources
- Banding before release for post-release monitoring

## General Principles for Waterbird Cases

### Waterproofing Maintenance During Rehabilitation

- Handle waterbirds with clean, powder-free gloves to avoid contaminating feathers
- Minimize contact with feathers during medical procedures
- House in clean, dry environments when not in water
- Provide access to clean swimming water for preening and waterproofing maintenance
- Avoid petroleum-based ointments or products near feathers
- Assess waterproofing regularly: float test, feather condition evaluation

### Nutritional Support

- Piscivorous species (herons, pelicans, many seabirds): whole fish appropriate to species size; supplement with thiamine if frozen fish is used (freezing degrades thiamine)
- Herbivorous/granivorous waterfowl: leafy greens, grain, aquatic vegetation
- Filter-feeding species: require specialized diets that may be difficult to replicate in captivity
- Monitor body weight daily; waterbirds can decline rapidly

### Housing Requirements

- Access to appropriate water depth for species (swimming pool, shallow wading area)
- Non-abrasive substrate to prevent keel sores and bumblefoot
- Adequate space to prevent feather damage from enclosure walls
- Protection from predators, weather extremes, and disturbance
- Appropriate grouping: some species are colonial and benefit from conspecific housing; others are territorial
