# Marine Mammals

## Overview

Marine mammals present some of the most logistically and clinically complex cases in wildlife veterinary medicine. These species have evolved remarkable adaptations for aquatic life—including diving physiology, thermoregulation in cold water, and osmoregulation—that profoundly influence clinical assessment and treatment. This chapter covers cetaceans, pinnipeds, sirenians, and sea otters, with emphasis on stranding response, common diseases, and the unique physiological challenges of treating marine species.

## Cetaceans

### Mysticetes (Baleen Whales)

#### Species Groups

Includes right whales (Balaenidae), rorquals (Balaenopteridae: blue, fin, humpback, minke whales), and gray whales (Eschrichtiidae).

#### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Baleen plates (keratinous filter-feeding structures) replace teeth; examination of baleen condition provides health information
- Body size ranges from 6-meter minke whales to 30-meter blue whales; direct clinical intervention is extremely limited in free-ranging animals
- Thick blubber layer (up to 50 cm in right whales) serves thermoregulation, energy storage, and buoyancy functions
- Obligate ram ventilators: blowholes must be above water for respiration
- Lack vocal cords but produce complex vocalizations via laryngeal structures

#### Common Clinical Concerns

- **Entanglement**: Fishing gear (lobster pot lines, gillnets) entanglement is a leading cause of mortality in North Atlantic right whales. Disentanglement requires specialized teams and carries significant risk to both responders and whales. Chronic entanglement causes line-related wounds, systemic infection, emaciation, and reproductive failure.
- **Ship strikes**: Blunt trauma and propeller lacerations; often fatal. Speed restrictions in whale habitat areas are a primary mitigation strategy.
- **Biotoxin exposure**: Harmful algal blooms (domoic acid from *Pseudo-nitzschia*, brevetoxin from *Karenia brevis*) cause neurological disease and mortality.
- **Parasitism**: Cyamid whale lice, barnacles (commensal in most cases), and internal parasites including nematodes, cestodes, and trematodes.

### Odontocetes (Toothed Whales and Dolphins)

#### Species Groups

Includes dolphins (Delphinidae), porpoises (Phocoenidae), beaked whales (Ziphiidae), sperm whales (Physeteridae), and river dolphins (Iniidae, Platanistidae).

#### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- **Echolocation (biosonar)**: Sound production via melon and nasal air sacs; reception through lower jaw fat bodies. Audiometric testing is an important diagnostic tool.
- **Telescoped skull**: Nasal passages migrated dorsally during evolution; creates unique CT/radiographic anatomy
- **Homodont dentition**: Teeth are uniform in shape (peg-like); tooth counts vary by species. Tooth sections can be used for age determination (growth layer groups).
- **Voluntary respiration**: Unlike terrestrial mammals, breathing in cetaceans is under conscious control. Implications for anesthesia are profound—general anesthesia eliminates the drive to breathe.

#### Common Diseases

- **Cetacean morbillivirus (CeMV)**: Causes mass mortality events in dolphins and porpoises worldwide. Pneumonia, encephalitis, and immunosuppression. Outbreaks in immunologically naive populations are devastating.
- **Lobomycosis-like disease (lacaziosis)**: Chronic fungal skin disease; granulomatous nodules primarily in bottlenose dolphins. Caused by *Paracoccidioides cetii*.
- **Brucellosis (*Brucella ceti*)**: Causes reproductive failure, meningoencephalitis, and subcutaneous abscesses. Zoonotic potential.
- **Antibiotic-resistant bacteria**: Increasing prevalence of methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA) and other resistant organisms documented in stranded cetaceans, reflecting environmental contamination.
- **Noise-related injury**: Sonar, seismic surveys, and pile driving cause acoustic trauma; beaked whales are particularly susceptible and may strand in response to military sonar.

## Diving Physiology

Understanding diving physiology is essential for clinical management of all marine mammals:

### Oxygen Storage and Conservation

| Adaptation | Mechanism | Clinical Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| High blood volume | 2–3x terrestrial mammal blood volume per kg | Hemorrhage tolerance; blood sampling limits different from terrestrial species |
| Elevated hematocrit | 40–65% in many species | Normal values may appear polycythemic by terrestrial standards |
| High myoglobin | 3–10x terrestrial levels | Dark muscle color is normal; elevated CK after exertion may be extreme |
| Splenic contraction | Spleen releases stored red blood cells during dives | Splenomegaly is a normal finding; splenic hematocrit differs from systemic |
| Diving bradycardia | Heart rate drops to 10–20% of surface rate | ECG interpretation must account for normal arrhythmias associated with dive reflex |
| Peripheral vasoconstriction | Blood flow redirected to brain and heart during dives | Hypothermic extremities during/after dives are normal |

### Decompression and Nitrogen Narcosis

- Marine mammals were historically thought immune to decompression sickness, but evidence of gas bubble lesions (gas emboli) has been documented, particularly in stranded beaked whales
- Rapid ascent scenarios (e.g., fleeing sonar) may overwhelm normal nitrogen management mechanisms
- Clinical significance is an active area of research

## Thermoregulation

Marine mammals maintain core body temperature (36–38°C) in water that can be near freezing:

- **Blubber**: Primary insulation in cetaceans, pinnipeds, and sirenians; thickness varies seasonally and with nutritional status. Blubber biopsy is a standard technique for contaminant analysis, genetics, and hormone measurement.
- **Countercurrent heat exchangers**: Arteriovenous rete mirabile in flippers, flukes, and fins allows heat conservation or dissipation as needed
- **Hyperthermia risk on land**: Stranded cetaceans and hauled-out pinnipeds are at risk of hyperthermia, especially in warm conditions. Active cooling during stranding response is critical.
- **Sea otters**: Rely on dense fur (up to 100,000 hairs per cm²) and high metabolic rate rather than blubber for thermoregulation. Oil contamination destroys insulative properties of fur and is rapidly fatal.

## Pinnipeds

### Phocids (True Seals)

#### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- No external ear pinnae (hence "earless seals")
- Hindflippers cannot rotate forward; locomotion on land is by undulation
- Annual molt involves epidermal and hair replacement; "catastrophic molt" in elephant seals and monk seals involves large sheets of skin and hair
- Relatively large eyes adapted for low-light underwater vision
- Large body size range: 45-kg ringed seals to 3,700-kg southern elephant seals

#### Common Diseases

- **Phocine distemper virus (PDV)**: Morbillivirus that caused massive die-offs in harbor seals (North Sea, 1988 and 2002). Pneumonia, neurological disease, and immunosuppression.
- **Avian influenza**: Increasingly documented in pinnipeds; H5N1 highly pathogenic avian influenza caused mass mortality in South American sea lions and elephant seals (2023).
- **Leptospirosis**: *Leptospira interrogans* serovar Pomona causes annual epizootic renal disease in California sea lions. Acute renal failure, lethargy, dehydration, polydipsia.
- **Hookworm (*Uncinaria* spp.)**: Causes significant mortality in neonatal fur seals and sea lions; percutaneous and transmammary transmission.
- **Domoic acid toxicosis**: Neurotoxin produced by *Pseudo-nitzschia* diatoms; bioaccumulates through fish prey. Causes seizures, abnormal behavior, hippocampal necrosis.

### Otariids (Fur Seals and Sea Lions)

#### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- External ear pinnae present
- Hindflippers rotate forward, enabling quadrupedal locomotion on land
- Dense underfur (fur seals) provides insulation
- Sexual dimorphism is extreme; male Steller sea lions can be 4x the mass of females
- More agile on land than phocids; can be aggressive and fast-moving

### Odobenids (Walruses)

- Tusks (elongated upper canines) are used for hauling out, social display, and defense; tusk fractures and abscesses occur
- Extremely thick skin (up to 5 cm) with sparse hair
- Vibrissae (whiskers) are highly innervated and used for benthic foraging
- Body mass up to 1,800 kg; handling requires specialized facilities

### Pinniped Handling Considerations

- **Phocids**: Hoop nets, net guns, or physical restraint with boards/cages for small species; chemical immobilization for large species
- **Otariids**: More dangerous on land due to mobility; squeeze cages, nets, and chemical immobilization preferred. Bite wounds are a significant risk—powerful jaws can cause deep, contaminated wounds.
- **All pinnipeds**: Isoflurane/sevoflurane via mask or intubation for maintenance anesthesia; injectable protocols (medetomidine-midazolam-butorphanol or similar) for field induction
- **Venipuncture sites**: Epidural venous sinus (intervertebral), flipper veins, or jugular vein depending on species and clinical context

## Sirenians

### Species Groups

Manatees (Trichechidae: West Indian, Amazonian, West African) and dugongs (Dugongidae).

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Fully aquatic herbivores; hindlimbs absent, forelimbs modified as flippers, horizontal tail fluke (manatees) or fluked tail (dugongs)
- Low metabolic rate; susceptible to cold stress (manatees aggregate near warm-water sources in winter)
- Continuous tooth replacement (manatees): worn molars are shed anteriorly and replaced from behind, similar to elephants
- Dense, heavy bones (pachyostotic and osteosclerotic) provide negative buoyancy for bottom feeding
- Slow reproductive rate: long gestation (~12 months), single calf, extended maternal care

### Common Diseases and Clinical Concerns

- **Watercraft strikes**: The leading cause of manatee mortality in Florida. Propeller lacerations are characteristic and often used for individual identification from scar patterns.
- **Cold stress syndrome**: Prolonged exposure to water below 20°C causes immunosuppression, skin lesions, secondary infections, and death. Rehabilitation of cold-stressed manatees is a major clinical effort in Florida winters.
- **Brevetoxicosis**: Florida red tide (*Karenia brevis*) toxin exposure through inhalation at the water surface and ingestion of contaminated seagrass. Causes neurological signs, respiratory distress, and death.
- **Papillomatosis**: Cutaneous papillomas are common; generally benign but may become extensive.
- **Pneumonia**: Bacterial and parasitic (*Pulmonicola* trematodes) pneumonia are significant causes of morbidity and mortality.

### Handling Considerations

- Capture typically involves nets and physical restraint in shallow water
- Chemical immobilization is rarely used and carries high risk in aquatic settings
- Skin must be kept moist during out-of-water examinations
- Transport in padded slings suspended in water-filled containers
- Dense bones make radiographic interpretation different from other marine mammals

## Sea Otters

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Smallest marine mammal; body mass 14–45 kg
- No blubber layer; relies on fur insulation (densest fur of any mammal) and extremely high metabolic rate (2–3x predicted for body size)
- Must consume 25–30% of body weight daily to maintain thermal homeostasis
- Dexterous forepaws used for tool use (stone anvils to crack shellfish)
- Keystone species: sea otter presence controls sea urchin populations, maintaining kelp forest ecosystems

### Common Diseases

- **Toxoplasma gondii**: Leading infectious cause of death in southern sea otters. Land-to-sea transmission via freshwater runoff carrying cat feces. Meningoencephalitis, myocarditis.
- **Sarcocystis neurona**: Protozoal meningoencephalitis; opossum is the definitive host. Another example of land-to-sea pathogen transmission.
- **Acanthocephalan peritonitis**: Intestinal perforation by acanthocephalan parasites (*Profilicollis* spp.) causes fatal peritonitis.
- **Valvular endocarditis**: Often associated with dental disease and periodontal infection; bacteria seed cardiac valves.
- **Oil spill vulnerability**: Fur contamination eliminates insulative capacity. Hypothermia, toxic ingestion during grooming, and organ damage (hepatic, renal, pulmonary) follow. Sea otter rehabilitation after oil spills is resource-intensive and well-protocolized.

## Stranding Response

### Initial Assessment Protocol

1. **Scene safety**: Assess hazards (waves, traffic, aggressive animals, crowds)
2. **Species identification and count**: Document species, number of animals, and approximate sizes
3. **Live vs. dead determination**: Observe for respiration, eye movement, and flipper movement
4. **Contact authorities**: Notify regional stranding network coordinator; all marine mammal strandings in the US are coordinated under NOAA authorization
5. **Initial stabilization (live animals)**:
   - Keep blowhole/nares clear and above water
   - Provide shade; keep skin moist with wet towels (cetaceans) but do not cover blowhole
   - Minimize noise and crowd presence
   - Do not attempt to push live cetaceans back into the water without veterinary assessment
   - Monitor respiration rate and character

### Mass Stranding Events

- Most commonly involve odontocetes (pilot whales, dolphins) or pinnipeds
- Triage is essential: assess each animal and categorize as releasable, treatable, or candidates for euthanasia
- Cause investigation: sonar exposure, biotoxin, infectious disease, and navigational error are common differentials
- Necropsy of dead animals is critical for cause determination
- Coordinate with public health authorities if zoonotic disease is suspected

## Oil Spill Response

Marine mammal response during oil spills follows established protocols:

### Triage Categories

| Category | Description | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Lightly oiled** | <25% body surface oiled, alert, good body condition | Capture, stabilize, wash |
| **Moderately oiled** | 25–75% oiled, responsive, fair condition | Capture, stabilize, pre-wash care, wash |
| **Heavily oiled** | >75% oiled, depressed, poor condition | Capture, intensive stabilization, wash when stable |
| **Moribund** | Non-responsive, severe hypothermia, seizures | Euthanasia may be most humane option |

### Washing Protocol Principles

- Stabilize medically before washing (hydration, thermal support, GI protectants)
- Dawn dishwashing detergent is the standard washing agent for oiled wildlife
- Water temperature approximately 40°C for sea otters; adjusted by species
- Thorough rinsing is essential to restore waterproofing
- Post-wash monitoring in pool to confirm waterproofing before release
- Sea otters require extensive fur grooming and conditioning post-wash

## Sample Collection in Marine Mammals

Maximize data collection during each handling event:

- **Blood**: Jugular, epidural sinus, or flipper veins depending on species; collect for CBC, chemistry, serology, contaminant analysis, genetics
- **Blubber biopsy**: Remote biopsy darts for free-ranging cetaceans; punch biopsy during handling for pinnipeds
- **Respiratory cytology**: Blow samples (exhaled breath condensate) from cetacean blowholes using collection plates
- **Fecal/rectal swabs**: Parasitology, microbiology, hormone metabolites
- **Skin swabs**: Dermatology, molecular diagnostics
- **Morphometrics**: Standard body measurements, weight, girth, blubber depth (ultrasound)
