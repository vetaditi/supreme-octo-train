# Raptors

## Overview

Raptors—birds of prey—are among the most frequently presented wildlife patients in rehabilitation facilities worldwide. Their position as apex and mesopredators makes them vulnerable to bioaccumulation of toxins, and their hunting behaviors expose them to specific injury patterns. This chapter covers diurnal raptors (eagles, hawks, falcons) and nocturnal raptors (owls), with emphasis on raptor-specific anatomy, common injuries and diseases, and rehabilitation considerations.

## Raptor-Specific Anatomy

### General Avian Anatomy Relevant to Raptors

- **Pneumatic bones**: Many bones contain air spaces connected to the respiratory system. Fractures of pneumatic bones can cause subcutaneous emphysema and complicate fracture repair.
- **Unidirectional respiratory system**: Air sacs (typically nine) and parabronchial lungs provide continuous unidirectional airflow and highly efficient gas exchange. No residual lung volume; anesthetic gas concentrations change rapidly.
- **Keel (carina)**: Prominent ventral extension of the sternum for pectoral muscle attachment; keel condition is a rapid body condition indicator.
- **Crop**: Esophageal dilation in the ventral cervical region for food storage. Crop stasis, impaction, or burns (from improper tube feeding) are clinical concerns.
- **Uropygial (preen) gland**: Dorsal to the pygostyle; produces oil for feather conditioning. Abscessation and neoplasia occur.

### Raptor-Specific Features

- **Cere**: Fleshy, waxy covering at the base of the upper beak surrounding the nares. Color varies by species and can indicate age (in some species) and health status. Cere swelling or discoloration may indicate sinusitis, aspergillosis, or trichomoniasis.
- **Talons**: Powerful grasping feet with sharp, curved talons are the primary weapons for prey capture. Talon grip strength varies: large eagles can exert 400+ psi. Handler injury from talons is the primary safety concern. Bumblefoot (pododermatitis) is one of the most common captive raptor conditions.
- **Beak**: Hooked upper beak (maxilla) with tomial tooth in falcons (notch on the cutting edge used to sever cervical vertebrae of prey). Beak overgrowth, fractures, and avascular necrosis occur.
- **Nictitating membrane (third eyelid)**: Translucent protective membrane; provides eye protection during prey capture. Examination of the nictitating membrane is part of the standard ophthalmic assessment.
- **Exceptional visual acuity**: Raptor eyes are large relative to skull size; some species have visual acuity 4–8x that of humans. Eye injuries are common and often career-ending for rehabilitation candidates.

### Owl-Specific Features

- **Asymmetric ear placement**: In many owl species, ears are positioned asymmetrically on the skull, enabling precise sound localization for hunting in darkness. Auditory function assessment is important for rehabilitation candidates.
- **Facial disc**: Concave feather arrangement around the face that functions as a parabolic sound reflector, directing sound to the ears.
- **Silent flight**: Specialized feather structure (comb-like leading edge of primary feathers, fringed trailing edges, velvety dorsal surface) reduces turbulence noise. Feather damage that compromises silent flight may render owls non-releasable.
- **Fixed eye sockets**: Owl eyes are tubular rather than spherical and cannot rotate in the orbit. Compensated by extreme cervical rotation (up to 270°).
- **Reversed fourth toe (zygodactyl arrangement)**: Owls can rotate the outer toe backward, giving them a two-forward, two-backward grip.

## Common Injuries

### Fractures

Fractures are the most common traumatic presentation in raptors. Causes include vehicle collisions, gunshot, window strikes, power line collisions, and territorial combat.

#### Assessment

- Palpation of all long bones, coracoid, clavicle (furcula), and keel during initial examination
- Radiography is essential: minimum two orthogonal views
- Classify fractures by location, type (transverse, oblique, comminuted), open vs. closed status, and joint involvement
- Assess adjacent soft tissue damage, particularly vascular supply and nerve function

#### Fracture Location and Prognosis

| Fracture Location | Prognosis for Release | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mid-shaft humerus | Fair to good | If good alignment achieved; radial nerve damage possible |
| Distal humerus/elbow | Poor to fair | Joint involvement significantly worsens prognosis |
| Mid-shaft radius/ulna | Good (if one bone intact) | Single bone fractures often heal with coaptation alone |
| Carpometacarpus | Poor to fair | Affects flight feather attachment |
| Mid-shaft femur | Fair | Intramedullary pin with cerclage wires common approach |
| Tibiotarsus | Fair to good | Most common long bone fracture in raptors |
| Tarsometatarsus | Good | External coaptation often sufficient |
| Coracoid | Good | Supportive care; body wrap for stabilization |
| Keel | Good | Usually conservative management |

#### Fixation Methods

- **External coaptation**: Figure-of-eight bandage (wing fractures), body wrap, Altman tape splints
- **Intramedullary pinning**: Single or stacked pins; consider pin size relative to medullary canal diameter
- **External skeletal fixation (ESF)**: Type I or Type II fixators; lightweight acrylic connecting bars preferred
- **Cerclage wires**: Often used with IM pins for oblique fractures

### Lead Poisoning

Lead toxicosis is one of the most significant clinical and conservation issues affecting raptors.

#### Sources

- **Spent lead ammunition**: Bullet fragments in carcasses and gut piles left by hunters; primary exposure route for scavenging raptors (bald eagles, golden eagles, vultures)
- **Lead fishing tackle**: Ingested directly or through prey fish
- **Lead shot in waterfowl**: Raptors consuming shot waterfowl

#### Clinical Signs

- Acute: depression, weakness, green-stained vent (biliverdinuria), crop stasis, ataxia, seizures, death
- Chronic: weight loss, intermittent weakness, wing droop, decreased hunting success

#### Diagnosis

- Blood lead concentration: >0.2 ppm (20 mcg/dL) is elevated; >0.6 ppm (60 mcg/dL) indicates clinical toxicosis
- Radiographs: metallic densities in GI tract (particulate lead fragments)
- Chemistry: elevated CK, AST, LDH, uric acid; decreased PCV

#### Treatment

- **Chelation therapy**: CaEDTA (35 mg/kg IM BID for 5 days, 2 days off, repeat as needed) or D-penicillamine (55 mg/kg PO BID) or succimer (DMSA, 25–35 mg/kg PO BID)
- **GI decontamination**: Remove metallic fragments endoscopically or surgically if present in ventriculus; cathartics if fragments are in intestines
- **Supportive care**: Fluid therapy, nutritional support, anti-seizure medication if needed

### Electrocution and Power Line Injuries

- Occurs when raptors perch on or contact power poles and lines, completing a circuit between conductors or between a conductor and ground
- Burns are typically on feet, legs, and wing tips (contact points)
- Severity ranges from superficial burns to full-thickness necrosis requiring amputation
- Initial assessment may underestimate injury extent; tissue necrosis progresses over days
- Electrical injury to the heart (arrhythmias) and kidneys should be monitored
- Utility companies can retrofit poles with raptor-safe configurations (insulation, perch deterrents, increased conductor spacing)

### Rodenticide Toxicosis

#### Types

- **First-generation anticoagulants** (warfarin, chlorophacinone): Require multiple feedings for lethal dose; shorter half-life
- **Second-generation anticoagulants (SGARs)** (brodifacoum, bromadiolone, difethialone): Single feeding can be lethal to rodents; highly persistent in tissues (half-life weeks to months); bioaccumulate through food chains. SGARs are the primary concern for raptor secondary poisoning.

#### Clinical Presentation

- Hemorrhage: subcutaneous, coelomic cavity, pulmonary, or from minor wounds that fail to clot
- Anemia, lethargy, dyspnea
- May present after minor trauma that would normally be insignificant but causes fatal hemorrhage in a coagulopathic bird

#### Diagnosis

- Prolonged clotting times (PCV/TS, activated clotting time)
- Liver anticoagulant residue analysis (post-mortem)
- Blood or liver toxicology for specific compounds

#### Treatment

- Vitamin K1 (phytonadione): 2.5–5 mg/kg IM initially, then PO for 2–4 weeks depending on compound
- Blood transfusion if severely anemic (PCV <15%)
- Supportive care; minimize handling to reduce hemorrhage risk

## Ophthalmic Assessment

Eye injuries are common in raptors and frequently determine releasability. A thorough ophthalmic examination is essential for every raptor case.

### Standard Examination Protocol

1. **Menace response**: Test each eye independently; assess visual field coverage
2. **Pupillary light reflex (PLR)**: Direct and consensual; note that PLR in birds is partially under voluntary control (striated iris musculature)
3. **External examination**: Periorbital swelling, discharge, eyelid integrity, cere condition
4. **Slit lamp biomicroscopy**: Corneal surface, anterior chamber depth and clarity, lens examination
5. **Fundoscopy (direct and indirect ophthalmoscopy)**: Retinal vasculature, pecten oculi (highly vascularized intraocular structure unique to birds), retinal detachment, hemorrhage
6. **Fluorescein staining**: Corneal ulceration
7. **Tonometry**: Intraocular pressure measurement (normal approximately 10–20 mmHg in most raptors)

### Common Ophthalmic Conditions

- **Traumatic uveitis**: Anterior chamber inflammation from blunt trauma; common after vehicle collisions
- **Hyphema**: Blood in the anterior chamber; often accompanies head trauma
- **Lens luxation**: Displacement of the lens; may be traumatic or secondary to chronic uveitis
- **Cataracts**: Congenital or acquired; surgical extraction is possible but availability is limited
- **Pecten damage**: Hemorrhage from or detachment of the pecten; pathognomonic for intraocular trauma
- **Retinal detachment**: Usually traumatic; poor visual prognosis

### Releasability and Vision

- Raptors with unilateral vision loss may be considered for release in some cases (species-dependent; large soaring species may compensate better than agile pursuit hunters)
- Bilateral vision loss is incompatible with release
- Flight testing in controlled environments (flight cages) with prey items helps assess functional vision and hunting capability

## Rehabilitation Considerations

### Assessment for Releasability

Key criteria for determining whether a raptor can be released:

- **Flight capability**: Full range of motion in both wings; able to sustain flight, gain altitude, and maneuver in a flight cage
- **Vision**: Adequate visual acuity for hunting (species-specific requirements)
- **Foot function**: Grip strength sufficient for prey capture; no bumblefoot or chronic pododermatitis
- **Feather condition**: Complete flight feathers or repairable through imping (surgical replacement of damaged feathers)
- **Behavior**: Appropriate fear of humans; wild behavior maintained

### Feather Imping

Imping (from "implant") is the technique of replacing broken or damaged flight feathers with donor feathers:

- Donor feathers must be species-matched (or close size/shape match) and from the correct wing position
- The shaft of the donor feather is joined to the remaining shaft in the wing using an imping needle (bamboo, fiberglass, or metal) secured with epoxy
- Proper feather alignment is essential for aerodynamic function
- Imped feathers are replaced during the next natural molt

### Conditioning for Release

- Gradual transition from small enclosure to large flight cage
- Flight conditioning: encourage sustained flight with increasing distance and complexity
- Live prey testing (using legally obtained, pre-killed or live prey as regulations permit) to assess hunting capability
- Weathering (outdoor exposure) to acclimate to environmental conditions
- Minimize human contact during conditioning phase

### Non-Releasable Raptor Placement

Raptors that cannot be released may be placed in:

- Education programs at licensed facilities
- Captive breeding programs (for threatened species)
- Falconry programs (in some jurisdictions, with appropriate permits)
- Euthanasia if no suitable placement is available and quality of life would be compromised

## Species-Specific Notes

### Eagles (Bald Eagle, Golden Eagle)

- Federally protected under the Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act (US); additional protections in other jurisdictions
- Lead poisoning is the leading cause of admission; scavenging behavior concentrates exposure
- Large body size requires heavy-duty equipment for handling and housing
- Powerful talons and beak; two experienced handlers recommended for restraint

### Hawks (Red-tailed Hawk, Cooper's Hawk, etc.)

- Vehicle collisions and window strikes are leading causes of admission
- Accipiter species (Cooper's hawk, sharp-shinned hawk) are agile, nervous patients prone to stress
- Buteo species (red-tailed hawk) are generally calmer in captivity

### Falcons (Peregrine, American Kestrel, Merlin)

- High-speed aerial pursuit hunters; wing and keel injuries from high-velocity impacts
- Tomial tooth is a species-defining feature; beak alignment critical for feeding
- Peregrine falcon recovery from DDT-era declines is a conservation success story
- Kestrels are the smallest North American falcons and among the most commonly presented

### Owls (Great Horned, Barred, Barn, Screech Owls)

- Vehicle collisions (attracted to roadside prey) and rodenticide toxicosis are primary presentations
- Nocturnal species; minimize disturbance during daytime; provide dark, quiet housing
- Great horned owls are the most powerful North American owl species; extreme caution during handling
- Barn owls are sensitive to cold stress and have lower body reserves than similarly sized raptors
