# Non-Human Primates

## Overview

Non-human primates (NHPs) occupy a unique position in wildlife veterinary medicine due to their close phylogenetic relationship to humans, which creates exceptional risks for bidirectional disease transmission. Their complex cognitive and social behaviors demand specialized handling approaches, and nearly all NHP species face significant conservation threats. This chapter covers prosimians, New World monkeys, Old World monkeys, and great apes, emphasizing zoonotic risks, behavioral considerations, and the intersection of clinical care with conservation.

## Prosimians

### Species Groups

Lemurs (Lemuridae, Indriidae, and related families—endemic to Madagascar), lorises (Lorisidae), galagos (Galagidae), and tarsiers (Tarsiidae).

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- **Dental comb**: Lower incisors and canines form a forward-projecting comb used for grooming; this structure can harbor bacteria and inflict wounds during bites
- **Tapetum lucidum**: Present in most prosimians; enhances nocturnal vision
- **Torpor**: Some lemur species (e.g., dwarf lemurs, *Cheirogaleus*) undergo seasonal torpor or hibernation—unique among primates
- **Scent glands**: Well-developed in many lemur species; used for territorial marking and social communication
- **Rhinarium**: Moist, bare nose tip (like dogs); distinguishes strepsirrhines from haplorhines

### Common Diseases and Concerns

- **Toxoplasmosis**: Lemurs are exquisitely susceptible to *Toxoplasma gondii*; infection is frequently fatal. Preventing cat access to lemur enclosures and food is critical in captive settings.
- **Herpesvirus**: Several lemur herpesviruses have been identified; cross-species transmission in mixed-species exhibits is a concern.
- **Dental disease**: Periodontal disease and dental caries are common, especially in captive animals on inappropriate diets.
- **Nutritional disorders**: Vitamin D deficiency, obesity, and hemosiderosis (iron storage disease) in captive lemurs.
- **Conservation crisis**: Over 90% of lemur species are threatened with extinction; Madagascar's deforestation is the primary driver. Veterinary involvement in captive breeding and reintroduction programs is critical.

### Handling Considerations

- Many prosimians are nocturnal; examinations may need to accommodate circadian rhythms
- Squeeze cages and nets are standard for capture
- Chemical immobilization with ketamine-based protocols is well established
- Slow lorises (*Nycticebus* spp.) possess brachial gland secretions that, when mixed with saliva, can cause anaphylactic reactions in humans—the only venomous primate group

## New World Monkeys

### Species Groups

Callitrichids (marmosets, tamarins), cebids (capuchins, squirrel monkeys), atelids (howler monkeys, spider monkeys, woolly monkeys), pitheciids (sakis, uakaris, titis).

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- **Prehensile tails**: Present in atelids; functions as a fifth limb. Tail injuries require careful assessment as they significantly impact mobility and survival.
- **Platyrrhine nasal structure**: Widely spaced, laterally directed nostrils distinguish New World from Old World monkeys
- **Callitrichid reproductive biology**: Marmosets and tamarins typically produce dizygotic twins; bone marrow chimerism between twins is normal
- **Vitamin D3 requirements**: Many species, particularly callitrichids, have high UV-B requirements for vitamin D3 synthesis; deficiency causes metabolic bone disease in captivity
- **Vitamin C synthesis**: Unlike Old World primates and humans, many New World monkeys can synthesize vitamin C; however, some species have reduced capacity

### Common Diseases

- **Callitrichid hepatitis (Lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus—LCMV)**: Fatal hepatitis in callitrichids; transmitted from rodent reservoir hosts. Strict rodent control is essential.
- **Wasting marmoset syndrome**: Multifactorial chronic condition in captive callitrichids characterized by weight loss, muscle wasting, chronic diarrhea, and alopecia. Dietary, infectious, and stress factors contribute.
- **Herpes simplex virus (HSV-1)**: Human cold sore virus is frequently fatal in New World monkeys; barrier precautions essential for caretakers with active lesions.
- **Yellow fever**: Howler monkeys are highly susceptible; epizootics in wild populations serve as sentinels for sylvatic yellow fever transmission cycles. Vaccine is available for captive animals.
- **Klebsiella pneumonia**: Rapidly fatal septicemia in spider monkeys and other atelids.

### Handling Considerations

- Arboreal species are agile and difficult to capture; nets, squeeze cages, and dart guns are used depending on setting
- Long prehensile tails must be managed carefully during restraint; do not use the tail as a restraint point
- Callitrichids are small enough for manual restraint with gloves but bite readily
- Capuchins are intelligent and dexterous; they can manipulate latches and equipment

## Old World Monkeys

### Species Groups

Cercopithecines (macaques, baboons, mangabeys, guenons, vervets) and colobines (colobus monkeys, langurs, proboscis monkeys).

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- **Catarrhine nasal structure**: Closely spaced, downward-directed nostrils
- **Ischial callosities**: Thickened skin pads on buttocks; present in all Old World monkeys
- **Cheek pouches**: Present in cercopithecines; used for temporary food storage. Can become impacted or infected.
- **Colobine foregut fermentation**: Colobus monkeys and langurs have a sacculated stomach for foregut fermentation of leaves, convergent with ruminant physiology. Diet must be managed carefully in captivity; inappropriate diet causes bloat and GI disease.
- **Sexual skin swelling**: Cyclic perineal swelling in females of many species indicates reproductive status

### Zoonotic Disease Risks

Old World monkeys present some of the highest zoonotic risks of any wildlife group:

- **Herpes B virus (*Macacine alphaherpesvirus 1*)**: Enzootic in macaques; causes mild disease in the natural host but frequently fatal meningoencephalitis in humans. Bite wounds, scratches, or mucosal splash from macaques require immediate first aid (vigorous wound irrigation for 15+ minutes) and post-exposure prophylaxis (valacyclovir). This is a **critical occupational hazard**.
- **Tuberculosis (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis* complex)**: Old World monkeys are susceptible to human TB; bidirectional transmission occurs. Regular TB testing (intradermal tuberculin test) is standard in managed populations.
- **Simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV)**: Natural reservoir in African cercopithecines; related to HIV. Not directly zoonotic but of research importance.
- **Simian retroviruses (SRV, STLV)**: Various retroviruses circulate in Old World monkey populations with varying zoonotic potential.
- **Shigellosis**: *Shigella* spp. cause dysentery in primates and humans; bidirectional transmission occurs readily.

### Common Clinical Presentations

- Bite wounds from intraspecific aggression (especially in macaques and baboons)
- GI disease (parasitic, bacterial, and dietary)
- Respiratory infections
- Trauma from human-wildlife conflict (crop-raiding species)

### Handling Considerations

- All handlers working with macaques must be trained in Herpes B virus risk management
- Bite-resistant gloves, face shields, and long sleeves are minimum PPE requirements
- Chemical immobilization is preferred for all procedures; ketamine (5–10 mg/kg IM) with medetomidine is a standard protocol
- Baboons are powerful and aggressive; trap-and-squeeze cage systems are standard for capture

## Great Apes

### Species Groups

Chimpanzees (*Pan troglodytes*), bonobos (*Pan paniscus*), gorillas (*Gorilla gorilla*, *G. beringei*), and orangutans (*Pongo pygmaeus*, *P. abelii*, *P. tapanuliensis*).

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Closest living relatives to humans; share 95–99% genetic similarity depending on species
- Large body size: male gorillas can exceed 200 kg
- No tail; broad chest; modified shoulder and wrist joints for brachiation (orangutans) or knuckle-walking (African apes)
- Complex cognitive abilities: tool use, self-recognition, social learning, and cultural traditions documented in all great ape species
- Physiology closely parallels human physiology; many human clinical references are applicable

### Zoonotic and Reverse-Zoonotic Disease Risks

Bidirectional disease transmission between humans and great apes is the defining concern in great ape medicine:

#### Human-to-Ape Transmission (Reverse Zoonoses)

- **Respiratory viruses**: Human respiratory syncytial virus (HRSV), human metapneumovirus (HMPV), influenza, rhinoviruses, and coronaviruses (including SARS-CoV-2) have caused outbreaks and deaths in wild and captive great ape populations.
- **Measles**: Highly lethal in immunologically naive great apes.
- **Tuberculosis**: Human TB can be transmitted to great apes through close contact.
- **Gastrointestinal pathogens**: *Giardia*, pathogenic *E. coli*, and other enteric pathogens of human origin documented in great apes near human settlements.

**Mitigation measures for field work with wild great apes:**

- Maintain a minimum distance of 7 meters (recommended by IUCN)
- Wear N95 masks when within 10 meters
- No field work if ill (any symptoms)
- Hand hygiene before entering great ape habitat
- Quarantine period for personnel arriving from other regions
- Proper latrine management at field sites

#### Ape-to-Human Transmission

- **Simian foamy virus (SFV)**: Transmitted through bites; persistent infection in humans with uncertain long-term consequences
- **Ebola virus**: Great apes (gorillas, chimpanzees) suffer catastrophic mortality from Ebola; transmission to humans through bushmeat handling has initiated outbreaks
- **HIV origins**: HIV-1 originated from SIV in chimpanzees; HIV-2 from SIV in sooty mangabeys

### Anesthesia Challenges

- Intubation and airway management follow human protocols; human-sized laryngoscopes and endotracheal tubes are appropriate
- Cardiovascular monitoring equipment (ECG, blood pressure, pulse oximetry) used as in human medicine
- Drug dosing generally extrapolated from human medicine on a per-kilogram basis
- Recovery from anesthesia in great apes is slow; maintain monitoring until fully conscious
- Cognitive and emotional effects of anesthesia and procedures should be considered; great apes can experience post-procedural behavioral changes

### Biosafety Considerations

- **BSL-2 equivalent precautions** recommended for all direct contact with great apes
- Full PPE: gloves, gown, face shield/mask, eye protection
- Sharp injury protocols paralleling those for human patients
- Serum banking for handlers (baseline and periodic) recommended at institutions housing great apes
- Necropsy of great apes requires heightened biosafety precautions due to potential for unknown pathogen exposure

### Conservation Status

All great ape species are Endangered or Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List:

| Species | IUCN Status | Estimated Wild Population |
|---|---|---|
| Chimpanzee | Endangered | 170,000–300,000 |
| Bonobo | Endangered | 15,000–20,000 |
| Western gorilla | Critically Endangered | ~360,000 |
| Eastern gorilla | Critically Endangered | ~5,000 |
| Bornean orangutan | Critically Endangered | ~104,000 |
| Sumatran orangutan | Critically Endangered | ~14,000 |
| Tapanuli orangutan | Critically Endangered | ~800 |

Veterinary interventions in wild great ape populations (e.g., snare removal, treatment of respiratory disease) must balance individual welfare with population-level disease risk. The IUCN Best Practice Guidelines for great ape health monitoring and disease prevention provide detailed recommendations.

## General Principles for Primate Cases

### Ethical Considerations

Non-human primates' cognitive complexity raises ethical questions that differ from those in other wildlife groups:

- Decisions about euthanasia carry additional weight given cognitive and emotional capacity
- Prolonged captivity for rehabilitation has welfare implications related to social deprivation
- Research procedures must meet stringent ethical review standards
- Orphan care and socialization require expertise in species-specific social development

### Diagnostic Approach

- Many human diagnostic tests and equipment are directly applicable to NHPs
- Human clinical laboratories can process NHP samples (blood chemistry, hematology, serology) with appropriate calibration
- Imaging (radiography, ultrasound, CT, MRI) follows human protocols with size adjustments
- Molecular diagnostics (PCR) designed for human pathogens often detect closely related primate pathogens

### Pharmacology

- Drug metabolism in NHPs closely parallels humans for most drug classes
- Human dosing guidelines (mg/kg) are generally appropriate starting points
- NSAIDs, antibiotics, and antiparasitic agents used in human medicine are typically effective
- Anesthetic protocols: ketamine-based combinations for induction; isoflurane or sevoflurane for maintenance
- Opioids for analgesia follow human protocols

### Rehabilitation and Release

- Social species require appropriate conspecific socialization before release
- Behavioral competence (foraging, predator avoidance, social interaction) must be assessed
- Disease screening before release must be thorough to avoid introducing pathogens to wild populations
- Post-release monitoring is essential; GPS collars, camera traps, and regular observation
- Reintroduction into established social groups requires careful planning to minimize conflict
