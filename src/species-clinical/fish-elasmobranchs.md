# Fish and Elasmobranchs

## Overview

Fish represent the most species-rich vertebrate group, with over 35,000 described species spanning three major lineages: Agnatha (jawless fish—lampreys, hagfish), Chondrichthyes (cartilaginous fish—sharks, rays, skates, chimaeras), and Osteichthyes (bony fish—teleosts and others). Wildlife veterinarians encounter fish in aquaculture health management, zoo and aquarium medicine, rehabilitation of stranded elasmobranchs, and conservation programs for threatened species. This chapter covers clinical principles for teleost fish and elasmobranchs, with emphasis on aquatic examination, anesthesia, water quality, and common disease conditions.

## Aquatic Physiology: Clinical Foundations

### Respiration

- Fish extract dissolved oxygen from water via gills (highly vascularized lamellae); oxygen diffuses across a thin epithelial barrier into blood flowing in a countercurrent exchange system
- Gill surface area and oxygen extraction efficiency vary enormously; obligate ram ventilators (many sharks, tunas) must swim continuously to ventilate gills
- **Clinical implication**: gill disease rapidly becomes life-threatening; any compromise to lamellar surface area (parasites, bacterial gill disease, ammonia burns) directly reduces oxygen uptake

### Osmoregulation

| Parameter | Freshwater Fish | Marine Teleosts | Elasmobranchs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Body fluid tonicity relative to environment | Hypertonic | Hypotonic | Slightly hypertonic (isosmotic) |
| Primary challenge | Water influx, ion loss | Water loss, ion gain | Urea and TMAO retention |
| Kidney function | Dilute, copious urine | Concentrated, scant urine | Moderate urine; urea reabsorbed |
| Gill chloride cells | Active ion uptake (Na+, Cl-) | Active ion excretion (Na+, Cl-) | Active NaCl excretion via rectal gland |

### Immune System

- Fish possess both innate and adaptive immunity, but adaptive responses are slower and less robust than in mammals
- Temperature-dependent immune function (ectotherms): immunosuppression at suboptimal temperatures is a major factor in disease outbreaks
- Mucus layer on skin and gills is a critical first-line defense (contains lysozyme, immunoglobulins, complement, antimicrobial peptides)
- Lymphoid organs: thymus, head kidney (anterior kidney; equivalent to mammalian bone marrow), spleen

## Water Quality

Water quality is the single most important determinant of fish health. The aphorism "treat the water, not the fish" reflects the reality that the majority of clinical disease in captive fish is secondary to environmental stressors.

### Critical Parameters

| Parameter | Freshwater | Marine | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Species-specific | Species-specific | Most critical parameter; affects metabolism, oxygen solubility, immune function |
| Dissolved oxygen | > 6 mg/L | > 6 mg/L | Minimum; many species require higher |
| Ammonia (NH3, unionized) | < 0.02 mg/L | < 0.02 mg/L | Highly toxic; gill damage, neurological signs; pH- and temperature-dependent (more toxic at higher pH) |
| Nitrite (NO2-) | < 0.1 mg/L | < 0.1 mg/L (less toxic in SW) | Causes methemoglobinemia ("brown blood disease"); chloride ions are protective |
| Nitrate (NO3-) | < 50 mg/L | < 20 mg/L | Chronic exposure causes immunosuppression |
| pH | 6.5–8.5 (species-specific) | 8.0–8.4 | Rapid pH shifts are more dangerous than suboptimal stable pH |
| Salinity | 0–5 ppt (FW) | 30–35 ppt (SW) | Critical for osmoregulation; salt baths used therapeutically in FW fish |
| Alkalinity (KH) | 50–200 mg/L CaCO3 | 125–200 mg/L CaCO3 | Buffers against pH swings |
| Chlorine/chloramine | 0 mg/L | 0 mg/L | Lethal at trace concentrations; dechlorinate all water |

### Nitrogen Cycle

Understanding the biological nitrogen cycle is essential for maintaining water quality in closed aquatic systems:

1. Fish waste and uneaten food produce **ammonia** (NH3/NH4+)
2. *Nitrosomonas* bacteria oxidize ammonia to **nitrite** (NO2-)
3. *Nitrobacter* and *Nitrospira* bacteria oxidize nitrite to **nitrate** (NO3-)
4. Nitrate is removed by water changes or denitrification

**New tank syndrome**: A common cause of acute fish mortality; occurs when fish are placed in a system before the biological filter is established. Ammonia and nitrite spike to lethal levels.

## Clinical Examination

### Environmental Assessment (Always First)

- Complete water quality panel (ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, pH, temperature, dissolved oxygen, salinity)
- System history: recent changes, new fish introductions, feeding changes, equipment failures, chemical treatments
- Population-level observations: mortality rate, percentage affected, species affected, behavioral changes

### Physical Examination in Water

- Observe fish in the water before capture: swimming pattern, buoyancy, respiratory rate (opercular movements), fin position, body condition, skin lesions, color changes, behavioral abnormalities
- Normal opercular rate varies by species and temperature (40–80 beats/min for many temperate species)

### Physical Examination Out of Water

- Minimize time out of water; keep gills moist with a spray of system water
- Small fish: examine in a shallow dish of system water over a light source
- Large fish: wet examination table; cover eyes with a damp cloth to reduce stress
- Systematic approach: skin and scales, fins, eyes, operculum (lift to visualize gills), oral cavity, vent, body condition (palpation of coelom)

### Skin Scrape and Gill Biopsy

- **Skin scrape**: gently scrape a microscope slide or coverslip from cranial to caudal along the flank; mount in a drop of system water for wet-mount microscopy
- **Gill biopsy**: snip a small section of gill filament with fine scissors; mount in system water. Examine for parasites (monogeneans, trichodinids, *Ichthyophthirius*), epithelial hyperplasia, lamellar fusion, mucus excess
- **Fin clip**: for bacterial/fungal culture, PCR, or histopathology

## Anesthesia

### Tricaine Methanesulfonate (MS-222)

The most widely used fish anesthetic; approved for use in food fish in many jurisdictions with a 21-day withdrawal period.

| Species Group | Induction Dose | Maintenance Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freshwater teleosts | 50–150 mg/L | 25–75 mg/L | Buffer with NaHCO3 to pH 7.0–7.5 |
| Marine teleosts | 75–200 mg/L | 50–100 mg/L | Higher doses often needed |
| Elasmobranchs | 50–100 mg/L | 25–50 mg/L | More sensitive; use lower range |
| Koi and goldfish | 100–150 mg/L | 50–75 mg/L | Relatively resistant |

**Stages of anesthesia** (adapted from Summerfelt and Smith):

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| I – Light sedation | Decreased reactivity; slight loss of equilibrium |
| II – Deep sedation | Total loss of equilibrium; reduced opercular rate |
| III – Surgical anesthesia | No response to stimuli; opercular movements slow and regular |
| IV – Medullary collapse | Cessation of opercular movements; death imminent if not reversed |

**Recovery**: Transfer to well-oxygenated, anesthetic-free water; gently move fish back and forth to irrigate gills until normal swimming resumes.

### Eugenol (Clove Oil) and AQUI-S (Isoeugenol)

- Eugenol: 40–100 mg/L for induction in teleosts; create stock solution in ethanol (1:10) before adding to water (eugenol is poorly water-soluble)
- AQUI-S (isoeugenol): 17–40 mg/L for teleosts; marketed as a fish anesthetic in some countries
- Smooth induction; longer recovery time than MS-222
- **Not recommended for elasmobranchs** due to unpredictable responses

### Alfaxalone

- 2–5 mg/kg IV or IM for teleosts; 1–3 mg/kg IV for elasmobranchs
- Useful for injectable anesthesia when immersion is impractical (very large fish, field conditions)
- Can be administered intravascularly via caudal vein

### Monitoring During Anesthesia

- **Opercular rate**: primary indicator; should be slow, regular, and present throughout
- **Response to stimuli**: tail pinch or corneal reflex to assess depth
- **Continuous gill irrigation**: for prolonged procedures, pump anesthetic-containing water over the gills via a recirculating system or syringe
- **Pulse oximetry**: experimental; limited validated equipment for fish

## Common Diseases of Teleosts

### Ichthyophthirius multifiliis ("Ich" / White Spot Disease)

- **Etiology**: Ciliated protozoan; obligate parasite with direct life cycle (trophont in skin → tomont in environment → theront infective stage)
- **Clinical signs**: White nodules (0.5–1 mm) on skin, fins, and gills; "flashing" (rubbing against surfaces); increased mucus production; respiratory distress; mortality if untreated
- **Diagnosis**: Wet mount of skin scrape showing large (up to 1 mm) ciliated trophonts with characteristic horseshoe-shaped macronucleus
- **Treatment**: Only the free-swimming theront stage is susceptible to treatment
  - **Salt**: 2–5 g/L indefinitely (freshwater species); disrupts theront osmotic balance
  - **Formalin**: 25 mg/L (25 ppm) continuous or 150–250 mg/L for 30–60 minute bath; repeat every other day for 3 treatments. **Caution**: reduces dissolved oxygen; increase aeration
  - **Malachite green**: 0.05–0.1 mg/L continuous; effective but banned for food fish due to carcinogenicity
  - **Heat**: Raising temperature to 28–30°C accelerates the life cycle and shortens the treatment period (not suitable for cold-water species)

### Columnaris Disease

- **Etiology**: *Flavobacterium columnare*; gram-negative rod; ubiquitous in freshwater environments
- **Clinical signs**: Yellow-white plaques on skin, fins, and gills ("saddleback" lesions on dorsum); fin erosion; gill necrosis; acute mortality in severe cases
- **Diagnosis**: Wet mount showing long, thin, flexing rod-shaped bacteria in "haystack" columns; culture on selective media (Hsu-Shotts or cytophaga agar)
- **Treatment**: Potassium permanganate (2 mg/L continuous), oxytetracycline in feed (50–75 mg/kg/day for 10 days), or florfenicol in feed (10 mg/kg/day for 10 days); reduce stocking density and improve water quality

### Mycobacteriosis

- **Etiology**: *Mycobacterium marinum*, *M. fortuitum*, *M. chelonae*, and others; slow-growing acid-fast bacteria
- **Clinical signs**: Chronic wasting, scale loss, skin ulceration, granulomas in viscera (spleen, kidney, liver), spinal deformities, exophthalmia
- **Diagnosis**: Acid-fast staining of impression smears or histopathology; PCR; culture (slow, 2–8 weeks)
- **Treatment**: Generally not attempted in individual pet or wild fish. In valuable aquarium specimens: combination therapy with rifampin, ethambutol, and clarithromycin (extrapolated from human M. marinum protocols); response is variable and prolonged
- **Zoonotic risk**: *M. marinum* causes "fish tank granuloma" in humans; gloves mandatory when handling suspect fish or contaminated water

### Aeromonas and Pseudomonas Infections

- Ubiquitous gram-negative opportunists causing hemorrhagic septicemia, ulceration, fin rot, and dropsy (ascites with scale protrusion)
- Typically secondary to stress, poor water quality, or concurrent disease
- Treatment: improve water quality; systemic antibiotics guided by culture and sensitivity (enrofloxacin 5–10 mg/kg IM or PO SID; florfenicol 10 mg/kg PO SID for 10 days)

### Saprolegniasis (Water Mold)

- **Etiology**: *Saprolegnia* spp. and related oomycetes (not true fungi)
- **Clinical signs**: Cotton-like white to gray growths on skin, fins, gills, and eggs
- **Treatment**: Salt bath (15–30 g/L for 5–30 minutes), formalin bath, or hydrogen peroxide (250–500 mg/L for 15–30 minutes)

## Elasmobranch Medicine

### Unique Physiology

#### Skeleton and Integument

- Cartilaginous skeleton (no true bone); complicates fracture repair and radiographic interpretation
- Skin covered with dermal denticles (placoid scales); extremely abrasive; can cause glove and equipment damage
- Skin is thicker and less permeable than teleost skin; topical drug absorption is limited

#### Urea Retention and Osmoregulation

- Elasmobranchs maintain plasma osmolality near or slightly above seawater by retaining urea (300–400 mmol/L) and trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO, ~75 mmol/L)
- **Clinical significance**: blood chemistry reference ranges differ fundamentally from teleosts; BUN values of 800–1200 mg/dL are normal. Standard mammalian or teleost reference ranges are inapplicable.
- TMAO counteracts the protein-denaturing effects of urea
- If elasmobranchs are placed in freshwater or significantly hyposaline water, osmotic water influx and urea loss cause rapid physiological collapse

#### Electrosensory System (Ampullae of Lorenzini)

- Gel-filled pores on the head and snout detect weak electric fields; used for prey detection and navigation
- **Clinical relevance**: avoid electrical equipment (electrocautery, certain monitoring devices) near the head during procedures; may cause distress or interfere with recovery behavior

#### Spiral Valve Intestine

- Instead of a long, coiled intestine, elasmobranchs have a short intestine with an internal spiral valve that increases surface area
- Drug transit time is relatively short; oral medication may pass through rapidly with reduced absorption
- Rectal prolapse of the spiral valve is an occasionally encountered emergency

### Elasmobranch Handling and Restraint

- **Sharks**: Manual restraint in water with one handler at the head (controlling direction) and one supporting the body/tail; tonic immobility (TI) can be induced in some species by inverting the animal (dorsal recumbency) — useful for brief procedures but monitor respiratory function
- **Rays**: Restrain flat on a padded, wet surface; tail spine must be controlled or covered in venomous species (stingrays); batoids can be wrapped in damp towels with the disc edges folded dorsally
- **Avoid**: removing large elasmobranchs from water for extended periods; visceral organ compression under their own weight causes organ damage

### Elasmobranch Anesthesia and Sedation

| Agent | Dose/Method | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| MS-222 | 50–100 mg/L immersion (buffered) | Widely used; slower induction than teleosts; monitor closely |
| Alfaxalone | 1–3 mg/kg IV (caudal vein) | Smooth induction; preferred injectable in many facilities |
| Propofol | 1–3 mg/kg IV | Rapid onset; short duration; useful for brief procedures |
| Ketamine | 1–2 mg/kg IM or IV | Variable efficacy; often combined with midazolam (0.1–0.2 mg/kg) |
| Midazolam | 0.1–0.5 mg/kg IM | Sedation; useful premedication; can be reversed with flumazenil |

**Ventilation during anesthesia**: Continuous ram ventilation or active gill irrigation is essential. Use a submersible pump to direct oxygenated water (with or without maintenance anesthetic) into the mouth and over the gills.

### Elasmobranch Surgery

- **Wound closure**: Use monofilament absorbable suture (PDS, Monocryl); elasmobranch skin is tough but heals well; denticles must be penetrated with cutting needles
- **Coelomic surgery**: Midline ventral approach for gonad removal, foreign body retrieval, or transmitter implantation; waterproof surgical drapes; minimize air exposure to coelomic organs
- **Common surgical indications**: Hook removal (ingested or embedded), transmitter/tag implantation, gonadectomy in display animals, abscess drainage
- **Wound healing**: Elasmobranchs generally exhibit excellent wound healing capacity; large sharks can heal substantial bite wounds within weeks

### Common Elasmobranch Diseases

| Condition | Etiology | Clinical Signs | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goiter | Iodine deficiency (common in closed-system aquaria) | Bilateral ventral pharyngeal swelling | Iodine supplementation (KI in water or food); 0.5–1 mg/kg in food |
| Skin ulceration/dermatitis | *Vibrio* spp., *Aeromonas*, mechanical trauma | Focal to diffuse ulcers, erythema | Debridement; topical or systemic antibiotics; improve water quality |
| Spirorchid trematodes | Digenean trematodes in vasculature | Chronic debilitation; thrombosis; vascular lesions at necropsy | Praziquantel 5–10 mg/kg PO; limited efficacy |
| Monogenean gill parasites | *Dermophthirius* spp. and others | Increased respiratory rate; gill pallor; rubbing | Praziquantel bath (2–10 mg/L for 3–6 hours); freshwater dip for marine species (2–5 minutes) |
| Urolith/cloacal calculi | Calcium-based concretions | Straining; cloacal distension | Manual extraction; surgical removal if large |
| Steatitis | Vitamin E deficiency from rancid frozen fish | Subcutaneous nodules; lethargy; anorexia | Vitamin E supplementation (100 IU/kg in food); source fresh-quality fish |

## Diagnostic Techniques in Fish

### Hematology

- Blood collection sites: caudal vein (most fish and elasmobranchs), dorsal aorta (salmonids), cardiac puncture (small fish, terminal)
- Nucleated red blood cells in fish; automated hematology analyzers designed for mammals are unreliable; manual counts required
- Elasmobranch blood clots rapidly due to efficient coagulation; use heparinized syringes and process samples immediately
- PCV normal ranges: teleosts 20–45%; elasmobranchs 15–30%

### Imaging

- **Radiography**: Useful for skeletal abnormalities, swim bladder evaluation, foreign bodies, egg retention; cartilaginous skeleton of elasmobranchs is less radiopaque than bone
- **Ultrasound**: Excellent for coelomic evaluation, reproductive assessment, cardiac imaging; can be performed in water using a waterproof probe or through a thin plastic bag
- **CT/MRI**: Available in advanced facilities for complex cases; requires prolonged anesthesia with gill irrigation

## Euthanasia

- **Preferred**: MS-222 overdose (>250 mg/L, buffered) until cessation of opercular movements, followed by physical method (pithing, decapitation, or exsanguination) to confirm death
- **Alternative**: Alfaxalone overdose IV; pentobarbital injection (intracardiac or IV)
- **Elasmobranchs**: MS-222 overdose or pentobarbital IV (caudal vein); ensure complete cessation of gill movement and cardiac activity before confirming death

## Biosecurity and Quarantine

- Quarantine all new fish for a minimum of 30–60 days in a separate system
- Prophylactic treatments during quarantine: praziquantel (2 mg/L bath for 24 hours, repeated weekly for 3 weeks) for monogeneans and cestodes; freshwater or saltwater dips for external parasites
- Disinfect nets, siphons, and equipment between systems (Virkon-S, dilute bleach, or complete drying)
- Prevent cross-contamination of water between systems; dedicated equipment for each system
- Screen for *Mycobacterium* spp. in facilities with a history of mycobacteriosis (acid-fast staining of splenic tissue from mortalities)
