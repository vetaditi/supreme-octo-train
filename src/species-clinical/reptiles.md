# Reptiles

## Overview

Reptiles present unique clinical challenges rooted in their ectothermic physiology, diverse anatomy, and the profound influence of environmental conditions on every aspect of their biology—from drug metabolism to immune function. This chapter covers chelonians (sea turtles, freshwater turtles, and tortoises), crocodilians, and squamates (snakes and lizards), with emphasis on physiological principles that directly affect clinical management.

## Ectotherm Physiology: Clinical Implications

Understanding ectothermy is fundamental to reptile medicine. Environmental temperature directly controls:

### Metabolic Rate and Drug Metabolism

- Metabolic rate approximately doubles for every 10°C increase in temperature within the species' tolerated range
- Drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion are all temperature-dependent
- **Clinical rule**: Always maintain reptile patients within their preferred optimal temperature zone (POTZ) during treatment. Drugs administered to hypothermic reptiles will have delayed onset, prolonged duration, and unpredictable effects.

| Reptile Group | Typical POTZ (°C) |
|---|---|
| Temperate freshwater turtles | 22–28 |
| Tropical tortoises | 26–32 |
| Sea turtles | 24–30 |
| Crocodilians | 28–33 |
| Temperate snakes | 24–30 |
| Tropical snakes | 27–32 |
| Desert lizards | 30–38 |
| Tropical lizards | 26–32 |

### Immune Function

- Reptilian immune responses are significantly impaired at temperatures below the POTZ
- Wound healing, white blood cell function, and antibody production all decline with hypothermia
- Seasonal immunosuppression during brumation (winter dormancy) may predispose to infection
- Leukocyte differentials differ from mammals: heterophils (equivalent to neutrophils), lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils, and azurophils (in some species)

### Cardiovascular Considerations

- Three-chambered heart in most reptiles (two atria, one ventricle with incomplete septation); crocodilians have a four-chambered heart with a unique foramen of Panizza allowing intracardiac shunting
- Intracardiac shunting is physiologically normal and regulated; right-to-left shunting increases during diving and breath-holding
- Renal portal system: blood from the caudal body can pass through the kidneys before returning to the heart. Drugs injected in the hindlimbs may undergo first-pass renal metabolism or excretion. **Inject medications in the forelimbs when possible.**

## Chelonians

### Sea Turtles

#### Species

Seven species worldwide: green (*Chelonia mydas*), loggerhead (*Caretta caretta*), hawksbill (*Eretmochelys imbricata*), Kemp's ridley (*Lepidochelys kempii*), olive ridley (*Lepidochelys olivacea*), leatherback (*Dermochelys coriacea*), and flatback (*Natator depressus*). All are listed under CITES Appendix I and national endangered species legislation.

#### Key Anatomical Features

- Shell (carapace dorsally, plastron ventrally) is fused to the vertebral column and ribs; the shell cannot be separated from the animal
- Leatherback turtles lack a bony shell; instead have a flexible, leathery carapace with embedded osteoderms
- Salt glands (lacrimal) excrete excess sodium chloride; produces copious "tears" that are often mistaken for distress
- Lungs are dorsal; in stranded turtles, ensure dorsal side remains up for optimal ventilation
- No diaphragm; ventilation is accomplished by limb and body wall movements

#### Fibropapillomatosis (FP)

The most significant disease of sea turtles globally, particularly green turtles.

- **Etiology**: Associated with Chelonid herpesvirus 5 (ChHV5); environmental cofactors (eutrophication, biotoxins, immunosuppression) likely influence expression
- **Clinical presentation**: Single to hundreds of external fibroepithelial tumors on skin, eyes, flippers, and oral cavity. Internal tumors (lungs, kidneys, heart) occur in advanced cases. Tumors can obstruct vision, feeding, and locomotion.
- **Diagnosis**: Visual identification of characteristic tumors; histopathology confirms fibropapilloma; PCR for ChHV5
- **Treatment**: Surgical excision (CO2 laser preferred for precision and hemostasis); electrochemotherapy under investigation. Recurrence is common. Small tumors may regress spontaneously.
- **Epidemiology**: Prevalence correlates with degraded coastal habitats; nearshore juvenile foraging populations most affected. Prevalence has reached 50–70% in some Florida and Hawaiian green turtle populations.

#### Cold Stunning

- Occurs when water temperature drops below approximately 10°C; sea turtles become hypothermic, lethargic, and unable to swim or feed
- Mass cold-stunning events occur annually in shallow embayments (e.g., Cape Cod Bay, Laguna Madre)
- Triage and treatment: gradual rewarming (increase body temperature no more than 2–3°C per day to avoid reperfusion injury), fluid therapy, blood work (assess organ damage, electrolytes), radiographs (pneumonia assessment)
- Complications: pneumonia, myocardial damage, renal failure, immune suppression, and secondary infections

#### Additional Common Conditions

- **Boat strike injuries**: Propeller and hull impact cause shell fractures; may penetrate coelomic cavity
- **Entanglement**: Fishing line, nets, marine debris cause flipper constriction, ingestion, and drowning
- **Hook ingestion**: Fishing hooks in esophagus or stomach; endoscopic or surgical removal
- **Oil contamination**: Affects skin, eyes, respiratory tract, and GI tract

### Freshwater Turtles

#### Common Clinical Presentations

- **Vehicle trauma (road mortality)**: One of the most common presentations during nesting season when females cross roads. Shell fractures range from minor cracks to catastrophic crushing injuries.
- **Dog bite injuries**: Shell fractures, soft tissue trauma, and infection
- **Upper respiratory disease**: *Mycoplasma* spp. in tortoises and freshwater turtles; nasal discharge, conjunctivitis, lethargy
- **Parasitism**: Leeches, shell algae and barnacles (more cosmetic than pathological in most cases), internal parasites (nematodes, trematodes)
- **Ranavirus**: Causes systemic hemorrhagic disease in some freshwater turtle populations; also affects amphibians and fish

### Shell Repair Techniques

Shell fractures are among the most common reasons for chelonian admission to rehabilitation. Repair techniques include:

#### Assessment

- Radiograph to evaluate fracture extent, fragment displacement, and coelomic involvement
- Evaluate for coelomic membrane compromise: fluid leakage, exposed viscera, contamination
- Assess neurological function if spinal cord may be involved (tail tone, hindlimb reflexes, cloacal tone)

#### Repair Methods

| Method | Indication | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Epoxy/fiberglass patches** | Stable, non-displaced fractures with intact coelomic membrane | Clean wound margins, apply fiberglass mesh with epoxy. Leave drainage holes. Low-cost, effective. |
| **Bridge plates and screws** | Displaced fractures requiring reduction and rigid fixation | Orthopedic screws and plates bridging fracture lines; technique borrowed from orthopedic surgery |
| **Wire cerclage** | Moderate displacement; can be used with epoxy | Drill holes on either side of fracture; wire together, then overlay with epoxy |
| **Zip ties** | Field expedient stabilization | Drill holes; secure with zip ties. Temporary measure for transport to treatment facility |
| **Wound management only** | Minor cracks without displacement | Clean, protect with tegaderm or similar dressing; monitor for healing |

#### Healing Timeline

- Shell fracture healing is slow: 6–18 months or longer depending on fracture severity, species, and environmental conditions
- Maintain patients at POTZ to optimize healing
- Monitor for infection at fracture site; systemic antibiotics if contaminated or open fracture
- Radiographic follow-up to assess healing progression

### Tortoises

#### Specific Concerns

- **Upper Respiratory Tract Disease (URTD)**: *Mycoplasma agassizii* and *M. testudineum* cause chronic respiratory disease in desert tortoises and gopher tortoises. Significant conservation concern; diseased animals may be restricted from translocation.
- **Metabolic bone disease (MBD)**: Calcium deficiency, vitamin D3 deficiency, or phosphorus excess cause softening and deformity of shell and bones. Common in captive tortoises with inadequate UV-B exposure and improper diet.
- **Herpesvirus**: Testudinid herpesvirus causes stomatitis, rhinitis, and mortality; particular concern in Mediterranean tortoises.
- **Dystocia (egg binding)**: Obstructive and non-obstructive forms; may require medical management (oxytocin, calcium) or surgical intervention (coeliotomy through prefemoral fossa or plastron osteotomy).

## Crocodilians

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Four-chambered heart with foramen of Panizza (connection between left and right aortic arches); allows physiological shunting during diving
- Extremely powerful jaws and tail; a 3-meter crocodilian can exert over 3,000 psi bite force
- Armored skin with osteoderms (bony plates within the dermis); makes injection and venipuncture challenging
- Ectothermic with behavioral thermoregulation; bask to raise body temperature
- Remarkably robust immune system; heal severe wounds in contaminated environments with rare secondary infection. Crocodilian serum has demonstrated broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity in vitro.
- Cerebral cortex more developed than in other reptiles; capable of learning, social behavior, and tool use

### Common Diseases and Clinical Concerns

- **West Nile virus**: Farmed alligators experienced significant mortality from WNV; wild crocodilian susceptibility varies
- **Mycoplasmosis**: Upper respiratory disease in captive and wild crocodilians
- **Chlamydiosis**: *Chlamydia* spp. cause hepatitis and pneumonia
- **Parasitism**: Pentastomids (respiratory parasites), nematodes, trematodes, coccidia
- **Human-wildlife conflict**: Nuisance animal removals, bite injuries to humans, vehicle strikes

### Handling Considerations

- **Extreme danger**: Crocodilians of any size can inflict serious injury. Jaws must be secured immediately upon capture.
- Capture methods: snare poles, harpoon-style capture devices, box traps, spotlight/hand capture for small individuals
- Jaw restraint: electrical tape or rope around the jaws; closing muscles are extremely powerful but opening muscles are relatively weak—a strong rubber band can hold the jaws of a small crocodilian closed
- Chemical immobilization rarely used in the field; injectable anesthetics are unpredictable in crocodilians due to cardiovascular shunting
- Venipuncture: supravertebral sinus (occipital sinus) is the standard site; ventral coccygeal vein is an alternative

## Squamates

### Snakes

#### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Single functional lung (right) in most species; left lung is vestigial or absent. Implications for respiratory disease and positioning.
- No limbs, eyelids, or external ear openings
- Spectacle (brille): fused transparent eyelid covering the eye; subspectacular abscess can occur. Retained spectacle during ecdysis (shedding) is a common problem.
- Jacobson's organ (vomeronasal organ): chemosensory organ in the palate accessed by tongue flicking
- Highly distensible skull and body wall for prey ingestion
- Venomous species: numerous families with diverse venom compositions. Envenomation of handlers is a serious occupational hazard.

#### Common Clinical Presentations

- **Respiratory infections**: Bacterial, viral, and fungal pneumonia. Presenting signs: open-mouth breathing, mucus in oral cavity, abnormal respiratory sounds, postural changes (head elevation). *Nidovirus* (ball pythons), paramyxovirus, and *Ophidiomyces ophiodiicola* (Snake Fungal Disease/ophidiomycosis) are significant pathogens.
- **Snake Fungal Disease (SFD/ophidiomycosis)**: *Ophidiomyces ophiodiicola*; causes facial swelling, crusting skin lesions, ulceration, and systemic granulomatous disease. Emerging disease of conservation concern in North American snake populations (timber rattlesnakes, eastern massasaugas, and others).
- **Inclusion Body Disease (IBD)**: Arenavirus infection in boid snakes (boas and pythons); causes neurological signs (stargazing, loss of righting reflex, regurgitation), lymphoproliferative disease. No treatment; diagnosis confirmed by identification of intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies in lymphocytes or liver biopsy.
- **Dysecdysis**: Abnormal shedding; retained spectacles, retained skin. Usually indicates suboptimal humidity, dehydration, ectoparasitism, or systemic illness.
- **Stomatitis (mouth rot)**: Bacterial infection of oral mucosa; petechiae progressing to caseous exudate and bone involvement. Husbandry correction, wound care, and systemic antibiotics.

#### Handling Venomous Snakes

- Specialized training required; never handle venomous snakes alone
- Snake hooks, tubes, and tongs for initial capture and restraint
- Transparent tubes for examination: snake is guided headfirst into a clear tube; once the head is past the midpoint, the body can be held
- Antivenom availability and emergency medical protocols must be established before working with venomous species
- Chemical immobilization: isoflurane or sevoflurane via face mask or induction chamber

### Lizards

#### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Diverse body plans: from limbless (glass lizards, legless geckos) to robust-bodied (monitors, iguanas) to dorsoventrally flattened (horned lizards)
- Autotomy: Many species can voluntarily shed their tail as a predator escape mechanism. The tail regenerates but the replacement is cartilaginous, not bony, and differs in scale pattern. Handling by the tail can trigger autotomy.
- Femoral pores: Waxy secretions from femoral or precloacal pores in some species; used for scent marking and sex identification
- Hemipenes (males): Paired copulatory organs in cloaca; prolapse is a clinical condition requiring reduction or amputation
- Third eye (parietal eye): Photosensory organ on the dorsal head in many species; involved in thermoregulatory behavior and circadian rhythms

#### Common Clinical Presentations

- **Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)**: The most common nutritional disease in captive lizards. Caused by inadequate UV-B exposure (required for cutaneous vitamin D3 synthesis), dietary calcium deficiency, or excessive dietary phosphorus. Clinical signs: soft or deformed bones, pathological fractures, muscle tremors (hypocalcemic tetany), inability to stand or climb. Treatment: calcium supplementation, UV-B provision, dietary correction. Prevention through proper husbandry.
- **Respiratory infections**: Nasal discharge, open-mouth breathing, lethargy. Bacterial cultures and sensitivity testing guide antibiotic therapy. Environmental temperature and humidity optimization is essential for recovery.
- **Parasitism**: Coccidia, nematodes (especially pinworms in herbivorous lizards), mites (*Ophionyssus* spp.). Heavy mite infestations cause anemia, dysecdysis, and stress.
- **Thermal burns**: Contact with unguarded heat sources (heat lamps, heat rocks) causes full-thickness burns. Reptiles may not move away from excessive heat until severe damage occurs due to impaired thermal nociception.
- **Adenovirus**: Causes hepatitis in bearded dragons and other agamids; immunosuppression and co-infections are common sequelae.

## General Principles for Reptile Cases

### Anesthesia Considerations

- Pre-anesthetic fasting: 24–48 hours for most species (longer for large snakes after a meal)
- Induction: isoflurane or sevoflurane via face mask, induction chamber, or intubation; propofol IV; alfaxalone IV or IM
- Intubation is facilitated by the glottis being visible at the base of the tongue in most species
- Ventilate manually: most reptiles do not breathe spontaneously under general anesthesia. Ventilation rate of 2–6 breaths per minute is typical.
- Recovery is prolonged compared to mammals and birds due to temperature-dependent metabolism and breath-holding ability; may take hours
- Monitor heart rate with Doppler flow detector or ECG; pulse oximetry is unreliable in many reptiles

### Fluid Therapy

- Maintenance fluid requirements: 10–30 mL/kg/day (lower in desert species, higher in aquatic species)
- Routes: oral (if GI function is normal), subcutaneous (in lateral body wall), intravenous (jugular, cephalic), intraosseous (tibia, femur), intracoelomic (caution: avoid organs)
- Crystalloid fluids (LRS, 0.9% NaCl) are standard; warmed to patient's POTZ

### Euthanasia Considerations

- Reptiles have prolonged survival of neural tissue after cardiac arrest; cessation of heartbeat does not confirm death
- Two-step euthanasia recommended: general anesthesia followed by intracardiac or IV injection of pentobarbital
- Physical methods (decapitation, pithing) alone are not acceptable as the sole euthanasia method due to prolonged neural activity
- Cold (freezing) is not an acceptable euthanasia method for reptiles despite historical use
