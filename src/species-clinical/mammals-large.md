# Large Mammals

## Overview

Large mammals represent some of the most challenging and rewarding patients in wildlife veterinary medicine. Their size demands specialized equipment, trained personnel, and careful logistical planning. This chapter covers ungulates, large carnivores, elephants, and rhinoceroses—groups that are frequently encountered in field research, translocation projects, and rehabilitation contexts.

## Ungulates

### Cervids (Deer, Elk, Moose)

#### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Ruminant digestive system with four-compartment stomach (rumen, reticulum, omasum, abomasum)
- Seasonal antler growth cycle regulated by photoperiod and testosterone
- Highly developed flight response with powerful hindlimb musculature
- Relatively fragile long bones compared to domestic ruminants of similar size
- Specialized nasal and sinus anatomy; some species (elk, red deer) have prominent bugling structures

#### Baseline Vital Parameters

| Parameter | White-tailed Deer | Elk | Moose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heart rate (bpm) | 60–120 | 40–80 | 40–70 |
| Respiratory rate (breaths/min) | 15–30 | 10–25 | 10–20 |
| Temperature (°C) | 38.5–39.5 | 38.0–39.5 | 37.5–39.0 |
| Approximate body mass (kg) | 40–140 | 225–450 | 350–700 |

#### Common Diseases

- **Chronic Wasting Disease (CWD)**: Transmissible spongiform encephalopathy caused by prion proteins. Progressive neurological disease with weight loss, behavioral changes, excessive salivation, and death. No treatment; diagnosis requires post-mortem examination of brainstem and retropharyngeal lymph node tissue. Significant regulatory and population management implications.
- **Epizootic Hemorrhagic Disease (EHD) / Bluetongue**: Orbivirus infections transmitted by *Culicoides* midges. Cause hemorrhagic fever with edema, mucosal ulceration, and hoof sloughing. Seasonal occurrence correlates with vector activity. Mortality varies by species and viral strain.
- **Meningeal Worm (*Parelaphostrongylus tenuis*)**: Nematode parasite with white-tailed deer as definitive host. Aberrant migration in moose, elk, and other cervids causes severe neurological disease. Snail and slug intermediate hosts.
- **Bacterial pneumonia**: Often secondary to stress, viral infection, or parasitism. *Mannheimia*, *Pasteurella*, and *Fusobacterium* species commonly implicated.

#### Handling Considerations

- Extremely susceptible to capture myopathy; minimize chase time and handling duration
- Chemical immobilization preferred; physical restraint of conscious large cervids is dangerous
- Antler injuries to personnel are a serious risk during velvet and hard antler seasons
- Blindfold animals immediately upon capture to reduce stress
- Monitor rectal temperature continuously; hyperthermia above 41°C requires active cooling

### Bovids (Wild Sheep, Goats, Antelope, Bison)

#### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Ruminant physiology similar to cervids but with permanent horns (keratin sheath over bony core) rather than deciduous antlers
- Many species adapted to extreme environments (mountain goats, desert antelope) with specialized thermoregulatory and renal physiology
- Bison possess massive muscular anterior body construction; extremely powerful and dangerous
- Wild sheep and goats have specialized hoof anatomy for steep terrain

#### Common Clinical Presentations

- **Respiratory disease complex**: *Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae* causes fatal pneumonia in bighorn sheep populations; domestic sheep contact is a primary risk factor
- **Contagious ecthyma (orf)**: Parapoxvirus causing proliferative lesions on lips and muzzle; zoonotic
- **Parasitic gastroenteritis**: *Haemonchus*, *Ostertagia*, and other GI nematodes significant in many wild bovid species
- **Copper toxicosis**: Some species (particularly exotic bovids in captive settings) highly susceptible to copper accumulation
- **Malignant catarrhal fever (MCF)**: Gamma-herpesvirus; wildebeest-associated form (AlHV-1) and sheep-associated form (OvHV-2). Fatal in many susceptible bovid species.

#### Handling Considerations

- Bison require heavy-duty facilities; standard cattle chutes are often inadequate
- Horn injuries pose significant risk to handlers
- Mountain sheep and goats: net-gun capture from helicopter is common; altitude and terrain add logistical complexity
- Domestic livestock disease transmission is a critical biosecurity concern during handling

## Large Carnivores

### Felids (Lions, Tigers, Leopards, Cougars, etc.)

#### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Obligate carnivores with short, simple gastrointestinal tract
- Retractable claws (except cheetah) present significant handler risk
- Powerful jaw musculature; bite force capable of crushing bone
- Highly developed senses (vision, hearing) create extreme stress response in captive settings
- Barbed tongue (filiform papillae) can abrade skin during prolonged contact

#### Baseline Vital Parameters

| Parameter | African Lion | Tiger | Cougar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heart rate (bpm) | 40–80 | 50–90 | 60–120 |
| Respiratory rate (breaths/min) | 10–25 | 10–20 | 15–30 |
| Temperature (°C) | 37.5–39.5 | 37.5–39.0 | 38.0–39.5 |
| Approximate body mass (kg) | 120–250 | 100–300 | 35–100 |

#### Common Diseases

- **Canine distemper virus (CDV)**: Increasingly recognized in wild felids; caused significant mortality in Serengeti lions (1994). Clinical signs include respiratory, gastrointestinal, and neurological disease.
- **Feline immunodeficiency virus (FIV)**: Species-specific strains; highly prevalent in some lion populations. Generally less pathogenic than in domestic cats but may increase susceptibility to secondary infections.
- **Tuberculosis (*Mycobacterium bovis*, *M. tuberculosis*)**: Significant concern in African wildlife (lions, buffalo interface); also in captive felids. Diagnosis challenging in live animals.
- **Sarcoptic mange**: *Sarcoptes scabiei* causes severe dermatitis and can be population-limiting in some felid species.

### Canids (Wolves, Wild Dogs, Foxes)

#### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Digitigrade stance; long limbs adapted for cursorial hunting
- Highly social species (wolves, African wild dogs) with complex pack dynamics affecting capture and management decisions
- Non-retractable claws; less laceration risk than felids but significant bite risk
- Relatively high metabolic rate for body size

#### Common Diseases

- **Rabies**: All canids are susceptible; some species (e.g., raccoon dog, certain fox species) serve as primary reservoir hosts in their geographic range. Always a differential for neurological signs in wild canids.
- **Canine distemper virus**: Major cause of mortality in African wild dogs, Ethiopian wolves, and other endangered canid populations.
- **Canine parvovirus**: Causes high mortality in pups; significant population-level impacts in endangered species.
- **Mange (*Sarcoptes scabiei*, *Demodex*)**: Sarcoptic mange can cause population-level declines; well documented in wolves, coyotes, and foxes.

### Ursids (Bears)

#### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Plantigrade stance; powerful forelimbs with non-retractable claws
- Omnivorous diet with seasonal hyperphagia; dramatic body condition variation through the year
- Hibernation physiology (temperate species): reduced heart rate (8–10 bpm), metabolic rate drops 50–75%, no urination or defecation for months, parturition occurs during hibernation
- Extremely dense muscle mass makes intramuscular injection challenging; subcutaneous fat layer varies seasonally

#### Baseline Vital Parameters (Active Season)

| Parameter | Black Bear | Grizzly/Brown Bear | Polar Bear |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heart rate (bpm) | 50–100 | 40–80 | 40–70 |
| Respiratory rate (breaths/min) | 10–25 | 8–20 | 8–18 |
| Temperature (°C) | 37.5–39.0 | 37.5–38.5 | 37.0–38.5 |
| Approximate body mass (kg) | 60–270 | 150–600 | 200–700 |

#### Common Diseases and Conditions

- **Trichinellosis**: Bears are reservoir hosts for *Trichinella* spp.; important zoonotic consideration when handling or sampling
- **Hepatozoonosis**: Tick-transmitted protozoal infection; often subclinical but can cause debilitation
- **Tooth and periodontal disease**: Common in older bears; may affect foraging ability
- **Human-wildlife conflict injuries**: Gunshot wounds, vehicle strikes, snare injuries frequently encountered

#### Handling Considerations

- Extreme physical strength requires appropriate chemical immobilization protocols
- Thick subcutaneous fat (especially pre-hibernation) may impede dart delivery of drugs
- Remote dart delivery from tree stands, culvert traps, or helicopter is standard
- Recovery monitoring is essential; bears may appear recovered but still be ataxic and at risk of falling from trees

## Elephants

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Largest terrestrial mammals; African savanna elephants reach 6,000 kg or more
- Highly specialized musculoskeletal system; column-like limbs support body weight
- Trunk contains over 40,000 muscles; serves respiratory, feeding, drinking, social, and manipulative functions
- Unique dental anatomy: horizontal tooth replacement with six sets of molars through life
- Thick, rugose skin (up to 2.5 cm) with sparse hair; thermoregulation via ear flapping, mud bathing, and behavioral means
- Highly developed brain; complex social structure and cognitive abilities

### Baseline Vital Parameters

| Parameter | African Elephant | Asian Elephant |
|---|---|---|
| Heart rate (bpm) | 25–35 | 25–40 |
| Respiratory rate (breaths/min) | 4–8 | 4–8 |
| Temperature (°C) | 35.5–37.0 | 35.5–36.5 |
| Approximate body mass (kg) | 3,000–6,000 | 2,000–5,000 |

### Common Diseases

- **Elephant endotheliotropic herpesvirus (EEHV)**: Causes fatal hemorrhagic disease, primarily in young Asian elephants. Rapid progression; early detection and aggressive antiviral therapy (famciclovir) may improve survival.
- **Tuberculosis (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis*)**: Significant concern in captive Asian elephants; respiratory transmission to humans documented. Trunk wash culture is the standard diagnostic method.
- **Foot disease**: Chronic foot infections, sole abscesses, and nail pathology are among the most common clinical problems, particularly in captive elephants on hard substrates.
- **Salmonellosis**: Elephants can be asymptomatic carriers of multiple *Salmonella* serovars; shedding increases with stress.

### Handling Considerations

- Field immobilization of free-ranging elephants requires experienced teams and potent opioids (etorphine, thiafentanil)
- Lateral recumbency must be managed carefully; prolonged recumbency causes respiratory compromise due to weight on dependent lung
- Positioning: sternal recumbency preferred when possible; prop with hay bales or sandbags
- Trunk must remain patent for breathing when animal is recumbent
- Reversal must be prompt and complete; standing recovery is safest

## Rhinoceroses

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Hindgut fermenters (unlike ruminant ungulates); simple stomach with large cecum and colon
- One or two horns composed of compacted keratin (no bony core); horn poaching is a critical conservation crisis
- Thick, folded skin with limited subcutaneous fat
- Poor eyesight but acute hearing and olfaction
- Relatively solitary except for mother-calf pairs (most species)

### Baseline Vital Parameters

| Parameter | White Rhinoceros | Black Rhinoceros |
|---|---|---|
| Heart rate (bpm) | 30–50 | 35–55 |
| Respiratory rate (breaths/min) | 8–15 | 10–18 |
| Temperature (°C) | 36.0–37.5 | 36.5–38.0 |
| Approximate body mass (kg) | 1,500–2,300 | 800–1,400 |

### Common Diseases and Clinical Concerns

- **Iron storage disease (hemosiderosis/hemochromatosis)**: Particularly problematic in black rhinoceroses and Sumatran rhinoceroses in captivity. Hepatic iron accumulation may contribute to mortality. Managed with dietary modification and phlebotomy.
- **Babesiosis (*Babesia bicornis*)**: Tick-borne protozoal disease causing hemolytic anemia in black rhinoceroses. Can be fatal; treatment with diminazene aceturide or imidocarb dipropionate.
- **Trypanosomosis**: Tsetse fly-transmitted infection in African species; may cause chronic debilitation.
- **Skin conditions**: Dermatophilosis, papillomatosis, and ectoparasite infestations are common.
- **Capture-related complications**: Rhinoceros immobilization carries high risk. Respiratory depression is the primary concern with potent opioids. Hyperthermia, renarcotization, and capture myopathy are additional risks.

### Handling and Conservation Considerations

- Chemical immobilization requires experienced teams; mortality rates during capture can be significant
- Etorphine is the primary immobilization agent; partial opioid agonists (butorphanol) used adjunctively to improve respiration
- Oxygen supplementation during recumbency is standard practice
- Anti-poaching efforts have driven significant advances in rhinoceros translocation, dehorning, and monitoring techniques
- All five rhinoceros species are threatened or critically endangered; every clinical interaction must consider conservation implications

## General Principles for Large Mammal Cases

### Pre-Capture Planning

- Assemble adequate personnel, equipment, drugs, and emergency supplies before beginning
- Establish communication protocols and emergency procedures
- Identify transport routes and receiving facilities if translocation is planned
- Assess weather conditions; avoid capture during extreme heat

### Monitoring During Immobilization

- Continuous monitoring of heart rate, respiratory rate, blood oxygen saturation (pulse oximetry), and rectal temperature
- Supplemental oxygen delivery when possible
- IV catheter placement for emergency drug administration
- Maintain airway patency; position head and neck appropriately for species

### Post-Capture Monitoring

- Extended observation period appropriate for species and drugs used
- Monitor for signs of capture myopathy in the days following capture (dark urine, stiffness, reluctance to move)
- Document all procedures, samples collected, and drugs administered

### Sample Collection Priorities

When handling large mammals, maximize the scientific and clinical value of each capture event by collecting:

- Blood (hematology, serum chemistry, serum banking, genetic analysis)
- Ectoparasites for identification and pathogen screening
- Feces for parasitology and microbiome studies
- Hair, skin, or tissue biopsies as indicated
- Morphometric measurements and body condition scoring
- Photographs for identification and clinical documentation
