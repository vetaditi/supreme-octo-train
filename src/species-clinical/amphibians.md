# Amphibians

## Overview

Amphibians (class Amphibia) encompass three extant orders: Anura (frogs and toads, ~7,500 species), Urodela/Caudata (salamanders and newts, ~800 species), and Gymnophiona (caecilians, ~220 species). They occupy a critical position in global biodiversity yet face unprecedented population declines driven by emerging infectious diseases, habitat loss, pollution, and climate change. Clinically, amphibians present unique challenges rooted in their permeable skin, biphasic life cycles, ectothermic physiology, and extreme sensitivity to environmental contaminants.

## Permeable Skin Physiology

The skin of amphibians is their most clinically significant organ. Understanding cutaneous physiology is essential for examination, drug delivery, and disease management.

### Key Functions

| Function | Clinical Relevance |
|---|---|
| Gas exchange (cutaneous respiration) | Up to 100% of O2 uptake in lungless salamanders (Plethodontidae); skin must remain moist |
| Water absorption | Amphibians do not drink; water is absorbed transcutaneously, primarily via the pelvic patch (ventral skin) in anurans |
| Electrolyte regulation | Active ion transport through the skin; highly sensitive to water chemistry |
| Toxin secretion | Granular (poison) glands produce alkaloids, peptides, and biogenic amines; handle with gloves |
| Immune defense | Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) secreted by skin glands constitute a critical innate immune barrier |
| Thermoregulation | Evaporative cooling and behavioral thermoregulation; skin color changes in some species |

### Clinical Implications

- **Drug absorption**: Many drugs can be administered via bath immersion or topical application (transcutaneous absorption). This is a practical and low-stress route for small patients.
- **Toxin sensitivity**: The permeable skin absorbs waterborne contaminants readily. Residues on gloves (hand sanitizer, lotions, cleaning chemicals) are directly toxic. **Always use powder-free, chemical-free nitrile gloves rinsed with dechlorinated water before handling amphibians.**
- **Desiccation risk**: Rapid water loss occurs in dry environments. Keep patients moist at all times during examination and procedures. Use damp towels, misting, and shallow water baths.
- **Handling damage**: The skin is delicate and easily traumatized. Minimize handling time and avoid abrasive surfaces.

## Taxonomy and Natural History

### Anurans (Frogs and Toads)

- Most diverse amphibian order; occupy aquatic, semi-aquatic, arboreal, and terrestrial niches
- Representative families encountered in clinical settings: Ranidae (true frogs), Bufonidae (toads), Hylidae (tree frogs), Dendrobatidae (poison-dart frogs), Pipidae (*Xenopus*, *Pipa*), Ceratophryidae (horned frogs)
- Toads (Bufonidae) possess prominent parotoid glands producing bufotoxins (cardiotoxic bufadienolides); significant toxicosis risk if ingested by domestic animals
- *Xenopus laevis* and *X. tropicalis* are widely used laboratory species with well-characterized biology

### Urodeles (Salamanders and Newts)

- Include fully aquatic species (axolotl, *Ambystoma mexicanum*; hellbender, *Cryptobranchus alleganiensis*), semi-aquatic newts (Salamandridae), and fully terrestrial lungless salamanders (Plethodontidae)
- Axolotls are neotenic (retain larval features including external gills throughout life) and possess remarkable regenerative capacity
- Newts of genus *Taricha* and *Notophthalmus* produce tetrodotoxin (TTX) in skin secretions

### Caecilians (Gymnophiona)

- Limbless, fossorial (burrowing) or aquatic amphibians; elongate, annulated body
- Rarely presented clinically except in zoological collections
- Possess a unique sensory tentacle between the eye and nostril (chemosensory organ)
- Some species are viviparous with dermatotrophic larvae (feed on maternal skin secretions)

## Environmental Requirements

Correct husbandry is the foundation of amphibian health. The majority of clinical disease in captive amphibians is attributable to husbandry failures.

### Water Quality Parameters

| Parameter | Acceptable Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Species-specific (see below) | Most critical single parameter |
| pH | 6.5–8.0 (species-specific) | Acidic or alkaline extremes damage skin |
| Ammonia (NH3/NH4+) | 0 mg/L (< 0.02 mg/L tolerated) | Toxic; absorbed through skin; primary concern in static water |
| Nitrite (NO2-) | 0 mg/L (< 0.1 mg/L tolerated) | Causes methemoglobinemia |
| Nitrate (NO3-) | < 20 mg/L | Less acutely toxic but cumulative |
| Chlorine/chloramine | 0 mg/L | Lethal at low concentrations; always dechlorinate |
| Dissolved oxygen | > 6 mg/L | Critical for aquatic species relying on cutaneous respiration |
| Hardness (GH) | 50–200 mg/L CaCO3 | Species-dependent; soft water species sensitive to high GH |

### Temperature Ranges

| Species/Group | Preferred Temperature (°C) |
|---|---|
| Tropical frogs (Dendrobatidae, Hylidae) | 22–26 |
| Temperate anurans (*Rana*, *Bufo*) | 15–22 |
| *Xenopus laevis* | 18–22 |
| Axolotl (*Ambystoma mexicanum*) | 14–18 (never >22) |
| Temperate salamanders | 12–18 |
| Tropical caecilians | 24–28 |

### Housing Essentials

- **Substrate**: sphagnum moss, coconut fiber, or damp paper towel for terrestrial species; bare-bottom or fine sand for aquatic species (avoid gravel that can be ingested)
- **Water**: dechlorinated, aged tap water or reverse osmosis water reconstituted with amphibian-safe mineral supplements
- **Humidity**: 60–90% for most tropical species; misting systems or covered enclosures to reduce evaporation
- **Lighting**: low-intensity, full-spectrum lighting; most amphibians are nocturnal or crepuscular; avoid high-intensity UVB (excessive UVB is harmful to some species, unlike reptiles)
- **Hiding spots**: essential to reduce stress; cork bark, leaf litter, PVC pipe sections

## Clinical Examination

### Pre-Examination Considerations

- Review husbandry history thoroughly before physical examination (water quality testing, temperature logs, diet, enclosure setup)
- Prepare all equipment before removing the animal from its enclosure to minimize handling time
- Work surface: damp towel or shallow container of dechlorinated water
- Wear rinsed, powder-free nitrile gloves

### Physical Examination Approach

1. **Observation in enclosure**: posture, activity level, respiration, skin color, body condition, buoyancy (aquatic species)
2. **Gentle restraint**: cup the animal in moistened, gloved hands; anurans can be gently restrained with fingers around the pelvic girdle; salamanders along the body
3. **Systematic assessment**:

| Body System | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Skin | Color, texture, lesions, ulceration, sloughing, hyperemia, edema, petechiae |
| Eyes | Clarity, symmetry, corneal opacity, periorbital edema |
| Oral cavity | Mucous membranes, stomatitis, masses (gently open with a moistened cotton-tipped applicator) |
| Body condition | Pelvic prominences, muscle mass over forelimbs and hindlimbs; pectoral girdle visible in emaciated anurans |
| Coelom | Distension (hydrocoelom, edema syndrome), palpable masses, organomegaly |
| Limbs | Symmetry, deformities, fractures, swelling, digit loss |
| Cloaca | Prolapse, swelling, discharge |
| Neurological | Righting reflex, response to stimuli, swimming coordination (aquatic species), seizure activity |

### Diagnostic Sampling

- **Skin scrape/swab**: for cytology, fungal culture, *Batrachochytrium* PCR; swab the ventral skin with a sterile dry swab (10 strokes along the ventral surface for Bd/Bsal qPCR)
- **Blood collection**: ventral abdominal (midline) vein, femoral vein, or lingual vein in large anurans; cardiac puncture in terminal sampling; maximum safe volume: 1% of body weight
- **Coelomic fluid aspiration**: ultrasound-guided paracentesis for hydrocoelom evaluation
- **Fecal examination**: direct wet mount and fecal flotation for nematodes, trematodes, protozoans

## Chytridiomycosis

### Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd)

The most devastating amphibian pathogen globally, responsible for the decline or extinction of over 200 species.

- **Etiology**: Chytrid fungus (*Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*); infects keratinized epidermal cells. Motile zoospores are the infectious stage.
- **Epidemiology**: Pandemic lineage (BdGPL) has spread globally, likely facilitated by the international amphibian trade. Some populations harbor endemic, less virulent Bd lineages.
- **Pathogenesis**: Infection disrupts epidermal electrolyte transport, leading to osmotic imbalance, hyponatremia, hypokalemia, and ultimately asystolic cardiac arrest.
- **Clinical signs**: Lethargy, anorexia, abnormal posture (splayed limbs), excessive skin shedding, skin erythema, failure to right when overturned, seizures, sudden death. In tadpoles: depigmentation of mouthparts (keratinized tooth rows and jaw sheaths).
- **Diagnosis**: qPCR of skin swabs (gold standard); histopathology showing intracellular zoosporangia in the stratum corneum and stratum granulosum with irregular hyperkeratosis.
- **Treatment**:
  - **Itraconazole bath**: 0.01% solution (100 mg/L) for 5 minutes daily for 10–11 days; rinse after each treatment. **Caution**: toxic to some species (particularly salamanders and small-bodied frogs); test individuals before treating groups.
  - **Heat therapy**: maintaining temperatures at 28–30°C for 10 days eliminates Bd in thermotolerant species (Bd ceases growth above 28°C). Not suitable for cold-adapted species.
  - **Voriconazole bath**: 1.25 microgram/mL for 5 minutes daily for 7–10 days; promising alternative with lower toxicity in some species.
  - **Terbinafine bath**: 0.01% for 5 minutes daily; effective in vitro; variable in vivo results.
- **Biosecurity**: disinfection with 1% Virkon-S, 10% bleach solution (contact time >1 minute), or complete drying and UV exposure. Equipment must be disinfected between enclosures and collection sites.

### Batrachochytrium salamandrivorans (Bsal)

- **Etiology**: A second chytrid species discovered in 2013, primarily pathogenic to urodeles (salamanders and newts). Origin: East Asia.
- **Pathogenesis**: Causes deep erosive skin ulceration rather than the superficial keratinization disruption seen with Bd. Produces multifocal, coalescing dermal ulcers.
- **Clinical signs**: Multifocal skin ulceration, anorexia, lethargy, ataxia, rapid death. Mortality rates approach 100% in naive European fire salamanders (*Salamandra salamandra*).
- **Diagnosis**: qPCR of skin swabs (species-specific primers); histopathology showing epidermal erosions with intracellular chytrid thalli.
- **Treatment**: Combination of polymyxin E (2000 IU/mL) and voriconazole (12.5 microgram/mL) bath for 10 minutes daily plus heat (20–25°C) for 12 days has shown efficacy. Bsal tolerates lower temperatures than Bd and is not reliably eliminated by heat alone.
- **Regulatory status**: Bsal has not yet been confirmed in the Americas; import restrictions on salamanders enacted in multiple countries as a preventive measure (U.S. Lacey Act interim rule, EU trade restrictions).

## Ranavirus

- **Etiology**: Genus *Ranavirus* (family Iridoviridae); double-stranded DNA viruses that infect amphibians, reptiles, and fish. Common species: frog virus 3 (FV3), Ambystoma tigrinum virus (ATV), common midwife toad virus (CMTV).
- **Epidemiology**: Affects multiple amphibian life stages; mass mortality events in larvae and metamorphs are characteristic. Transmission via direct contact, water, and ingestion of infected tissue.
- **Clinical signs**: Systemic hemorrhage, skin ulceration and erythema, edema (subcutaneous, coelomic), hepatic necrosis, lethargy, buoyancy abnormalities. Larval die-offs may be the only sign in wild populations.
- **Diagnosis**: PCR (MCP gene target), virus isolation in cell culture (FHM or EPC cells), histopathology showing basophilic intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies and multicentric necrosis.
- **Treatment**: No specific antiviral therapy. Supportive care: fluid therapy, thermal support, antimicrobials for secondary bacterial infections. Mortality is typically high.
- **OIE/WOAH notifiable**: Ranavirus infection in amphibians is a notifiable disease in many jurisdictions.

## Common Clinical Conditions

### Edema Syndrome (Hydrocoelom)

- Multifactorial condition presenting as generalized subcutaneous edema and/or coelomic fluid accumulation
- Causes: renal disease, cardiac disease, hypoproteinemia, septicemia, osmotic imbalance (inappropriate water salinity), chytridiomycosis, toxin exposure
- Diagnosis: coelomic fluid analysis (transudate vs. exudate vs. modified transudate), blood chemistry, ultrasound, culture
- Treatment: address underlying cause; gentle coelomocentesis for respiratory compromise; adjust water osmolality (isotonic amphibian Ringer's solution: 6.6 g NaCl, 0.15 g KCl, 0.15 g CaCl2, 0.2 g NaHCO3 per liter)

### Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

- Common in captive anurans fed calcium-deficient diets (undusted insects)
- Clinical signs: mandibular and skeletal deformities, pathologic fractures, tetanic seizures (hypocalcemia), reduced jaw tone ("rubber jaw")
- Treatment: calcium gluconate 50–100 mg/kg ICe or IV for acute hypocalcemic tetany; long-term dietary correction with calcium-dusted prey and UVB exposure (low-level, species-appropriate)

### Bacterial Dermatosepticemia ("Red Leg Syndrome")

- A syndrome rather than a single disease; characterized by erythema and petechiation of the ventral skin
- Commonly associated with *Aeromonas hydrophila*, *Citrobacter freundii*, *Chryseobacterium* spp., and other gram-negative opportunists
- Predisposing factors: poor water quality, overcrowding, immunosuppression, concurrent disease
- Treatment: culture and sensitivity-guided antimicrobials; enrofloxacin (5–10 mg/kg PO, ICe, or bath SID), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (15–20 mg/kg PO SID), or amikacin (3–5 mg/kg ICe every 72 hours); correct husbandry deficiencies

### Mycobacteriosis

- Chronic, granulomatous disease caused by *Mycobacterium* spp. (M. marinum, M. liflandii, M. gordonae)
- Clinical signs: weight loss, chronic skin ulceration, granulomas in liver, spleen, and kidneys
- Diagnosis: acid-fast staining, PCR, culture (slow-growing)
- Treatment: generally unrewarding in amphibians; euthanasia is often the most humane option for clinically affected individuals. Zoonotic risk (M. marinum causes fish tank granuloma in humans).

### Parasitic Diseases

- **Nematodes**: *Rhabdias* spp. (lungworms), *Cosmocercoides* spp. (intestinal); treated with fenbendazole 50–100 mg/kg PO, repeated in 14 days
- **Trematodes**: complex life cycles involving snail intermediate hosts; *Ribeiroia ondatrae* causes limb malformations in anurans
- **Myxosporeans and microsporidians**: intracellular parasites; limited treatment options
- **Protozoans**: *Entamoeba ranarum*, trypanosomes, opalinids (commensal, typically non-pathogenic)

## Analgesia and Anesthesia

### Analgesia

Pain recognition in amphibians is evolving. There is increasing evidence that amphibians possess nociceptors and behavioral indicators of pain.

| Drug | Dose | Route | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Meloxicam | 0.1–0.2 mg/kg | ICe, topical | SID | Limited PK data; widely used |
| Butorphanol | 0.5–2.0 mg/kg | SC, ICe | q 4–12 h | Kappa-opioid receptor-mediated |
| Morphine | 20–50 mg/L bath | Immersion | PRN | Transcutaneous absorption; effective in *Xenopus* |
| Lidocaine | 1–2 mg/kg | Local infiltration | PRN | Dilute to 0.5% for small patients |
| Flunixin meglumine | 0.1 mg/kg | ICe | SID | Limited data; use as alternative to meloxicam |

### Anesthesia

| Agent | Method | Onset | Recovery | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222) | 1–3 g/L immersion (buffered to pH 7.0 with NaHCO3) | 5–15 min | 5–30 min | Gold standard; must be buffered; acidic solutions cause pain and skin damage |
| Eugenol (clove oil) | 300–600 mg/L immersion | 5–15 min | 10–30 min | Less tissue irritation than MS-222; narrow safety margin in some species |
| Isoflurane | Saturated gauze in closed container or bubbled through water | 5–10 min | 5–15 min | Alternative for terrestrial species; keep skin moist |
| Alfaxalone | 10–30 mg/kg ICe or SC | 5–20 min | Variable | Smooth induction; growing evidence base |
| Propofol | 10–35 mg/kg IV or ICe | 1–5 min | 10–30 min | Rapid onset; limited data on safety across species |

**Monitoring during anesthesia**: Loss of righting reflex indicates light surgical plane. Loss of withdrawal reflex (toe/tail pinch) confirms surgical anesthesia. Monitor heart rate visually (ventral cardiac impulse) or via Doppler. Maintain skin moisture throughout.

## Euthanasia

- **Preferred method**: MS-222 immersion at 5–10 g/L (buffered) until cessation of cardiac activity, confirmed by Doppler. Follow with secondary physical method (pithing, cardiac injection of pentobarbital, or freezing) to ensure death.
- **Alternative**: Intracoelomic injection of pentobarbital (100–200 mg/kg).
- **Not acceptable as sole method**: freezing or hypothermia without prior chemical anesthesia. While historically practiced, evidence indicates that ice crystal formation in tissues is painful.

## Biosecurity in Amphibian Medicine

- Change gloves between every patient
- Disinfect all equipment between patients (1% Virkon-S or 10% bleach for 1 minute)
- Maintain separate water supplies for each enclosure
- Quarantine new arrivals for a minimum of 60 days with Bd/Bsal screening (qPCR on days 0 and 30)
- Decontaminate field equipment between sites to prevent pathogen translocation
- Follow PARC (Partners in Amphibian and Reptile Conservation) and AArk (Amphibian Ark) biosecurity guidelines

## Conservation and Regulatory Considerations

- Approximately 41% of amphibian species are threatened with extinction (IUCN Red List)
- CITES listings apply to many species in the pet and research trade (e.g., *Mantella* spp., *Dyscophus antongilii*)
- Chytridiomycosis and ranavirosis are WOAH (OIE) notifiable diseases
- Ex situ breeding programs (amphibian survival assurance populations) are managed by AZA Species Survival Plans and EAZA Breeding Programs
- The Global Amphibian Assessment and Amphibian Survival Alliance coordinate international conservation efforts
