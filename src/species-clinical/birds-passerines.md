# Passerines and Other Avian Orders

## Overview

Passerines (Order Passeriformes) constitute over half of all bird species, with more than 6,000 described species worldwide. Their ubiquity in human-modified landscapes makes them frequent patients in wildlife rehabilitation, and their ecological roles as insectivores, seed dispersers, and pollinators make their population health a conservation priority. This chapter also covers several non-passerine orders commonly encountered in wildlife practice: corvids (which are passerines but merit special attention), wild psittacines, columbids, and galliformes.

## General Passerine Physiology

### High Metabolic Rate

Passerines have the highest mass-specific metabolic rates among birds, which in turn are among the highest of all vertebrates. This has direct clinical implications:

- **Rapid energy depletion**: A small passerine that is not eating can become critically hypoglycemic within hours. Triage must include immediate caloric support.
- **High body temperature**: Normal cloacal temperature ranges from 40–43°C in most passerines; temperatures perceived as febrile in other taxa may be normal.
- **Rapid heart rate**: 400–900+ bpm depending on species and body size; monitoring is challenging without specialized equipment.
- **Short anesthetic windows**: Inhalant anesthesia (isoflurane) induction and recovery are rapid; dose adjustments happen quickly due to efficient avian respiratory system.

### Body Condition Assessment

- **Keel palpation**: The primary method; muscle mass on either side of the keel is graded on a 1–5 scale
  - Grade 1: Emaciated; keel very prominent, no palpable muscle
  - Grade 2: Thin; keel prominent, reduced muscle mass
  - Grade 3: Normal; keel palpable but muscles rounded on either side
  - Grade 4: Overweight; keel difficult to palpate through muscle
  - Grade 5: Obese; keel not palpable
- **Body weight**: Essential; use a gram-scale with 0.1g precision for small passerines
- **Fat scoring**: Subcutaneous fat deposits visible through thin skin (furcular hollow, abdomen) can be scored

### Feather Biology

- Passerines typically have one complete annual molt (prebasic molt) and some species have a partial pre-alternate molt before breeding season
- Feather condition is critical for thermoregulation, flight, and waterproofing
- Pin feathers (growing feathers with active blood supply) are susceptible to damage and hemorrhage
- Stress bars (fault bars): Translucent lines across feather vanes caused by nutritional or physiological stress during feather growth; indicate compromised feather integrity

## Songbirds (Suborder Passeri)

### Common Clinical Presentations

#### Window Strike Injuries

Window collisions are estimated to kill 365–988 million birds annually in the United States alone, making this one of the most significant anthropogenic causes of avian mortality.

**Presentation**: Bird found on the ground near a building; may be unconscious, stunned, or alert but unable to fly.

**Assessment protocol**:

1. Neurological examination: level of consciousness, pupil symmetry and response, head tilt, nystagmus, ability to perch
2. Palpation for fractures: coracoid, keel, wings, legs
3. Ophthalmic examination: hyphema, lens luxation, retinal damage
4. Radiographs if fractures suspected

**Common injuries from window strikes**:

| Injury | Frequency | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|
| Concussion (no structural damage) | Most common | Good with supportive care; 1–4 hours quiet, dark, warm environment |
| Coracoid fracture | Common | Fair; conservative management with body wrap |
| Beak fracture | Moderate | Variable; depends on severity and species |
| Ocular trauma (hyphema, retinal injury) | Moderate | Guarded; vision loss may preclude release |
| Cervical fracture/dislocation | Less common | Grave; usually fatal |
| Air sac rupture (subcutaneous emphysema) | Occasional | Good; resolves with needle aspiration and rest |

**Treatment**: Dark, quiet, warm environment; anti-inflammatory therapy (meloxicam 0.5–1 mg/kg PO or IM); fluid support if dehydrated; release when fully functional.

#### Cat Attack Injuries

Domestic and feral cats kill an estimated 1.3–4 billion birds annually in the US. Cat attack is one of the most common presentations in passerine rehabilitation.

**Critical points**:

- **All cat-caught birds require antibiotic therapy**: Cat saliva contains *Pasteurella multocida* and other bacteria that cause rapidly fatal septicemia in birds. Even birds with no visible wounds may have puncture wounds hidden by feathers.
- **Antibiotic of choice**: Amoxicillin-clavulanate (125 mg/kg PO BID) or enrofloxacin (15 mg/kg PO or IM BID). Begin antibiotics immediately; do not wait for clinical signs of infection.
- **Wound management**: Careful feather parting to identify all puncture wounds; wound lavage; debridement if needed
- **Stress management**: Cat-caught birds are severely stressed; minimize handling after initial assessment and treatment

### West Nile Virus (WNV)

#### Epidemiology

- Mosquito-borne flavivirus; arrived in North America in 1999 and spread across the continent within 5 years
- Corvids (crows, jays, magpies) are the most susceptible passerines, with mortality rates approaching 100% in American crows
- Many other passerine species are affected with varying susceptibility
- Wild birds serve as amplifying hosts; mosquitoes (*Culex* spp.) are the primary vector
- Zoonotic: birds are the reservoir, mosquitoes the vector, humans and horses are dead-end hosts

#### Clinical Signs in Birds

- Acute: depression, ruffled feathers, inability to fly, ataxia, head tilt, tremors, seizures, sudden death
- Chronic: persistent neurological deficits, blindness, weight loss
- Post-mortem findings: encephalitis, myocarditis, splenomegaly, hepatomegaly

#### Diagnosis

- Oral swab or cloacal swab for RT-PCR (live birds)
- Serology (VN or PRNT) for exposure assessment
- Post-mortem: tissue PCR, immunohistochemistry, viral isolation from brain, heart, kidney

#### Surveillance Value

- Dead bird reporting (particularly corvids) is a cost-effective early warning system for WNV activity in a region
- Sentinel bird surveillance programs use captive chickens to monitor seroconversion

### Avian Pox

#### Etiology and Transmission

- Avipoxvirus; transmitted by mosquitoes, direct contact, and fomites
- Multiple strains with varying host specificity; some strains cross species barriers

#### Clinical Forms

- **Cutaneous (dry pox)**: Proliferative, wart-like lesions on unfeathered skin (feet, legs, eyelids, cere, commissures of beak). Most common form. Self-limiting in mild cases but can cause visual obstruction, difficulty feeding, and secondary infection.
- **Diphtheritic (wet pox)**: Caseous plaques on mucous membranes of oral cavity, larynx, trachea, and esophagus. More serious; can cause respiratory obstruction and inability to eat.
- **Septicemic**: Rare; systemic infection with high mortality.

#### Treatment

- Supportive care; nutritional support; address secondary bacterial or fungal infections
- Cutaneous lesions: topical antiseptics; do not remove firmly attached scabs
- Diphtheritic lesions: careful debridement if causing obstruction; antifungal treatment if secondary *Candida* infection

### Trichomonosis (*Trichomonas gallinae*)

#### Epidemiology

- Protozoal infection primarily affecting columbids (pigeons and doves) and raptors that prey on them
- Transmitted through direct contact, contaminated crop milk (pigeon to squab), and contaminated water at bird feeders
- Emerging as a significant disease of finches in Europe (greenfinch, chaffinch) since 2005

#### Clinical Signs

- Caseous, yellow-white plaques in the oral cavity, pharynx, esophagus, and crop ("canker" or "frounce")
- Difficulty swallowing, weight loss, regurgitation
- Advanced cases: esophageal perforation, hepatic granulomas, air sac involvement

#### Diagnosis

- Wet mount of oral/crop swab: motile, flagellated protozoa visible under microscopy
- Culture for higher sensitivity
- PCR for species confirmation and strain typing

#### Treatment

- Carnidazole (20–30 mg/kg PO once) or metronidazole (50 mg/kg PO BID for 5–7 days)
- Supportive care; liquid diet initially if oral lesions are extensive
- Clean and disinfect bird feeders in areas with active outbreaks

## Corvids (Crows, Ravens, Jays, Magpies)

### Special Considerations

Corvids merit particular attention due to their cognitive complexity, ecological importance, and role in disease ecology.

- **Intelligence**: Corvids demonstrate tool use, problem-solving, social learning, and potentially self-awareness. This cognitive capacity creates specific rehabilitation challenges: corvids habituate to humans readily and may become imprinted if improperly managed during rehabilitation.
- **WNV sentinel species**: American crows are the most WNV-susceptible bird species; dead crow surveillance is a primary public health monitoring tool.
- **Highly social**: Communal roosting in some species creates potential for rapid disease transmission within populations.
- **Omnivorous diet**: Adaptable feeding behavior; relatively straightforward nutritional management in rehabilitation.
- **Legal status**: In many jurisdictions, corvids are classified as migratory birds (protected) while simultaneously being subject to depredation permits. Legal status varies by species and location.

### Common Presentations

- West Nile virus (extremely high susceptibility)
- Gunshot wounds (from depredation activities)
- Vehicle collisions
- Orphaned juveniles (fledglings on the ground are often mistakenly collected by the public; assessment of whether intervention is needed is critical)
- Avian pox

## Wild Psittacines (Parrots, Parakeets, Cockatoos)

### Key Considerations

- **Psittacosis (Chlamydiosis)**: *Chlamydia psittaci* infection is the primary zoonotic concern when handling any psittacine. Causes respiratory disease, hepatitis, and splenomegaly in birds; atypical pneumonia in humans. Reportable disease in many jurisdictions. Doxycycline is the treatment of choice (25–50 mg/kg PO BID for 45 days).
- **Proventricular Dilatation Disease (PDD)**: Avian bornavirus causes ganglioneuritis of the GI tract; proventricular dilation, crop stasis, undigested seeds in feces, weight loss, and neurological signs.
- **Psittacine Beak and Feather Disease (PBFD)**: Circovirus; causes feather dystrophy, beak necrosis, and immunosuppression. Significant conservation concern for endangered species (e.g., orange-bellied parrot, swift parrot).
- **Conservation status**: Many wild psittacine species are endangered due to habitat loss and illegal trade. Veterinary involvement in anti-trafficking enforcement (species identification, health assessment of seized birds) is increasingly important.

## Columbids (Pigeons and Doves)

### Common Clinical Presentations

- **Trichomonosis**: See above; pigeons and doves are the primary reservoir
- **Pigeon paramyxovirus (PPMV-1)**: Variant of Newcastle disease virus; causes neurological disease (torticollis, circling, paralysis), green diarrhea, and respiratory signs. Reportable due to Newcastle disease classification; important to differentiate from velogenic NDV strains that threaten poultry.
- **Salmonellosis**: *Salmonella typhimurium* causes arthritis, granulomatous ingluvitis, and septicemia; zoonotic.
- **Pigeon pox**: Avipoxvirus; cutaneous and diphtheritic forms as described above.
- **Parasitism**: *Capillaria* spp. (crop, esophagus), coccidia, roundworms, and ectoparasites (pigeon flies, feather lice).

### Handling

- Gentle restraint with wings folded against body; control head to prevent wing battering
- Crop palpation for assessment of crop fill and content
- Fecal examination for parasites is standard on intake

## Galliformes (Quail, Grouse, Pheasants, Turkeys, Prairie Chickens)

### Key Considerations

- **Ground-nesting and ground-dwelling**: Highly susceptible to predation, habitat management practices (mowing, burning), and ground-level pesticide exposure
- **Game bird status**: Many species are managed as game species; wildlife veterinarians interact with game management agencies
- **Mycoplasmosis**: *Mycoplasma gallisepticum* and *M. synoviae* cause chronic respiratory disease and conjunctivitis; house finch eye disease in eastern North America is caused by *M. gallisepticum* of poultry origin. Spills over between wild and domestic bird populations.
- **Histomoniasis (Blackhead)**: *Histomonas meleagridis* transmitted via cecal worm (*Heterakis gallinarum*) eggs; causes necrotizing hepatitis and typhlitis, particularly in turkeys and pheasants. Treatment options limited since the withdrawal of nitarsone.
- **Reticuloendotheliosis and lymphoproliferative disease**: Retroviruses affecting wild turkeys and other gallinaceous birds.

### Conservation Concerns

- **Greater sage-grouse**: Iconic species of sagebrush steppe; population declines linked to habitat loss and fragmentation. West Nile virus is an additional threat.
- **Prairie chickens**: Endangered subspecies (Attwater's prairie chicken) subject to intensive captive breeding and reintroduction efforts.
- **Helmeted guineafowl and other species**: Disease interface with poultry operations is a significant management concern in many regions.

## Rehabilitation Principles for Small Birds

### Triage

- **Assess immediately**: Level of consciousness, respiratory effort, obvious injuries, body condition
- **Warmth first**: Place in incubator or heated environment (30–32°C for most species) before detailed examination
- **Fluid and caloric support**: Oral electrolyte solution followed by calorie-dense formula (insectivore diet, critical care formula) within 1–2 hours of admission
- **Minimize handling**: Examination should be brief and focused; excessive handling increases stress mortality

### Orphan Management

- **Confirm orphan status**: Many fledglings found on the ground are being cared for by parents nearby. Unless injured, ill, or in immediate danger, they should be left alone or returned to a nearby sheltered spot.
- **Species identification**: Determines diet, housing, and developmental timeline
- **Feeding schedule**: Nestlings require feeding every 15–30 minutes during daylight hours; frequency decreases as they grow
- **Avoid imprinting**: Minimize visual and auditory contact; use puppet feeding when possible for species prone to imprinting
- **Fledging and flight conditioning**: Gradual transition to flight cage; self-feeding must be confirmed before release

### Release Criteria

- Full flight capability demonstrated in flight cage
- Self-feeding confirmed for at least 48–72 hours
- Appropriate body weight and condition
- Complete feather development (no pin feathers in flight or tail feathers)
- Appropriate season and weather for release
- Species-appropriate habitat available at release site
- Wild behavior maintained (appropriate fear response to humans)
