# Small Mammals

## Overview

Small mammals encompass a remarkably diverse group of wildlife species that are frequently encountered in rehabilitation settings, wildlife health surveillance programs, and ecological research. Their small body size presents unique clinical challenges, including rapid metabolic rates, susceptibility to hypothermia and hypoglycemia, and difficulty with venipuncture and drug dosing precision. This chapter covers mustelids, lagomorphs, rodents, insectivores, and bats.

## Mustelids

### Species Groups

This family includes weasels, ferrets, mink, otters, martens, fishers, badgers, and wolverines. Many species are of conservation concern, and several serve as sentinel species for environmental contamination.

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- Elongated, flexible body plan with short limbs; adapted for pursuit predation in burrows and dense cover
- Anal scent glands produce potent musk; significant consideration during handling
- High metabolic rate relative to body size; rapid gastric transit time
- Relatively large adrenal glands; adrenal disease (hyperadrenocorticism) common in some species
- Dense, water-resistant pelage in aquatic species (otters, mink)

### Baseline Vital Parameters

| Parameter | Weasel (Mustela spp.) | River Otter | Badger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heart rate (bpm) | 300–500 | 100–160 | 80–140 |
| Respiratory rate (breaths/min) | 30–60 | 15–30 | 15–25 |
| Temperature (°C) | 38.5–40.0 | 38.0–39.5 | 37.5–39.0 |
| Approximate body mass (kg) | 0.03–0.35 | 5–14 | 8–16 |

### Common Diseases

- **Canine distemper virus (CDV)**: Mustelids are highly susceptible; nearly 100% fatal in black-footed ferrets. CDV was a major obstacle to ferret recovery programs until vaccine development.
- **Aleutian disease (parvovirus)**: Causes immune-complex disease in mink and ferrets; chronic wasting, hypergammaglobulinemia, glomerulonephritis.
- **Tuberculosis**: *Mycobacterium bovis* infection is significant in European badger populations and drives complex wildlife-livestock management policies.
- **Toxoplasma gondii**: Sea otters are unusually susceptible; land-to-sea pathogen transmission via freshwater runoff is a One Health concern.
- **Ectoparasites**: Fleas, ticks, and mites; some species serve as hosts for plague-carrying fleas (*Yersinia pestis*).

### Handling Techniques

- Heavy leather gloves for initial capture; mustelids have powerful jaws relative to body size and can inflict deep bite wounds
- Squeeze cages or transparent tubes facilitate examination without full manual restraint
- Chemical immobilization recommended for prolonged procedures in larger mustelids (otters, badgers, wolverines)
- Small weasels: cone-shaped restraint devices or isoflurane induction in a chamber

## Lagomorphs

### Species Groups

Rabbits (Leporidae) and pikas (Ochotonidae). Wild rabbits and hares are among the most commonly presented small mammals in wildlife rehabilitation.

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- **Hindgut fermentation and cecotrophy**: Lagomorphs produce two types of fecal pellets—soft cecotrophs (re-ingested for nutrient absorption) and hard fecal pellets. Disruption of cecotrophy (e.g., during hospitalization) can cause nutritional deficiency and GI dysbiosis.
- **Fragile skeletal system**: The skeleton comprises only approximately 7–8% of body weight (compared to 12–13% in cats). Vertebral fractures from improper restraint are a well-documented risk.
- **Obligate nasal breathers**: Any nasal obstruction is a respiratory emergency
- **Continuously growing (elodont) teeth**: Dental malocclusion occurs in captive settings
- **Powerful hindlimbs**: Capable of delivering kicks that can fracture their own lumbar spine if restrained improperly

### Common Clinical Presentations

- **Myxomatosis**: Caused by Myxoma virus (Leporipoxvirus). Endemic in some wild rabbit populations; causes mucinous skin tumors, conjunctivitis, and edema. Highly lethal in European rabbits (*Oryctolagus cuniculus*); some populations have evolved resistance.
- **Rabbit Hemorrhagic Disease (RHD/RHDV2)**: Caused by a calicivirus. Acute, highly lethal hepatitis. RHDV2 has expanded geographic range and host range (affecting hares and some cottontail species). Peracute death with epistaxis is characteristic.
- **Pasteurellosis**: *Pasteurella multocida* causes upper respiratory infection, pneumonia, abscesses, and septicemia.
- **Encephalitozoonosis**: *Encephalitozoon cuniculi* causes granulomatous encephalitis and nephritis; head tilt is a common presentation.
- **GI stasis**: Cessation of normal gastrointestinal motility; can be rapidly fatal. Stress, pain, dietary change, and dehydration are predisposing factors.

### Handling Techniques

- Support the hindquarters at all times to prevent lumbar fractures from kicking
- Wrap in towel ("bunny burrito") for examination; cover eyes to reduce stress
- Never lift by ears or scruff alone
- Minimize handling duration; lagomorphs are extremely stress-susceptible
- For wild hares: chemical immobilization is often safer than manual restraint for prolonged procedures

## Rodents

### Species Groups

The order Rodentia is the most species-rich mammalian order, encompassing squirrels, beavers, porcupines, rats, mice, voles, muskrats, and many others.

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- **Continuously growing incisors**: All rodents have open-rooted, ever-growing incisors with enamel only on the labial surface. Malocclusion leads to overgrowth and inability to eat.
- **Diverse digestive strategies**: Ranges from simple omnivorous (rats, mice) to hindgut fermentation (beaver, porcupine, capybara)
- **Highly variable body size**: From 5-gram harvest mice to 50-kg capybaras
- **Rapid reproductive rate**: Short gestation periods and large litter sizes in many species
- **Specialized adaptations**: Beaver tail (thermoregulation, fat storage), porcupine quills (modified hairs), flying squirrel patagium

### Common Diseases and Concerns

- **Plague (*Yersinia pestis*)**: Prairie dogs and ground squirrels are highly susceptible; plague epizootics can eliminate entire colonies. Critical concern for endangered black-footed ferret recovery programs. Zoonotic.
- **Hantavirus**: Deer mice (*Peromyscus maniculatus*) are primary reservoir for Sin Nombre virus in North America. Causes hantavirus pulmonary syndrome in humans. Important biosafety consideration when handling rodents.
- **Tularemia (*Francisella tularensis*)**: Affects many rodent and lagomorph species. Zoonotic; transmission via tick bite, direct contact, or aerosol. Use appropriate PPE.
- **Leptospirosis**: Rodents are maintenance hosts for many *Leptospira* serovars. Renal shedding in urine; significant zoonotic and domestic animal health concern.
- **Baylisascaris neural larva migrans**: Rodents serve as intermediate hosts for *Baylisascaris procyonis*; fatal neurological disease from aberrant larval migration in CNS.

### Handling Techniques

- Small rodents: cupped hands, tubes, or small cloth bags for transport
- Larger rodents (beaver, porcupine): heavy gloves; porcupines can embed quills with reflexive tail slaps
- Beavers can inflict severe bite wounds with their incisors; approach with extreme caution
- Squirrels are agile and bite readily; leather gloves and towel wrap recommended
- Biosafety precautions essential: gloves, N95 mask recommended when handling wild rodents in hantavirus-endemic areas

## Insectivores

### Species Groups

Shrews (Soricidae), moles (Talpidae), and hedgehogs (Erinaceidae). These small mammals are encountered less frequently in clinical practice but are ecologically important and occasionally present in rehabilitation settings.

### Key Anatomical and Physiological Features

- **Extremely high metabolic rate**: Shrews have the highest mass-specific metabolic rate of any mammal. Some species must eat every 2–3 hours or face starvation. Heart rates can exceed 1,000 bpm.
- **Venomous saliva**: Some shrews (e.g., *Blarina brevicauda*) produce venomous saliva used to subdue prey; not dangerous to humans but notable.
- **Echolocation**: Some shrews and tenrecs use rudimentary echolocation for navigation
- **Hedgehog spines**: Modified hairs; hedgehogs curl into a defensive ball using the orbicularis muscle. Self-anointing behavior (coating spines with foamy saliva) is normal.
- **Fossorial adaptations in moles**: Shovel-like forelimbs, reduced eyes, velvety reversible fur

### Common Diseases

- **Hedgehog-associated *Salmonella***: Zoonotic concern; hedgehogs can be asymptomatic carriers of *Salmonella tilene* and other serovars
- **Wobbly hedgehog syndrome**: Progressive demyelinating paralysis in African pygmy hedgehogs; suspected genetic component
- **Ectoparasites**: Heavy flea and tick burdens are common; hedgehogs may carry *Ixodes* ticks that transmit Lyme disease
- **Lungworm**: *Crenosoma* and *Capillaria* spp. cause pulmonary disease in hedgehogs
- **Dental disease**: Common in captive hedgehogs; gingivitis and tooth loss

### Handling Considerations

- Shrews: handle minimally; hypoglycemia and death from stress can occur within minutes. Provide food (insects, meat) immediately upon capture.
- Hedgehogs: unrolling requires patience or light sedation (isoflurane); grasping the ventral skin while the animal is rolled into a ball allows a grip point
- Moles: rarely presented; handle with gloves as they can bite; sensitive to light and heat stress

## Bats (Chiroptera)

### Species Groups

Bats represent approximately 20% of all mammalian species diversity, with over 1,400 described species. Major groups include megabats (Pteropodidae: fruit bats, flying foxes) and microbats (multiple families: insectivorous, nectarivorous, carnivorous, and sanguivorous species).

### Unique Physiology

- **Flight**: The only mammals capable of true powered flight. Wing membrane (patagium) is a thin, vascularized extension of skin supported by elongated finger bones. Wing membrane injuries are among the most common clinical presentations.
- **Thermoregulation**: Many microbat species use torpor (daily heterothermy) to conserve energy, reducing body temperature to near ambient and dramatically lowering metabolic rate. This must be distinguished from hypothermia in clinical assessment.
- **Echolocation**: Most microbats use laryngeal echolocation for navigation and prey capture. Hearing protection during examination is unnecessary for handlers, but noise stress should be minimized for the patient.
- **Longevity**: Bats are exceptionally long-lived for their body size; some species live 30–40 years. This is attributed to unique DNA repair mechanisms and immune adaptations.
- **Immune system**: Bats harbor numerous viruses (coronaviruses, paramyxoviruses, filoviruses, lyssaviruses) often without clinical disease. Their dampened inflammatory response (constitutively active interferon pathways, reduced NLRP3 inflammasome activation) is an active area of research.

### Common Clinical Presentations

- **Wing membrane injuries**: Tears, punctures, and holes from cat attacks, barbed wire, or other trauma. Small tears heal remarkably well; larger injuries may require surgical repair with tissue adhesive or fine absorbable sutures.
- **Grounding**: Bats found on the ground may be injured, ill, dehydrated, or orphaned. Differential diagnosis includes trauma, white-nose syndrome, rabies, toxicosis, and exhaustion.
- **White-Nose Syndrome (WNS)**: Caused by the fungus *Pseudogymnoascus destructans*. Causes cutaneous infection during hibernation, disrupting torpor cycles and leading to fat reserve depletion and death. Has devastated North American bat populations since its detection in 2006, with mortality exceeding 90% in some hibernacula.

### Zoonotic Concerns

- **Rabies**: All bat species should be considered potential rabies vectors. Any bat that has had contact with a human or domestic animal should be tested (euthanasia required for testing). Post-exposure prophylaxis recommendations apply to any bat contact where a bite cannot be excluded.
- **Histoplasmosis**: *Histoplasma capsulatum* grows in soil enriched with bat (and bird) guano. Inhalation of spores causes respiratory disease in humans. Relevant when entering bat roosts.
- **Novel viruses**: Bats are recognized reservoirs for SARS-related coronaviruses, Nipah virus, Hendra virus, Ebola virus, and Marburg virus. Direct zoonotic transmission from bats to humans is rare for most of these pathogens, but the role of intermediate hosts and spillover events is a critical One Health research area.

### Handling Techniques

- **Always wear leather gloves** when handling bats, regardless of rabies vaccination status
- Hold gently but securely around the body; avoid compressing the thorax
- Contain in small, cloth-lined containers; bats need textured surfaces to cling to
- Provide warmth (25–30°C for most temperate species) unless species normally undergoes torpor and is in appropriate condition
- Offer fluids (rehydration solution via dropper) before food in grounded bats
- Minimize handling duration; bats are stress-susceptible
- **Biosafety**: N95 respirator when entering bat roosts or handling bats from colonies with unknown disease status

## General Principles for Small Mammal Cases

### Triage Priorities

Small mammals deteriorate rapidly. Triage priorities include:

1. Warmth: Prevent hypothermia (supplemental heat source, incubator)
2. Hydration: Subcutaneous or oral fluid administration
3. Glucose: Address hypoglycemia, especially in shrews and bats
4. Quiet: Minimize visual, auditory, and tactile stimulation
5. Assessment: Systematic examination once stabilized

### Drug Dosing

- Allometric scaling is essential; per-kilogram doses derived from larger species often underestimate requirements in small mammals
- Use precision syringes (insulin syringes, tuberculin syringes) for accurate small-volume dosing
- Dilution of concentrated drugs may be necessary to measure accurate doses
- Oral, subcutaneous, and intramuscular routes are most practical; intravenous access is challenging in very small species

### Sample Collection

- Blood volume for sampling should not exceed 1% of body weight (approximately 10% of total blood volume)
- Microhematocrit tubes and point-of-care analyzers are valuable for small-volume samples
- Oral swabs, fecal samples, and ectoparasite collection can provide diagnostic information without blood sampling

### Housing During Rehabilitation

- Species-appropriate enclosures: arboreal species need vertical space and climbing structures; fossorial species need substrate for burrowing
- Temperature and humidity control appropriate for species
- Minimize human contact; wild small mammals habituate to captivity quickly, which compromises release success
- Diet matched to natural food sources as closely as possible
