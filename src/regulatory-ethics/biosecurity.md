# Biosecurity

## Introduction

Biosecurity in wildlife veterinary medicine encompasses the measures taken to prevent the introduction, spread, and release of infectious agents between wildlife, domestic animals, humans, and the environment. The wildlife veterinarian operates at the interface of all four domains: handling wild animals of unknown disease status, working in environments contaminated with diverse pathogens, transporting biological samples that may contain dangerous agents, and managing facilities where susceptible animals are housed in proximity.

The stakes are high. Wildlife veterinarians have contracted fatal infections in the line of duty, including rabies, herpesvirus B (*Macacine alphaherpesvirus 1*), and hantavirus. Disease transmission between handled wildlife and wild populations has caused conservation catastrophes. And breaches in sample handling protocols have resulted in regulatory sanctions and loss of diagnostic capacity. A systematic approach to biosecurity is therefore not optional — it is a core professional competency.

## Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

### Risk-Based PPE Selection

PPE selection must be matched to the anticipated risk level of each encounter. The following framework provides a tiered approach:

**Tier 1 — Baseline (routine handling, known healthy animals):**
- Examination gloves (nitrile preferred; latex acceptable if no allergy concern)
- Long-sleeved clothing or disposable coveralls
- Closed-toe footwear with protective covers or dedicated boots
- Eye protection if splash risk exists

**Tier 2 — Elevated risk (sick animals, unknown disease status, necropsy):**
- Double gloving (nitrile outer, examination inner)
- Disposable coveralls or dedicated scrubs with disposable gown
- Waterproof apron for necropsy
- Safety glasses or goggles
- N95 or FFP2 respirator (minimum for aerosol-generating procedures or suspected respiratory pathogens)
- Dedicated boots with boot covers or disinfection footbath
- Hair cover

**Tier 3 — High risk (suspected highly pathogenic agents, BSL-3 equivalent):**
- Powered air-purifying respirator (PAPR) or full-face respirator with appropriate cartridges
- Fluid-resistant coveralls (Tyvek or equivalent)
- Double gloving with outer glove taped to coverall sleeves
- Waterproof boot covers taped to coverall legs
- Full face shield over respirator
- Disposable head cover
- All PPE disposed of as biohazardous waste after single use

### Specific Hazard PPE Requirements

| Hazard | Minimum PPE |
|--------|-------------|
| Rabies suspect | Tier 2 + cut-resistant gloves if bite risk; pre-exposure vaccination strongly recommended |
| Avian influenza (HPAI) | Tier 3; N95/FFP2 minimum; PAPR preferred for necropsy; eye protection mandatory |
| Tuberculosis (aerosolized) | Tier 3; N95/FFP2 minimum; PAPR for necropsy of suspect cases; negative-pressure room if available |
| Hantavirus (rodents) | Tier 2; N95/FFP2 mandatory; avoid generating aerosols from rodent bedding and excreta |
| Herpesvirus B (macaques) | Tier 2 + face shield; immediate wound irrigation protocol; post-exposure antiviral prophylaxis availability |
| Psittacosis (*C. psittaci*) | Tier 2; N95/FFP2 for necropsy and cage cleaning; doxycycline prophylaxis may be indicated |
| Anthrax suspect | Tier 3; avoid opening carcass if anthrax suspected (spore formation); contact authorities immediately |
| Newcastle disease (virulent) | Tier 2; eye protection critical (conjunctivitis is common); report to regulatory authorities |
| Plague (*Yersinia pestis*) | Tier 2; flea control on carcass/animal before handling; doxycycline prophylaxis if exposure occurs |

### PPE Training and Compliance

- All personnel must be trained in proper PPE donning and doffing sequences
- Doffing is the highest-risk phase of PPE use; practice supervised doffing before working with high-risk agents
- Fit testing is required for N95 respirators and must be repeated annually and when respirator model changes
- PPE stocks must be maintained and checked for expiration dates
- Post-exposure protocols must be posted in all work areas and reviewed by all personnel

## Decontamination Procedures

### Disinfectant Selection

Disinfectant selection must account for the target pathogen, the surface or material to be decontaminated, organic load, contact time, and environmental conditions:

| Disinfectant Class | Effective Against | Limitations |
|-------------------|-------------------|-------------|
| Sodium hypochlorite (bleach) | Bacteria, viruses (enveloped and non-enveloped), fungi | Inactivated by organic matter; corrosive to metals; prepare fresh daily |
| Quaternary ammonium compounds | Bacteria, enveloped viruses, some fungi | Ineffective against non-enveloped viruses, mycobacteria, and bacterial spores |
| Potassium peroxymonosulfate (Virkon S/Trifectant) | Broad-spectrum: bacteria, viruses, fungi | Effective in the presence of moderate organic matter; widely used in veterinary settings |
| Glutaraldehyde | Bacteria, viruses, fungi, mycobacteria | Toxic fumes; requires adequate ventilation; long contact time for sporicidal activity |
| Chlorhexidine | Bacteria, some enveloped viruses, some fungi | Poor virucidal activity; not sporicidal; useful for skin antisepsis |
| 70% ethanol | Bacteria, enveloped viruses | Ineffective against non-enveloped viruses and spores; flammable |
| Accelerated hydrogen peroxide | Broad-spectrum including non-enveloped viruses | Less corrosive than bleach; effective at low concentrations |
| Formaldehyde (fumigation) | All pathogens including spores | Toxic; carcinogenic; restricted to facility decontamination with appropriate safety controls |

### Decontamination Protocols by Context

**Field equipment decontamination:**
1. Remove gross organic contamination with brushes and water
2. Apply disinfectant at appropriate concentration and ensure required contact time
3. Rinse if residual disinfectant could harm animals or the environment
4. Allow equipment to dry before storage
5. Special attention to boots, nets, traps, and surgical instruments

**Vehicle decontamination:**
- Remove all organic material from vehicle interior and undercarriage
- Spray or wipe all contact surfaces with appropriate disinfectant
- Pay particular attention to wheel wells, footwells, and cargo areas
- Allow adequate contact time before rinsing
- Vehicle decontamination is critical when moving between sites during disease outbreak investigations

**Facility decontamination:**
- Terminal cleaning between patients in rehabilitation facilities
- Full facility decontamination following confirmed infectious disease cases
- Directional workflow (clean to dirty) to prevent cross-contamination
- Dedicated cleaning equipment for each area or thorough decontamination of shared equipment

**Personnel decontamination:**
- Proper PPE doffing sequence (outer gloves, gown/coveralls, eye protection, respirator, inner gloves, hand hygiene)
- Hand washing with soap and water (preferred over alcohol-based hand sanitizer when hands are visibly soiled)
- Shower facilities available for personnel working with high-risk pathogens
- Dedicated work clothing that does not leave the facility

### Decontamination for Specific Pathogens

Certain pathogens require specific decontamination approaches:

- **Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex**: resistant to many common disinfectants; phenolic compounds, 5% sodium hypochlorite, or accelerated hydrogen peroxide required; extended contact time
- **Clostridium/Clostridioides spores**: sporicidal agents required (10% bleach, peracetic acid); quaternary ammonium compounds and alcohols are ineffective
- **Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd)**: desiccation, 70% ethanol, 1% sodium hypochlorite, or complete drying of equipment between amphibian contact sites
- **Chronic wasting disease (CWD) prions**: conventional disinfectants are insufficient; 40% bleach for one hour, or 1-2 N sodium hydroxide for one hour; incineration of heavily contaminated materials
- **Non-enveloped viruses (parvovirus, adenovirus)**: resistant to many disinfectants; sodium hypochlorite, potassium peroxymonosulfate, or accelerated hydrogen peroxide required

## Sample Handling and Shipping

### Classification of Biological Materials

Diagnostic samples from wildlife must be classified according to international dangerous goods regulations:

- **UN 3373 — Biological Substance, Category B**: the most common classification for diagnostic specimens from wildlife; defined as biological material that is not known or reasonably expected to contain pathogens that meet the criteria for Category A
- **UN 2814/UN 2900 — Infectious Substances, Category A**: substances known or reasonably expected to contain pathogens that, if exposed, are capable of causing permanent disability, life-threatening or fatal disease in otherwise healthy humans or animals; requires specialized packaging and shipping authorization
- **UN 1845 — Dry ice**: if used as a refrigerant, dry ice is classified as a dangerous good and must be declared and packaged accordingly
- **Exempt specimens**: samples that have a minimal likelihood of containing pathogens (e.g., dried blood spots for quality control, specimens in which pathogens have been neutralized) may qualify for exemption from dangerous goods regulations

### Triple Packaging System (UN 3373)

The triple packaging system is required for all Category B biological substances:

1. **Primary receptacle**: leak-proof container holding the specimen (tube, vial, bag); sealed with tape or parafilm
2. **Secondary packaging**: leak-proof container enclosing the primary receptacle(s); must contain sufficient absorbent material to absorb the entire contents of all primary receptacles in case of breakage
3. **Outer packaging**: rigid outer container meeting drop test requirements; minimum dimension 100 mm × 100 mm; bearing the UN 3373 diamond marking, shipper and consignee addresses, and emergency contact information

### Documentation Requirements

- **Shipping declaration**: not required for UN 3373 shipments but required for Category A
- **Specimen inventory**: detailed list of specimens including species, sample type, collection date, and preservative
- **CITES permits**: if the species is CITES-listed (see CITES chapter)
- **Import permits**: as required by the destination country's veterinary authority
- **Material safety data sheets**: for fixatives (formalin) and other hazardous chemicals included in shipment
- **Institutional material transfer agreements**: as required by participating institutions

### Cold Chain Management

Maintaining appropriate temperature during transport is critical for diagnostic integrity:

| Sample Type | Temperature Requirement | Shipping Method |
|-------------|------------------------|-----------------|
| Fresh tissue (histopathology) | 4°C (formalin-fixed: ambient) | Gel ice packs |
| Blood/serum/plasma (chemistry, serology) | 4°C (short-term); -20°C (long-term) | Gel ice packs or dry ice |
| Samples for viral isolation | -70°C or below | Dry ice (minimum 5 kg per 24 h transit) |
| Samples for bacterial culture | 4°C; transport within 24-48 h | Gel ice packs with transport media |
| Fecal samples (parasitology) | 4°C (fresh); ambient (formalin-fixed) | Gel ice packs or ambient |
| Genetic samples (DNA/RNA) | -20°C (DNA); -70°C (RNA) or RNA preservative (ambient) | Dry ice or RNAlater (ambient) |

## Disease Containment

### Outbreak Response Biosecurity

During wildlife disease outbreaks, enhanced biosecurity measures are essential:

- **Incident command structure**: establish clear roles, communication channels, and decision authority
- **Zoning**: define infected, buffer, and surveillance zones with controlled movement between them
- **Personnel restrictions**: limit the number of people entering infected areas; track all entries and exits
- **Equipment control**: dedicated equipment for each zone; no movement of equipment from infected to clean areas without decontamination
- **Carcass management**: safe collection, transport, and disposal of carcasses (incineration, deep burial, or rendering, depending on the pathogen)
- **Wildlife movement restrictions**: coordinate with wildlife management agencies to implement movement restrictions if warranted (e.g., translocation moratoriums during CWD outbreaks)

### Quarantine Protocols

- Newly admitted wildlife should be quarantined before introduction to resident populations
- Quarantine duration depends on the incubation period of diseases of concern (typically 30-90 days)
- Quarantine animals should be tested for relevant diseases before release from quarantine
- Staff should work with quarantine animals after attending to resident animals (directional workflow)
- Dedicated PPE and equipment for quarantine areas

## Zoonotic Risk Management

### Occupational Health Programs

Wildlife veterinary institutions should maintain formal occupational health programs:

- **Pre-employment health assessment**: baseline health screening, review of immunization status, assessment of risk factors (immunosuppression, pregnancy)
- **Immunizations**: rabies pre-exposure prophylaxis (essential for all wildlife workers handling mammals); tetanus; hepatitis B; influenza; other vaccinations based on risk assessment
- **Serum banking**: baseline serum samples stored for all personnel, with periodic updates; provides baseline for comparison if occupational exposure occurs
- **Exposure reporting system**: clear, accessible procedures for reporting bites, scratches, needlesticks, and mucosal exposures
- **Post-exposure protocols**: species-specific and pathogen-specific protocols immediately available (e.g., herpesvirus B exposure from macaque bite, etorphine needlestick)
- **Medical surveillance**: periodic health screening for personnel working with specific high-risk agents (e.g., tuberculosis screening for workers with non-human primates)

### High-Risk Exposures and Response

**Etorphine/carfentanil exposure:**
- Immediately administer naloxone (at least 2 mg IV/IM; may require repeated doses)
- Naloxone kits must be immediately available wherever potent opioids are in use
- All personnel must be trained to administer naloxone to an exposed colleague
- Buddy system mandatory: never work alone with potent opioids
- Transport to emergency medical facility even if naloxone reversal appears effective

**Rabies exposure:**
- Immediately wash wound with soap and water for minimum 15 minutes
- Apply povidone-iodine or equivalent virucidal agent
- Assess rabies vaccination status; administer post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) per current guidelines
- If the animal can be safely retained or submitted, arrange rabies testing
- Report to occupational health and public health authorities

**Needlestick or sharps injury:**
- Allow wound to bleed freely; wash with soap and water
- Assess the source material (species, disease status, drug involved)
- Seek immediate medical attention for injuries involving potent opioids, fixatives, or biological materials from high-risk species
- Document and report per institutional protocols

## Facility Biosecurity Levels

### Biosecurity Level Classification for Wildlife Facilities

Wildlife facilities can be classified by biosecurity level analogous to (but distinct from) laboratory biosafety levels:

**Level 1 — Basic:**
- Standard wildlife rehabilitation or holding facility
- Routine hygiene and disinfection protocols
- Basic PPE (gloves, coveralls)
- Appropriate for animals of known health status or low-risk species

**Level 2 — Enhanced:**
- Facilities handling animals of unknown disease status or animals from areas with known disease activity
- Enhanced PPE requirements (N95 respirators available, eye protection)
- Dedicated equipment for each area
- Directional workflow
- Quarantine capability
- Appropriate for most wildlife rehabilitation and research holding facilities

**Level 3 — Containment:**
- Facilities handling animals suspected or confirmed to be infected with serious zoonotic or wildlife pathogens
- Controlled access (authorized personnel only)
- Negative-pressure ventilation or HEPA filtration
- Dedicated PPE donning and doffing areas
- Shower-out facilities
- Full decontamination of all materials leaving the facility
- Appropriate for handling highly pathogenic avian influenza suspects, rabies suspects pending testing, and other high-consequence pathogens

**Level 4 — Maximum containment:**
- Rarely applicable to wildlife veterinary facilities
- Required for work with agents causing serious or lethal human disease for which no treatment or vaccine exists
- BSL-4 laboratory conditions with positive-pressure suits or Class III biological safety cabinets

### Facility Design Principles

Biosecurity should be integrated into facility design from the outset:

- Separate entrance and exit points to enforce directional workflow
- Boot wash stations or foot baths at all transition points
- Smooth, non-porous surfaces that can be easily cleaned and disinfected
- Adequate drainage with appropriate treatment of wastewater
- Separate ventilation systems for quarantine and isolation areas
- Storage for PPE at point of use
- Handwashing stations at all exits
- Secure storage for controlled substances and dangerous goods
- Adequate space for sample processing, packaging, and temporary storage
- Signage indicating biosecurity level and PPE requirements at all entry points
