# CITES: Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species

## Overview

The Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) is an international agreement between governments that aims to ensure international trade in specimens of wild animals and plants does not threaten their survival. Entered into force in 1975, CITES currently has 184 Parties (member states) and regulates trade in approximately 40,000 species. For the wildlife veterinarian, CITES compliance is a routine requirement whenever biological samples, live animals, or animal derivatives cross international borders.

CITES operates through a system of permits and certificates that must be obtained before trade in listed species is authorized. The Convention is administered internationally by the CITES Secretariat, housed within the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) in Geneva, while each Party designates a Management Authority (responsible for issuing permits) and a Scientific Authority (responsible for advising on the biological sustainability of trade).

## The Appendices

CITES species are listed on three appendices, each conferring a different level of trade restriction.

### Appendix I

Appendix I includes species threatened with extinction where trade is permitted only in exceptional circumstances. Commercial trade in wild-caught Appendix I specimens is generally prohibited.

- **Examples**: great apes (all *Hominidae*), Asian elephants (*Elephas maximus*), all rhinoceros species (*Rhinocerotidae*), tigers (*Panthera tigris*), snow leopards (*Panthera uncia*), most sea turtles (*Cheloniidae*, *Dermochelyidae*), several parrot species
- **Trade requirements**: both an export permit from the country of origin and an import permit from the country of destination are required
- **Non-detriment finding**: the Scientific Authority of the exporting state must confirm that export will not be detrimental to the survival of the species
- **Legal acquisition**: the specimen must have been legally acquired
- **Veterinary relevance**: biological samples (blood, tissue, feathers, feces) from Appendix I species require CITES permits for international transport, including shipment to reference laboratories in other countries

### Appendix II

Appendix II includes species that are not necessarily threatened with extinction but may become so unless trade is closely controlled. It also includes "look-alike" species that resemble listed species and require regulation to prevent misidentification.

- **Examples**: all primates not on Appendix I, all raptors (*Falconiformes*, *Strigiformes*) not on Appendix I, all parrots (*Psittaciformes*) not on Appendix I, most crocodilians, hippopotamus (*Hippopotamus amphibius*), many orchids and cacti
- **Trade requirements**: an export permit is required; no import permit is needed (though the importing country may impose additional requirements through domestic legislation)
- **Non-detriment finding**: required by the exporting country's Scientific Authority
- **Veterinary relevance**: the majority of species encountered in zoo and wildlife veterinary practice are listed on Appendix II

### Appendix III

Appendix III includes species listed by individual Parties that already regulate trade in those species within their jurisdiction and need cooperation from other Parties to prevent unsustainable or illegal trade.

- **Examples**: walrus (*Odobenus rosmarus*) listed by Canada, two-toed sloth (*Choloepus hoffmanni*) listed by Costa Rica
- **Trade requirements**: an export permit from the listing country, or a certificate of origin from non-listing countries
- **Veterinary relevance**: less commonly encountered in veterinary practice but still requires awareness during cross-border sample transport

## Permit Types and Documentation

### Standard Permits

| Permit Type | Purpose | Requirements |
|------------|---------|--------------|
| Export permit | Authorize export of CITES-listed specimens | Non-detriment finding, legal acquisition, appropriate housing (live animals) |
| Import permit | Required for Appendix I specimens entering a country | Confirmation that import is for non-detrimental purposes; adequate housing for live animals |
| Re-export certificate | Authorize re-export of previously imported specimens | Proof of legal import; original permit documentation |
| Introduction from the sea | Specimens taken from international waters | Non-detriment finding; applicable primarily to marine species |

### Special Certificates

- **Pre-Convention certificate** — for specimens acquired before CITES listing; requires proof of acquisition date
- **Bred-in-captivity certificate** — for specimens bred in captivity at facilities registered with the CITES Management Authority (Appendix I) or meeting breeding criteria (Appendix II)
- **Artificially propagated certificate** — the botanical equivalent of the bred-in-captivity certificate
- **Traveling exhibition certificate** — for live animals, including those in zoo exchange programs, circuses, and educational exhibits; allows multiple border crossings within a defined period
- **Musical instrument certificate** — for instruments containing CITES-listed materials (e.g., tortoiseshell, ivory, rosewood)

### Scientific Exchange Registration

Registered scientific institutions may benefit from simplified permit procedures under Article VII of the Convention. Institutions must be registered with the CITES Management Authority and may exchange herbarium specimens, preserved or dried museum specimens, and live plant material for non-commercial scientific research under a label system rather than full permit requirements.

**Note**: This exemption does not cover live animals, and veterinary diagnostic samples generally still require permits.

## Role of the Veterinarian in CITES Compliance

### Health Certificates for Live Animal Transport

CITES requires that live specimens be transported in a manner that minimizes the risk of injury, damage to health, or cruel treatment. The veterinarian's role includes:

- **Pre-shipment health evaluation** — examining the animal and certifying fitness for transport
- **Health certification** — providing official veterinary health certificates as required by the importing country
- **Compliance with IATA Live Animals Regulations (LAR)** — ensuring container specifications, labeling, and care during transport meet international standards
- **Disease testing** — performing required disease screening tests (e.g., tuberculosis testing for elephants, psittacosis testing for parrots)
- **Quarantine protocols** — advising on pre-export and post-import quarantine requirements

### Sample Transport Regulations

Biological samples from CITES-listed species require careful attention to both CITES permitting and biological material shipping regulations:

- **Blood, serum, and plasma samples** — CITES permits required if species is identifiable and sample crosses international borders
- **Tissue samples (biopsy, necropsy)** — same permit requirements; additional biosafety packaging requirements under UN 3373
- **Fecal samples** — CITES status varies by jurisdiction; some Management Authorities consider fecal samples exempt if the species cannot be identified from the sample, but most require permits as a precaution
- **Feathers, hair, and skin** — always require CITES permits as they are readily identifiable specimens
- **Genetic material (DNA extracts)** — the CITES status of extracted DNA is debated; many Parties treat purified DNA as exempt, but others require permits; always verify with the relevant Management Authority

### Specimen Documentation

Every CITES specimen must be traceable through a chain of documentation:

1. **Source documentation** — records establishing legal acquisition (capture permits, collection permits, breeding records, import permits)
2. **Institutional records** — accession numbers, studbook entries, or specimen catalogs linking the individual or sample to its source documentation
3. **Permit applications** — accurate species identification (scientific name), specimen description, quantity, and purpose of trade
4. **Permit copies** — retained by the exporting institution, importing institution, and customs authorities at both borders
5. **Return documentation** — for loaned specimens, documentation of return to the originating country

### Common Compliance Pitfalls

Wildlife veterinarians should be aware of frequent compliance failures:

- **Failing to obtain permits for diagnostic samples** — a common oversight; even a blood tube sent to a laboratory in another country requires a CITES permit if the species is listed
- **Incorrect species identification on permits** — taxonomic revisions may change the species name or CITES listing; always verify current nomenclature
- **Exceeding permit validity** — CITES permits are typically valid for six months; samples must be shipped within this window
- **Inadequate descriptions** — permit applications must accurately describe the specimen type, quantity, and container
- **Assuming exemptions** — veterinarians sometimes assume that diagnostic samples, scientific exchanges, or small quantities are exempt; exemptions are narrow and jurisdiction-specific

## Enforcement and Penalties

CITES violations are prosecuted under national implementing legislation. Penalties vary by jurisdiction but can be severe:

- **United States** (Endangered Species Act, Lacey Act): fines up to $50,000 per violation; imprisonment up to one year (felony violations under the Lacey Act carry penalties up to $250,000 and five years imprisonment)
- **European Union** (EU Wildlife Trade Regulations): seizure of specimens, fines, and criminal prosecution under member state laws
- **United Kingdom** (Control of Trade in Endangered Species Regulations): fines and imprisonment up to five years
- **Australia** (Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act): fines up to AUD 210,000 for individuals; imprisonment up to ten years

### Institutional Consequences

Beyond individual penalties, CITES violations can result in:

- Suspension or revocation of institutional permits
- Loss of CITES-registered institution status
- Reputational damage to the institution and its staff
- Suspension of a country's CITES trade (in cases of systemic non-compliance, the CITES Standing Committee may recommend trade suspensions)

## Practical Recommendations for Veterinarians

1. **Maintain a current list of CITES-listed species** in your care or likely to be encountered in your practice; the CITES Species+ database (speciesplus.net) is the authoritative reference
2. **Establish a working relationship** with your national CITES Management Authority before you need a permit
3. **Build permit application timelines** into research and clinical protocols; CITES permits may take weeks to months to process
4. **Keep meticulous records** of all biological samples, their source animals, and their disposition
5. **Consult with CITES authorities** when in doubt about the permit status of any sample or specimen
6. **Train laboratory and veterinary staff** on CITES requirements relevant to their role in sample handling and shipping
7. **Review CITES Resolutions and Decisions** periodically for changes affecting your species of interest; amendments to the appendices are adopted at triennial Conferences of the Parties (CoPs)
8. **Use the CITES Trade Database** (trade.cites.org) to monitor trade patterns in species of concern and to contextualize your institution's activities within broader trade trends
