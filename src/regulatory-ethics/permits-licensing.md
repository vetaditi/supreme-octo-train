# Permits and Licensing

## Overview

Virtually every aspect of wildlife veterinary practice requires some form of permit, license, or authorization. The permit landscape is layered and complex: a single capture operation on a federally listed species may require simultaneous authorization from federal wildlife agencies, state wildlife agencies, institutional animal care committees, controlled substance registrations, and facility licenses. The wildlife veterinarian must understand not only which permits are required but also the application timelines, renewal schedules, reporting obligations, and conditions attached to each authorization.

Failure to obtain required permits before undertaking wildlife veterinary activities constitutes a violation of law, regardless of the veterinarian's intent or the conservation value of the work. Permits must be in hand — not merely applied for — before regulated activities begin.

## Wildlife Rehabilitation Permits

### Federal Permits (United States)

Wildlife rehabilitation of federally protected species requires authorization from the US Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS):

- **Migratory Bird Rehabilitation Permit** — required for any person or facility providing care to injured, sick, or orphaned migratory birds; issued under 50 CFR 21.76
  - Applicants must demonstrate training and experience in wildlife rehabilitation
  - Facilities must meet minimum housing and care standards
  - Annual reporting of species received, outcomes (released, died, euthanized, transferred, retained as educational bird), and disposition is mandatory
  - Sub-permits may be issued for veterinarians working under a permitted facility
  - Eagles require a separate or additional authorization under the Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act

- **Endangered Species Permit (Section 10)** — required for rehabilitation of ESA-listed species
  - Applications reviewed for scientific justification and qualifications of the applicant
  - Processing times may exceed six months; emergency situations may be covered under existing agency authorizations

- **Marine Mammal Stranding Agreement** — organizations authorized to respond to marine mammal strandings enter into formal agreements with NMFS or USFWS
  - Veterinarians participating in stranding response must operate under these agreements
  - Stranding agreements specify authorized activities, species, geographic area, and reporting requirements

### State Permits

Every US state and most Canadian provinces require separate wildlife rehabilitation permits:

- Requirements vary enormously between jurisdictions, from minimal (self-certification with basic training) to extensive (examination, mentorship, facility inspection)
- Some states require direct veterinary supervision of rehabilitation activities; others require only periodic veterinary consultation
- State permits typically cover resident (non-migratory) species not protected under federal law
- Many states classify rehabilitation permits by experience level (e.g., basic, intermediate, advanced) with increasing species authorizations at each level

### International Rehabilitation Frameworks

- **United Kingdom**: wildlife rehabilitation is regulated under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981; no formal permit system exists, but possession of protected species for rehabilitation must be justified and documented
- **Australia**: state and territory wildlife authorities issue rehabilitation permits; requirements include training through approved courses, facility standards, and annual reporting
- **European Union**: member states regulate rehabilitation through national implementing legislation under the EU Birds and Habitats Directives; permit requirements vary significantly among member states

## Research Permits

### Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC)

In the United States, any research involving vertebrate animals conducted at institutions receiving federal funding must be approved by an IACUC. Similar requirements exist in other countries under different names:

- **United States**: IACUC review required under the Animal Welfare Act and the Public Health Service Policy on Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals
- **Canada**: Canadian Council on Animal Care (CCAC) oversees institutional animal care committees
- **Australia**: Animal Ethics Committees operate under the Australian Code for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes
- **European Union**: Directive 2010/63/EU requires ethical review of all animal research

#### IACUC Protocol Components for Wildlife Research

A wildlife research IACUC protocol must address:

| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| Justification | Scientific rationale for using wildlife; explanation of why the questions cannot be answered without animal use |
| Species and numbers | Target species, estimated sample size, and statistical justification for sample size |
| Capture methods | Physical and chemical capture techniques, including drug protocols and delivery methods |
| Handling procedures | Restraint methods, sampling procedures, marking techniques, and duration of handling |
| Anesthesia and analgesia | Drug protocols, monitoring plans, and reversal agents |
| Potential harms | Anticipated morbidity, mortality, and disturbance effects |
| Minimization measures | Steps taken to minimize pain, distress, and lasting harm |
| Endpoint criteria | Criteria for intervention, removal from study, or euthanasia |
| Personnel qualifications | Training and experience of all personnel involved in animal procedures |
| Veterinary oversight | Identified attending veterinarian with authority to intervene |
| Disposition | Fate of animals after study completion (release, continued monitoring, euthanasia) |

### Federal and State Research Permits

Research on protected species requires permits independent of IACUC approval:

- **USFWS Scientific Collecting Permit** — authorizes collection or handling of migratory birds for scientific purposes
- **USFWS ESA Section 10(a)(1)(A) permit** — authorizes take of endangered or threatened species for scientific research
- **NMFS/USFWS MMPA permit** — authorizes research involving marine mammals
- **State scientific collecting permits** — required for research on state-managed wildlife species
- **Bird banding permits** — issued by the USGS Bird Banding Laboratory; required for any bird banding or marking activity

Research permits specify authorized species, methods, numbers, locations, and time periods. All conditions must be strictly observed, and annual reports are typically required.

## Import and Export Permits

### CITES Permits

As detailed in the CITES chapter, international transport of specimens from CITES-listed species requires export permits, import permits (Appendix I), and/or re-export certificates from national CITES Management Authorities.

### USDA-APHIS Permits

The US Department of Agriculture's Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service regulates the import of animals and animal products to prevent the introduction of foreign animal diseases:

- **VS Permit 17-129** — required for importation of live animals, animal semen, or embryos
- **CDC Import Permit** — required for certain animal-origin materials that pose zoonotic disease risks (e.g., non-human primate materials, bat specimens)
- **Veterinary health certificates** — required for live animal transport; issued by accredited veterinarians in the country of origin and endorsed by the national veterinary authority

### Biological Material Shipping

Transport of biological samples across international borders requires attention to multiple regulatory frameworks simultaneously:

1. **CITES permits** (if the species is listed)
2. **USDA/APHIS import permits** (if entering the US)
3. **CDC import permits** (for certain zoonotic agents)
4. **Nagoya Protocol compliance** (for genetic resources)
5. **Institutional material transfer agreements** (between research institutions)
6. **Shipping regulations** (IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations, DOT, UN 3373)

## DEA Scheduling and Controlled Substances

Several of the most important drugs in wildlife immobilization are classified as controlled substances under the US Controlled Substances Act and equivalent legislation in other countries.

### Schedule II Substances in Wildlife Medicine

| Drug | DEA Schedule | Primary Use | Special Requirements |
|------|-------------|-------------|---------------------|
| Etorphine (M99) | Schedule II (with additional DEA-999 registration) | Large mammal immobilization | DEA Form 222 for ordering; separate locked storage; detailed use records; only DEA-registered researchers may possess |
| Carfentanil | Schedule II | Large mammal immobilization (use declining due to human safety concerns) | Same as etorphine; extreme human toxicity requires naloxone availability and buddy system |
| Thiafentanil (A-3080) | Schedule II | Ungulate immobilization | Same requirements as etorphine |
| Tiletamine-zolazepam (Telazol) | Schedule III | Broad-spectrum immobilization | Standard Schedule III controls; DEA registration required |
| Ketamine | Schedule III | Widely used in wildlife anesthesia | Standard Schedule III controls |
| Medetomidine/dexmedetomidine | Not scheduled (US) | Sedation and co-immobilization | Not a controlled substance in the US but may be scheduled in other jurisdictions |

### DEA-999 Registration

The use of etorphine, carfentanil, and diprenorphine (the reversal agent for etorphine) requires a special DEA registration (DEA-999) in addition to the standard DEA registration held by veterinarians:

- Only practitioners engaged in wildlife immobilization or research may apply
- Application requires documentation of training and experience in the use of potent opioids
- Separate locked storage (double-lock system) is required
- Detailed records of every milligram obtained, used, and disposed of must be maintained
- Loss or theft must be reported to the DEA immediately
- Biennial inventory is required
- State-level controlled substance registrations may impose additional requirements

### International Controlled Substance Regulations

- **United Kingdom**: etorphine classified under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971; strict protocols govern procurement, storage, use, and disposal
- **South Africa**: Schedule 7 (most restrictive) classification for potent opioids; provincial permits required
- **Australia**: state and territory legislation governs controlled substances; national coordination through the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA)
- **EU member states**: national controlled substance laws implement the UN Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs; requirements vary by country

## Veterinary Practice Across Jurisdictions

### Licensure Requirements

Veterinary practice is regulated at the state or provincial level in most countries, creating challenges for wildlife veterinarians who may work across multiple jurisdictions:

- **United States**: each state issues its own veterinary license; practicing in a state without holding that state's license constitutes unlicensed practice of veterinary medicine
- **Temporary practice permits**: some states issue temporary or limited licenses for consultations, emergency response, or continuing education events
- **USDA accreditation**: veterinarians issuing interstate or international health certificates must hold USDA accreditation, obtained through training and application to APHIS-Veterinary Services
- **State veterinarian authority**: state veterinarians may authorize emergency veterinary activities (e.g., during disease outbreaks) that would otherwise require individual state licensure

### Multi-Jurisdictional Practice Solutions

- **License reciprocity agreements**: some states have reciprocal licensing agreements that streamline the process
- **Federal employment**: veterinarians employed by federal agencies (USFWS, USDA, USGS) may practice under federal authority across state lines in the course of their official duties
- **Emergency declarations**: during wildlife disease outbreaks or natural disasters, emergency declarations may temporarily suspend state licensing requirements
- **Telehealth and teleconsultation**: the legal status of providing veterinary advice across state lines via telecommunications is evolving and varies by jurisdiction

### Good Samaritan Provisions

Some jurisdictions provide limited legal protection for veterinarians who provide emergency care to wildlife outside their normal scope of practice or geographic license:

- These provisions vary enormously in scope and should not be relied upon without legal review
- They typically cover only emergency situations where delay would result in animal death or suffering
- They do not exempt the veterinarian from the requirement to meet the standard of care

## Permit Management Best Practices

### Organizational Systems

Effective permit management requires systematic organization:

- Maintain a centralized permit database listing all active permits, their conditions, expiration dates, and reporting deadlines
- Designate a permit coordinator responsible for monitoring renewals and compliance
- Keep copies of all permits accessible at every location where regulated activities occur
- Distribute permit conditions to all personnel involved in permitted activities

### Compliance Monitoring

- Conduct internal compliance audits at least annually
- Reconcile controlled substance inventories with use records quarterly
- Submit required reports before deadlines (late reporting is a common compliance failure)
- Document any deviations from permit conditions and report to the issuing authority as required

### Application Timelines

Permit processing times vary dramatically and must be factored into project planning:

| Permit Type | Typical Processing Time |
|-------------|------------------------|
| State rehabilitation permit | 2–8 weeks |
| USFWS migratory bird permit | 4–12 weeks |
| ESA Section 10 permit | 6–18 months |
| MMPA research permit | 6–24 months |
| IACUC protocol approval | 4–12 weeks |
| CITES export permit | 2–8 weeks |
| DEA-999 registration | 4–12 weeks |
| USDA import permit | 2–6 weeks |

These timelines are approximate and can vary significantly based on the complexity of the application, the workload of the reviewing agency, and the completeness of the initial submission. Incomplete applications are the most common cause of delayed processing.
