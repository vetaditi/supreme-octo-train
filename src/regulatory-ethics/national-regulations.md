# National and Regional Wildlife Regulations

## Overview

While international treaties such as CITES and the Convention on Biological Diversity establish overarching frameworks, the practical regulatory environment for wildlife veterinarians is primarily defined by national and regional legislation. These laws determine which species are protected, what activities require permits, how diseases must be reported, and what penalties apply for violations. Wildlife veterinarians must be conversant with the regulations of every jurisdiction in which they practice, as requirements vary significantly between countries and, in many cases, between states, provinces, or territories within a single nation.

## United States Federal Legislation

The United States maintains one of the most complex wildlife regulatory frameworks in the world, with overlapping federal, state, and tribal jurisdictions.

### Endangered Species Act (ESA, 1973)

The ESA is the primary federal statute protecting imperiled species and their habitats.

- **Listing process**: species are listed as endangered or threatened based on biological criteria assessed by the US Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) or the National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS) for marine and anadromous species
- **Section 9 (Take prohibition)**: it is unlawful to "take" any endangered species, where "take" is broadly defined to include harass, harm, pursue, hunt, shoot, wound, kill, trap, capture, or collect
- **Section 10 permits**: permits may be issued for scientific research, enhancement of propagation or survival, and incidental take associated with otherwise lawful activities
- **Section 7 consultation**: federal agencies must consult with USFWS/NMFS to ensure their actions do not jeopardize listed species or destroy critical habitat
- **Veterinary implications**: any veterinary intervention on an ESA-listed species — including capture, sampling, treatment, or euthanasia — requires a Section 10 permit or must occur under the authority of an existing permit held by the responsible agency or institution
- **Critical habitat**: designation of critical habitat imposes additional regulatory requirements on activities within those areas
- **Recovery plans**: the USFWS develops recovery plans for listed species that may include specific veterinary protocols for health monitoring, disease management, and captive breeding

### Marine Mammal Protection Act (MMPA, 1972)

The MMPA prohibits the take of all marine mammals in US waters and by US citizens on the high seas, with limited exceptions.

- **General moratorium**: all marine mammals are protected regardless of population status; this is broader than the ESA, which protects only listed species
- **Take definition**: includes harassment, hunting, capture, or killing; harassment is further divided into Level A (potential to injure) and Level B (potential to disturb)
- **Permits**: scientific research permits are issued by NMFS (cetaceans, pinnipeds except walrus) or USFWS (walrus, sea otter, manatee, polar bear)
- **Stranding response**: the MMPA authorizes response to stranded marine mammals under agreements between NMFS/USFWS and authorized stranding network organizations; veterinarians participating in stranding response must operate under these agreements
- **Deterrence**: certain non-injurious deterrence methods are permitted to protect personal safety, property, and fishing gear
- **Veterinary implications**: all veterinary contact with marine mammals — including health assessments during research captures, stranding response, and rehabilitation — requires MMPA authorization

### Migratory Bird Treaty Act (MBTA, 1918)

The MBTA implements treaties with Canada, Mexico, Japan, and Russia for the protection of migratory birds.

- **Scope**: protects over 1,000 bird species that migrate between the US and treaty partner nations
- **Prohibitions**: unlawful to pursue, hunt, take, capture, kill, or possess any migratory bird, their parts, nests, or eggs without authorization
- **Permits**: the USFWS issues permits for scientific collecting, banding, rehabilitation, and other activities; permits specify authorized species, methods, and numbers
- **Rehabilitation**: wildlife rehabilitation of migratory birds requires a federal MBTA permit in addition to any state rehabilitation permit
- **Veterinary implications**: veterinary treatment of any migratory bird requires that the treating facility or individual operates under appropriate MBTA authorization; even temporary possession for treatment requires permit coverage

### Lacey Act (1900, amended 1981, 2008)

The Lacey Act is the oldest federal wildlife protection statute in the United States and functions as an enforcement backstop for other wildlife laws.

- **Trafficking provisions**: prohibits trade in wildlife that has been illegally taken, transported, or sold in violation of any federal, state, tribal, or foreign law
- **Marking and labeling**: requires accurate marking and labeling of wildlife shipments in interstate and foreign commerce
- **Penalties**: felony violations (involving species with a market value exceeding $350 or involving the sale of wildlife) carry fines up to $250,000 and imprisonment up to five years
- **Veterinary implications**: a veterinarian who knowingly accepts, treats, or facilitates the transfer of illegally obtained wildlife may be subject to Lacey Act prosecution; this underscores the importance of verifying the legal provenance of wildlife patients

### Additional Federal Statutes

- **Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act (BGEPA)**: provides additional protections for bald and golden eagles beyond the MBTA; permits required for any take, including disturbance
- **Wild Bird Conservation Act (WBCA)**: regulates import of CITES-listed birds to ensure wild-caught bird trade is sustainable
- **Animal Welfare Act (AWA)**: administered by USDA-APHIS; regulates warm-blooded animals used in research, exhibition, and commerce, including wildlife in captive settings; does not apply to free-ranging wildlife
- **National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA)**: requires environmental review of federal actions that may affect wildlife; may require veterinary input on wildlife health impacts

## European Union Regulations

### EU Habitats Directive (92/43/EEC)

The Habitats Directive is the cornerstone of EU nature conservation legislation.

- **Annex II**: species requiring designation of Special Areas of Conservation (SACs)
- **Annex IV**: species requiring strict protection throughout the EU; prohibits deliberate capture, killing, or disturbance, and destruction of breeding sites
- **Annex V**: species whose exploitation may be managed through regulatory measures
- **Article 16 derogations**: member states may grant derogations from strict protection for reasons including public health, research, and preventing serious damage, provided there is no satisfactory alternative and the derogation does not harm the species' conservation status
- **Veterinary implications**: interventions on Annex IV species (e.g., sampling, treatment, translocation) require derogation authorization from national competent authorities

### EU Birds Directive (2009/147/EC)

- Protects all wild bird species naturally occurring in the EU
- Prohibits deliberate killing, capture, egg collection, and nest destruction
- Designates Special Protection Areas (SPAs) for particularly vulnerable species (Annex I)
- Member states may grant derogations under specified conditions

### EU Wildlife Trade Regulations

The EU implements CITES through a set of Council and Commission Regulations that are, in some respects, stricter than CITES itself:

- **Annex A** (approximately equivalent to CITES Appendix I, with additional species): commercial trade generally prohibited
- **Annex B** (approximately equivalent to CITES Appendix II): import permit required in addition to export permit
- **Annex C and D**: monitoring and reporting requirements for additional species

## National Wildlife Health Authorities

Most countries maintain national agencies or institutions dedicated to wildlife health:

| Country/Region | Primary Authority | Key Functions |
|---------------|-------------------|---------------|
| United States | USGS National Wildlife Health Center | Disease investigation, diagnostic services, research |
| Canada | Canadian Wildlife Health Cooperative (CWHC) | National surveillance, diagnostics, education |
| United Kingdom | Animal and Plant Health Agency (APHA) | Disease investigation, statutory disease control |
| Australia | Wildlife Health Australia | National coordination, surveillance, information |
| European Union | European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) | Risk assessment, disease monitoring coordination |
| New Zealand | Ministry for Primary Industries (MPI) | Biosecurity, disease investigation |
| South Africa | State Veterinary Services / SANBI | Wildlife disease control, biodiversity management |

Wildlife veterinarians must know how to contact and report to the relevant authority in their jurisdiction, particularly for notifiable diseases and mass mortality events.

## State, Provincial, and Regional Regulations

### United States State Regulations

In the US, states retain primary authority over resident (non-migratory) wildlife through the public trust doctrine. State regulations commonly address:

- **Wildlife rehabilitation licensing** — most states require a specific rehabilitation permit; requirements for veterinary involvement vary from mandatory veterinary oversight to none
- **Game species management** — hunting seasons, bag limits, and methods of take for game species
- **Non-game and endangered species** — state endangered species lists often include species not listed under the federal ESA
- **Captive wildlife permits** — requirements for possessing, breeding, or exhibiting wildlife in captivity
- **Disease reporting** — some states have specific wildlife disease reporting requirements in addition to federal obligations
- **Veterinary practice acts** — state veterinary boards regulate the practice of veterinary medicine, including provisions for wildlife-related practice and emergency care

### Canadian Provincial Regulations

Canadian provinces and territories manage wildlife through their own wildlife acts, with federal oversight for species at risk (Species at Risk Act, SARA) and migratory birds (Migratory Birds Convention Act). Provincial regulations address rehabilitation permits, research permits, and wildlife possession.

### EU Member State Implementation

EU Directives are transposed into national legislation by each member state, resulting in variation in implementation, permitting procedures, and enforcement across the EU.

## Disease Reporting Requirements

### International Reporting

- **WOAH (World Organisation for Animal Health)** — member countries are obligated to report listed diseases in wildlife through the World Animal Health Information System (WAHIS); diseases are classified as notifiable (immediate reporting) or reportable (periodic reporting)
- **WOAH-listed wildlife diseases** include highly pathogenic avian influenza, rabies, anthrax, African swine fever, foot-and-mouth disease, chronic wasting disease, and others

### National Reporting

- **United States**: no single mandatory wildlife disease reporting system exists at the federal level; however, specific diseases are reportable to USDA-APHIS (foreign animal diseases, including highly pathogenic avian influenza), to CDC (zoonotic diseases), and to state agencies (varies by state)
- **Canada**: the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) manages reporting of federally reportable diseases; provinces have additional requirements
- **European Union**: member states report through the Animal Disease Information System (ADIS) and comply with the EU Animal Health Law (Regulation 2016/429)
- **United Kingdom**: post-Brexit, the UK maintains its own reporting framework through APHA, with specific requirements for notifiable diseases in wildlife
- **Australia**: the National Notifiable Disease Surveillance System (NNDSS) covers certain zoonotic diseases; wildlife-specific reporting is coordinated through Wildlife Health Australia

### Veterinarian's Duty to Report

In most jurisdictions, veterinarians have a legal obligation to report suspected or confirmed cases of notifiable diseases. Failure to report can result in professional sanctions and legal penalties. The specific reporting obligations, timelines, and contact points vary by jurisdiction, and wildlife veterinarians must familiarize themselves with local requirements.

## Practical Considerations

### Navigating Multi-Jurisdictional Compliance

Wildlife veterinarians working across jurisdictions — particularly those involved in translocation, research, or consulting — must account for:

- Federal, state/provincial, and sometimes municipal regulations that may apply simultaneously
- Tribal sovereignty in the US, where tribal wildlife management operates independently of state authority on tribal lands
- Maritime jurisdiction, where different laws apply in territorial waters, exclusive economic zones, and the high seas
- Cross-border research collaborations requiring harmonization of permits from multiple national authorities

### Record-Keeping and Documentation

Regulatory compliance depends on thorough documentation:

- Permit numbers and conditions for every animal handled
- Species identification verified by qualified personnel
- Location and date of capture, sampling, or treatment
- Disposition of every animal (released, transferred, euthanized, died)
- Chain of custody for all biological samples
- Copies of all permits and their conditions readily accessible at the point of use

### Staying Current

Regulations change frequently. Wildlife veterinarians should:

- Subscribe to regulatory agency mailing lists and newsletters
- Attend relevant professional conferences (Wildlife Disease Association, American Association of Zoo Veterinarians)
- Maintain professional networks that include regulatory personnel
- Review regulations before initiating any new project or activity
- Consult with institutional compliance offices and legal counsel when uncertain
