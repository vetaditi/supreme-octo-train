# Field Necropsy

## When to Perform Field vs. Laboratory Necropsy

The decision to perform a necropsy in the field versus transporting the carcass to a laboratory depends on several factors:

### Indications for Field Necropsy

- Carcass is too large or too decomposed to transport practically
- Remote location makes transport logistically impossible or cost-prohibitive
- Multiple carcasses during a mass mortality event require rapid assessment
- Preliminary examination is needed to guide further investigation
- Samples must be collected fresh for specific diagnostic tests (e.g., microbiology, virology)
- Regulatory restrictions prevent carcass transport (e.g., chronic wasting disease management zones)

### Indications for Laboratory Necropsy

- Carcass is small enough to transport intact and in fresh condition
- Forensic investigation is suspected or confirmed (laboratory provides controlled conditions and proper evidence handling)
- Specialized equipment or expertise is needed (e.g., radiology, advanced imaging)
- Environmental conditions in the field preclude adequate examination (extreme weather, safety hazards)
- Histopathology-quality tissue fixation requires controlled conditions

### Hybrid Approach

In many situations, a partial field examination with targeted sample collection is combined with subsequent laboratory analysis of samples. This approach maximizes diagnostic yield when full laboratory necropsy is not feasible.

## Equipment Checklist

### Essential Field Necropsy Kit

**Cutting instruments**:
- Scalpel handles (#4 and #3) with disposable blades (#22, #10)
- Sharpening steel or replacement blades
- Heavy-duty scissors (Mayo, Metzenbaum)
- Bone-cutting shears or pruning shears for ribs
- Stryker saw or hand saw for craniotomy (battery-powered reciprocating saw for large animals)
- Knife with fixed blade (6-8 inch) for large animal skinning and organ removal

**Forceps and probes**:
- Rat-tooth tissue forceps
- Dressing forceps (smooth)
- Hemostats (for clamping intestines)
- Blunt probe
- Scissors with blunt tips for intestinal opening

**Sample collection supplies**:
- Whirl-Pak bags (assorted sizes)
- Cryovials and liquid nitrogen dewar (or dry ice cooler)
- 10% neutral buffered formalin in leak-proof containers
- Histology cassettes
- Sterile swabs with transport media (bacterial, viral)
- Vacutainer tubes (red top, purple top) for blood/fluid collection
- Microscope slides and coverslips
- Fecal collection containers
- Zip-lock bags
- Permanent markers and waterproof labels
- Aluminum foil (for toxicology samples; avoid plastic for some analytes)

**Documentation**:
- Necropsy report forms (waterproof paper or tablet with waterproof case)
- Camera with macro capability and scale bars
- GPS unit or GPS-enabled device
- Ruler and/or measuring tape
- Whiteboard or ID cards for photo identification

**Personal protective equipment (PPE)** (see dedicated section below)

**Miscellaneous**:
- Spring scale or hanging scale
- Headlamp or portable light source
- Cutting board or clean surface
- Heavy-duty garbage bags for waste disposal
- Paper towels and absorbent pads
- Spray bottles with disinfectant
- Sharps container
- Cooler with ice packs for sample transport

## Environmental Considerations

### Site Selection

When choosing where to perform a field necropsy:

- Move the carcass to a flat, sheltered area if possible (shade reduces examiner heat stress and slows decomposition)
- Position upwind of any odors
- Avoid performing necropsies near water sources to prevent contamination
- Consider drainage: do not allow fluids to run into streams, ponds, or groundwater
- Ensure vehicle access for equipment and sample transport
- Be aware of scavenger activity and secure the carcass before examination

### Weather Considerations

- **Rain**: Use a tarp or canopy to protect the work area. Wet conditions compromise sample quality and documentation.
- **Extreme heat**: Prioritize sample collection. Autolysis accelerates rapidly in warm conditions. Begin with microbiology samples and tissues for molecular diagnostics.
- **Extreme cold**: Frozen carcasses must be thawed before examination. Partial thawing in the field is possible but complete thawing in a controlled environment is preferred.
- **Wind**: Secure paperwork and light equipment. Windborne dust and debris contaminate samples. Consider wind direction for aerosol hazards.

### Biosecurity at the Field Site

- Minimize the area disturbed by the necropsy
- Contain all fluids and tissues; do not leave biological material in the environment
- Disinfect the work area upon completion
- Dispose of contaminated materials properly (double-bag, transport to appropriate waste facility)
- If a reportable or highly contagious disease is suspected, restrict access to the site and consult relevant authorities

## Documentation

### Photography

Photographic documentation is essential for every field necropsy:

**Standard photo sequence**:
1. Carcass in situ (as found, before manipulation), with GPS coordinates recorded
2. Surroundings and habitat context
3. Identification information (whiteboard with case number, species, date, location)
4. Whole body views from multiple angles (dorsal, ventral, lateral)
5. Close-up of any external lesions with scale reference
6. Body cavity upon opening
7. Individual organs in situ and after removal
8. Any abnormalities or lesions at close range with scale
9. Samples collected (for chain of custody in forensic cases)

**Photography tips**:
- Always include a scale reference (ruler, coin, or photographic scale bar)
- Photograph before and after cleaning or manipulating tissues
- Use macro mode for close-up lesion photography
- Ensure adequate lighting; use flash or supplemental light if needed
- Take more photographs than you think necessary; storage is cheap, revisiting a carcass is not

### GPS Documentation

Record GPS coordinates for:
- Carcass location (as found)
- Necropsy site (if different from where the carcass was found)
- Any environmental features of interest (water sources, roads, agricultural land, bait stations)

### Written Records

Complete the necropsy report form at the time of examination. Essential elements:

- Case identification number
- Date and time of examination
- Species, age class, sex
- Location (GPS coordinates, description)
- Finder information and circumstances of discovery
- Estimated time since death
- Carcass condition code
- External examination findings
- Internal examination findings
- Samples collected (type, number, preservation method, destination)
- Preliminary assessment of cause of death
- Examiner name and contact information

## Carcass Condition Assessment

Assessing the degree of decomposition is critical for determining which diagnostic tests will be informative and for interpreting findings:

### Condition Code System

| Code | Condition | Description | Diagnostic Utility |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Live/euthanized | Animal alive or recently euthanized | All diagnostics available |
| 1 | Fresh | No decomposition; eyes clear; no bloating; rigor may be present | Excellent for all diagnostics |
| 2 | Early decomposition | Mild bloating; eyes cloudy; skin intact; slight odor | Good for most diagnostics; some bacterial culture results may be compromised |
| 3 | Moderate decomposition | Marked bloating; skin discoloration; moderate odor; organs softened | Histopathology may be compromised; toxicology, parasitology, some molecular diagnostics still useful |
| 4 | Advanced decomposition | Severe bloating or deflation; skin slippage; strong odor; organs liquefying | Limited diagnostic utility; toxicology of bone/feather/hair, parasitology, some molecular diagnostics may still be useful |
| 5 | Skeletal/mummified | Skeleton with or without dried tissue remnants | Morphological identification, some toxicology (bone lead), stable isotope analysis |

### Factors Affecting Decomposition Rate

- **Temperature**: Decomposition proceeds approximately twice as fast for every 10 degrees C increase
- **Humidity**: High humidity accelerates decomposition; arid conditions can lead to mummification
- **Insect access**: Fly larvae (maggots) dramatically accelerate soft tissue decomposition
- **Scavenger activity**: Scavenging can obscure cause of death and remove diagnostic tissues
- **Body size**: Large animals retain core body heat longer, accelerating internal decomposition
- **Submersion**: Water immersion accelerates decomposition (warm water) or slows it (cold water); produces distinctive changes (adipocere formation)
- **Cause of death**: Septicemic animals decompose more rapidly due to bacterial colonization of tissues at the time of death

## Personal Protective Equipment

### Standard PPE for Wildlife Necropsy

| PPE Item | Purpose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrile or latex gloves (double-gloved) | Barrier protection from pathogens and chemicals | Change outer gloves frequently; have ample supply |
| Disposable coveralls or dedicated scrubs | Protect clothing; prevent carrying pathogens away from site | Tyvek coveralls are lightweight and effective |
| Rubber boots or waterproof boot covers | Protect feet; decontamination | Dedicated necropsy boots are preferable |
| Eye protection (safety glasses or goggles) | Protect from splash and aerosol | Splash-proof goggles for high-risk necropsies |
| N95 or higher respirator | Protect from aerosols and zoonotic pathogens | Required when tuberculosis, tularemia, avian influenza, or fungal agents are suspected |
| Face shield | Additional splash protection | Use over N95 for high-risk procedures |
| Cut-resistant glove liners | Protect against scalpel and bone injuries | Wear between inner and outer nitrile gloves |

### Enhanced PPE for High-Risk Necropsies

When highly pathogenic or zoonotic agents are suspected (e.g., highly pathogenic avian influenza, rabies, anthrax, plague, hantavirus):

- Powered air-purifying respirator (PAPR) or fitted N95 minimum
- Fluid-resistant coveralls with sealed cuffs
- Double gloving with taped glove-sleeve junctions
- Dedicated boot covers taped to coveralls
- Strict decontamination protocol before doffing

### Zoonotic Disease Awareness

Wildlife veterinarians performing necropsies should be current on:

- Pre-exposure vaccinations (rabies, tetanus at minimum; consider plague, tularemia for high-risk work)
- Recognition of zoonotic disease symptoms in themselves
- Reporting protocols for occupational exposure incidents
- Location of nearest medical facility and relevant exposure treatment protocols

## Decontamination

### Equipment Decontamination

After completing a field necropsy:

1. Remove gross organic material from all instruments and surfaces
2. Wash instruments in warm soapy water
3. Disinfect instruments with appropriate agent:
   - General use: 10% bleach solution (1:10 dilution of household bleach), 10-minute contact time
   - Mycobacteria: Phenolic disinfectant or 2% glutaraldehyde
   - Prions (CWD): 40% bleach (1:2.5 dilution) for 1 hour, or 1-2N NaOH for 1 hour
   - Avian influenza: Quaternary ammonium compounds, Virkon S, or bleach
4. Rinse and dry instruments
5. Sharpen scalpel blades or replace

### Personal Decontamination

- Remove PPE in the correct sequence to avoid self-contamination: gloves last (outer), then inner gloves
- Dispose of single-use PPE in biohazard bags
- Wash hands and forearms thoroughly with soap and water
- Shower as soon as practical after returning from the field
- Launder field clothing separately from personal clothing

### Site Decontamination

- Remove all biological material and waste from the necropsy site
- Disinfect the work surface if a table or board was used
- For highly contagious diseases, consider lime application to the soil surface
- Double-bag all waste material for proper disposal
- Document decontamination procedures performed
