# Necropsy and Pathology

## Importance of Necropsy in Wildlife Medicine

Necropsy, the post-mortem examination of an animal, is one of the most valuable diagnostic tools available to wildlife veterinarians. Every dead animal represents an information source that, when properly examined, can yield data on disease, environmental threats, nutritional status, reproductive condition, and cause of death. In the context of wildlife medicine, where access to living patients is limited and population-level health assessment is the goal, necropsy data form a cornerstone of disease surveillance and health monitoring.

### Value of Systematic Necropsy Programs

Well-designed necropsy programs contribute to wildlife conservation in multiple ways:

- **Disease detection and characterization**: Identification of new and emerging diseases, characterization of pathogen host range, documentation of disease distribution and prevalence
- **Mortality cause determination**: Understanding the relative contribution of different mortality factors (predation, disease, starvation, trauma, toxicosis, anthropogenic causes) to population dynamics
- **Baseline health data**: Organ weights, tissue morphology, body composition, and parasitology data from apparently healthy individuals establish reference values
- **Environmental monitoring**: Detection of contaminant exposure, nutritional stress, and environmental change through tissue analysis
- **Law enforcement support**: Evidence collection for wildlife crime investigation
- **Research**: Tissue and sample archives enable retrospective studies as new diagnostic tools and research questions emerge

### Building and Maintaining Necropsy Programs

Effective wildlife necropsy programs require:

- **Consistent protocols**: Standardized procedures ensure data comparability across examiners, locations, and time periods
- **Trained personnel**: Veterinary pathologists are ideal, but field biologists and wildlife managers can perform valuable gross necropsies with appropriate training
- **Sample archiving**: Tissues preserved in formalin, frozen, and dried should be archived systematically for future analysis
- **Database management**: Necropsy findings must be recorded in searchable, standardized databases
- **Reporting infrastructure**: Results should be communicated to wildlife managers, regulatory agencies, and the scientific community in a timely manner
- **Sustained funding**: Long-term surveillance requires stable, sustained funding commitments

## Diagnostic vs. Forensic Necropsy

### Diagnostic Necropsy

The primary goal of a diagnostic necropsy is to determine the cause of death or illness and contribute to understanding of disease in the population. Key characteristics:

- **Focus**: Identifying pathological processes and etiologic agents
- **Approach**: Systematic examination of all organ systems with appropriate sample collection for histopathology, microbiology, toxicology, and other diagnostics
- **Documentation**: Detailed written descriptions of gross findings, supplemented by photographs
- **Sample handling**: Samples collected and submitted following standard diagnostic laboratory protocols
- **Reporting**: Results communicated to the submitting agency or individual, entered into surveillance databases

### Forensic Necropsy

A forensic necropsy is conducted when there is suspicion that wildlife laws may have been violated. The standards for evidence handling, documentation, and chain of custody are substantially higher than for diagnostic necropsies:

- **Focus**: Determining cause and manner of death in a legally defensible manner
- **Approach**: Meticulous, systematic examination with awareness that findings may be presented in court
- **Documentation**: Extensive photography (including scale references), detailed written descriptions, diagrams. All documentation must be contemporaneous.
- **Sample handling**: Strict chain of custody maintained for all evidence. Samples sealed, labeled, and signed at every transfer point.
- **Reporting**: Formal necropsy report suitable for legal proceedings. Expert opinion on cause and manner of death with supporting evidence.
- **Legal awareness**: Examiner must understand rules of evidence, expert witness requirements, and the relevant legal framework (Endangered Species Act, Lacey Act, Migratory Bird Treaty Act, CITES, national and local wildlife laws)

### When to Treat a Necropsy as Forensic

Any necropsy should be treated as potentially forensic when:

- Gunshot wounds, trap injuries, or other evidence of illegal take is found
- Poisoning is suspected, particularly if deliberate
- The species is protected under federal or international law
- There is evidence of poaching, trafficking, or illegal trade
- Law enforcement has requested the examination
- The circumstances of death are suspicious or unexplained and involve a protected species

**Best practice**: When in doubt, maintain forensic-level documentation and chain of custody. It is far easier to relax standards retroactively than to reconstruct evidence that was not properly collected.

## Contribution to Population Health Monitoring

### Mortality Surveillance

Systematic necropsy of wildlife mortalities provides population-level health information that cannot be obtained any other way:

- **Proportional mortality**: The relative frequency of different causes of death reveals the most important threats to a population
- **Spatial patterns**: Mapping mortality causes across the landscape identifies geographic hotspots and associations with habitat features, land use, or contamination sources
- **Temporal trends**: Long-term necropsy data reveal changes in mortality patterns over time, potentially detecting emerging threats or the effects of management interventions
- **Demographic patterns**: Age- and sex-specific mortality patterns inform population modeling and management

### Sentinel Surveillance

Certain wildlife species or situations serve as particularly valuable sentinels for broader environmental or disease threats:

- **Raptors**: Scavenging and predatory raptors are sentinels for lead contamination, anticoagulant rodenticide exposure, and pesticide contamination
- **Fish**: Fish kills are often the first indication of water quality problems affecting entire aquatic ecosystems
- **Amphibians**: Amphibian die-offs may signal emerging infectious diseases (chytrid fungus, ranavirus) or environmental contamination
- **Marine mammals**: Strandings provide information on ocean health, including harmful algal bloom events, pollution, and emerging marine diseases
- **Bats**: White-nose syndrome surveillance relies heavily on bat carcass examination

### Integration with Other Data Sources

Necropsy findings are most powerful when integrated with other data:

- **Demographic data**: Linking cause of death to age, sex, reproductive status, and location
- **Telemetry data**: GPS/VHF tracking data from marked individuals provides behavioral and spatial context for mortality events
- **Environmental data**: Weather, habitat conditions, prey availability, and pollution monitoring data
- **Live animal health data**: Hematology, serum biochemistry, and other health parameters from live-captured individuals in the same population
- **Genetic data**: Relatedness, inbreeding coefficients, and population of origin from tissue samples collected at necropsy
