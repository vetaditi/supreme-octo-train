# Forensic Investigation in Wildlife Pathology

## Overview

Wildlife forensic pathology applies the principles and techniques of forensic medicine to investigate suspected violations of wildlife protection laws. Wildlife veterinarians may be called upon to determine the cause and manner of death in cases involving illegal killing, poisoning, trafficking, or other wildlife crimes. The standards of evidence collection, documentation, and reporting must meet legal requirements, as findings may be presented in administrative proceedings, civil litigation, or criminal prosecution.

## Wildlife Crime Investigation

### Categories of Wildlife Crime

Wildlife crimes that may require forensic pathological investigation include:

- **Illegal take**: Killing, capturing, or possessing protected species in violation of federal, state, or international law
- **Poaching**: Illegal hunting or fishing, including take out of season, over bag limits, or in prohibited areas
- **Poisoning**: Intentional or negligent poisoning of wildlife, including use of illegal pesticides as toxicants
- **Trafficking**: Illegal trade in live animals, parts, or products
- **Habitat destruction**: Illegal destruction of critical habitat, nesting sites, or other protected areas
- **Oil and chemical spills**: Corporate negligence resulting in wildlife mortality
- **Animal cruelty**: Acts of deliberate cruelty toward wildlife

### Legal Framework

Key statutes governing wildlife crime investigation (United States context; other jurisdictions have parallel legislation):

| Statute | Scope |
|---|---|
| Endangered Species Act (ESA) | Protection of listed threatened and endangered species |
| Migratory Bird Treaty Act (MBTA) | Protection of migratory birds, nests, and eggs |
| Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act | Specific protection for eagles |
| Lacey Act | Prohibition of trade in illegally taken wildlife |
| Marine Mammal Protection Act | Protection of all marine mammals |
| Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES) | International trade regulation for listed species |
| Animal Welfare Act | Standards of care for certain captive wildlife |
| State wildlife codes | State-specific protections and regulations |

## Gunshot Wound Recognition

### Entrance Wounds

Characteristics of gunshot entrance wounds in wildlife:

- **Rifle/handgun**: Round or oval defect with abraded or inverted margins. Size depends on caliber and range. Close-range wounds may show powder stippling or soot deposition.
- **Shotgun**: Pattern depends on range. Close range produces a single large defect; intermediate range shows a central defect with surrounding pellet holes; long range produces a dispersed pellet pattern.
- **Arrow/crossbow bolt**: Slit-like wound consistent with broadhead geometry. May show clean-cut margins. Broadhead fragments may be present.

### Exit Wounds

- Generally larger and more irregular than entrance wounds
- Margins are everted (turned outward)
- May contain bone or tissue fragments
- Not always present (projectile may be retained in the body)

### Projectile Recovery and Analysis

- **Radiograph the carcass** before opening to locate projectiles and fragments
- Recover projectiles carefully to preserve ballistic markings (use fingers or padded forceps; never use metal instruments that could scratch the projectile surface)
- Document projectile location precisely (organ, depth, relationship to wound track)
- Photograph the projectile with scale reference before and after cleaning
- Place recovered projectiles in a padded, labeled evidence container
- Bullet fragments, shotgun pellets, and wads should all be recovered and preserved
- Submit projectile evidence to law enforcement for ballistic comparison if applicable

### Wound Track Documentation

- Trace the wound track through tissues, documenting all organs and structures damaged
- Photograph the wound track at each level
- Note the direction of the wound track (important for reconstructing the shooting position)
- Hemorrhage in the wound track indicates the animal was alive when shot (vital reaction); absence suggests post-mortem injury

## Poisoning Evidence

### Signs Suggestive of Poisoning

- Multiple carcasses of different species in a localized area
- Carcasses near a bait station, carcass, or treated area
- Stained or discolored material in the oral cavity or gastrointestinal tract
- Unusual odors (garlic-like for organophosphates, bitter almond for cyanide)
- Rapid death posture (opisthotonos, clenched talons in raptors)
- Green or blue granular material in GI contents (rodenticides)
- Constricted or dilated pupils
- Hemorrhage from body openings (anticoagulant rodenticides)
- Salivation, lacrimation, or muscle fasciculations observed before death

### Evidence Collection for Poisoning Cases

1. **Photograph the scene** including carcass position, potential bait material, and surrounding area
2. **Collect GI contents** in their entirety in a clean, sealed container
3. **Collect potential bait material** or treated carcasses from the scene
4. **Collect liver** (minimum 50 g, preferably 100+ g) in clean foil, then in a sealed bag
5. **Collect brain** (one hemisphere fresh-frozen for cholinesterase; other half in formalin)
6. **Collect blood/serum** if available
7. **Collect crop contents** (birds) separately from lower GI contents
8. **Collect environmental samples**: soil beneath the carcass, water from nearby sources, vegetation
9. **Freeze all toxicology samples** at -20 degrees C or colder
10. **Maintain chain of custody** for all samples from collection through laboratory analysis

### Common Intentional Poisons Used Against Wildlife

| Compound | Target Species | Clinical/Pathological Findings |
|---|---|---|
| Carbofuran (Furadan) | Raptors, carnivores | Cholinesterase inhibition; salivation, muscle tremors, constricted pupils |
| Strychnine | Carnivores | Violent convulsions; rigor-like posture post-mortem |
| Sodium fluoroacetate (1080) | Carnivores, rodents | Cardiac failure; relatively few gross lesions |
| Alpha-chloralose | Birds | Narcosis; comatose birds may be alive |
| Anticoagulant rodenticides | Non-target raptors, carnivores | Widespread hemorrhage; anemia; hemorrhage into body cavities |
| Aldicarb (Temik) | Non-specific | Rapid death; cholinesterase inhibition |
| Diclofenac | Vultures (South Asia) | Visceral gout; renal failure |

## Trap Injuries

### Legal vs. Illegal Trapping

Forensic examination may be needed to determine whether trapping injuries resulted from legal or illegal methods:

- **Trap type identification**: Foothold traps, body-gripping (Conibear) traps, snares, glue traps, cage traps all produce characteristic injury patterns
- **Trap placement**: Illegal placement may be inferred from location relative to protected areas or non-target species habitats
- **Trap standards compliance**: Injuries inconsistent with legal trap specifications may indicate violation

### Characteristic Injuries

- **Foothold traps**: Circumferential soft tissue swelling, abrasion, or laceration at the trapped limb. Fractures at or below the trap site. Self-mutilation (chewing of trapped limb) in prolonged restraint.
- **Snares**: Circumferential wire or cable marks, typically around the neck, torso, or limb. Deep lacerations or degloving injuries from struggle. Asphyxiation or strangulation.
- **Body-gripping traps**: Crush injuries to the head, neck, or thorax. Vertebral fractures or dislocations.

## Chain of Custody Procedures

### Principles

Chain of custody documents the chronological handling of evidence from collection through laboratory analysis and into court. Every transfer of evidence must be documented.

### Essential Elements

1. **Evidence identification**: Unique case number, item number, description, date and time of collection
2. **Collection documentation**: Who collected the evidence, where, when, and under what circumstances
3. **Packaging**: Evidence sealed in tamper-evident containers with collector's initials across the seal
4. **Transfer records**: Every person who handles the evidence signs and dates the chain of custody form, recording the date, time, and purpose of transfer
5. **Storage**: Evidence stored in secure, access-controlled locations (freezers for biological evidence, locked evidence rooms)
6. **Laboratory receipt**: Laboratory personnel document receipt, condition of packaging, and any anomalies

### Practical Recommendations

- Use pre-printed chain of custody forms
- Photograph evidence packaging before sealing and after receipt
- Use tamper-evident bags or tape with unique serial numbers
- Never leave evidence unattended or in unsecured locations
- Maintain a master evidence log for each case
- Keep original chain of custody forms; provide copies if needed

## Evidence Documentation

### Photography Standards

Forensic photography for wildlife cases follows the same principles as human forensic photography:

- **Overall photographs**: Scene overview with contextual landmarks
- **Mid-range photographs**: Carcass or evidence item with surrounding context
- **Close-up photographs**: Detail of lesions, injuries, or evidence items with scale reference (ABFO No. 2 scale or metric ruler)
- **Identification photographs**: Include case identification card in frame

### Written Documentation

- All notes should be contemporaneous (made at the time of examination)
- Use indelible ink on numbered pages
- Do not erase or use correction fluid; draw a single line through errors and initial
- Record the date, time, location, and personnel present
- Describe findings in objective, descriptive terms
- Diagrams and body maps supplement written descriptions

### Radiography

- Radiograph the carcass before necropsy whenever possible (essential for gunshot and suspected projectile injuries)
- Document metallic foreign bodies (projectiles, trap components, fishhooks)
- Radiographic findings may reveal fracture patterns, pneumothorax, or other findings not apparent on gross examination

## Working with Law Enforcement

### US Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS)

The USFWS Office of Law Enforcement is the primary federal agency responsible for wildlife crime investigation in the United States:

- Special agents investigate violations of federal wildlife laws
- The National Fish and Wildlife Forensics Laboratory (Ashland, Oregon) provides specialized forensic analysis (species identification, cause of death, ballistics, DNA analysis)
- Wildlife veterinarians may be asked to perform necropsies at the direction of USFWS agents

### State Wildlife Agencies

State conservation officers (game wardens) investigate violations of state wildlife laws:

- May request veterinary necropsy support for poaching and poisoning investigations
- Coordinate with federal agencies when both state and federal laws are involved

### National Wildlife Crime Units (International)

Many countries have dedicated wildlife crime investigation units:

- UK: National Wildlife Crime Unit (NWCU)
- Australia: Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water (compliance division)
- South Africa: Environmental Management Inspectorate (Green Scorpions)
- INTERPOL: Environmental Security Sub-Directorate coordinates international wildlife crime efforts

### Communication Protocols

- Report suspected wildlife crimes promptly to the appropriate law enforcement agency
- Preserve evidence and maintain chain of custody pending law enforcement response
- Cooperate with law enforcement requests for information and testimony
- Do not discuss case details publicly or with media without law enforcement authorization
- Document all communications with law enforcement

## Expert Witness Considerations

### Qualifications

A wildlife veterinarian may be qualified as an expert witness based on:

- Veterinary degree and licensure
- Board certification in veterinary pathology, wildlife medicine, or related specialty
- Relevant experience in wildlife necropsy and forensic investigation
- Publication record and professional standing
- Previous expert witness experience (helpful but not required)

### Report Writing

Forensic necropsy reports should be:

- **Clear and objective**: Written for a non-scientific audience (judges, jurors, attorneys)
- **Complete**: All findings documented, whether or not they support a particular conclusion
- **Organized**: Follow a logical structure (history, external examination, internal examination, ancillary testing, diagnosis, cause of death opinion)
- **Defensible**: Every conclusion supported by documented evidence
- **Honest about limitations**: Acknowledge uncertainty where it exists

### Testimony Preparation

- Review the case file thoroughly before testimony
- Prepare visual aids (photographs, diagrams) to explain findings to the court
- Anticipate cross-examination questions
- Distinguish between fact testimony (what you observed) and opinion testimony (your expert interpretation)
- Use clear, non-technical language
- Remain objective; the expert's role is to assist the court, not to advocate for either party
- Maintain professional composure under adversarial questioning
