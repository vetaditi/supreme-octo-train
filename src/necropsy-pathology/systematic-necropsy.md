# Systematic Necropsy Procedures

## General Principles

A systematic necropsy follows a consistent, thorough protocol that ensures no organ system is overlooked. Regardless of the species being examined, the following principles apply:

- **Examine every organ system**: Even if a cause of death seems obvious, complete the full examination. Incidental findings are valuable for population health monitoring, and the apparent cause may not be the actual cause.
- **Describe before cutting**: Record the gross appearance of organs and tissues before dissecting them.
- **Use consistent terminology**: Standardized descriptive terms (see Gross Lesion Description section) ensure clear communication and database consistency.
- **Collect samples systematically**: Follow a consistent sampling plan to avoid missing tissues needed for histopathology or other diagnostics.
- **Work from clean to contaminated**: Examine and sample organs in an order that minimizes cross-contamination (e.g., collect microbiology samples before opening the GI tract).

## External Examination

The external examination is the same across all taxa and should precede opening of the body cavity:

### Standard External Assessment

1. **Species identification**: Confirm species. Note any identification markers (bands, tags, microchips, ear notches).
2. **Body weight and measurements**: Record body mass and standard morphometric measurements appropriate to the species.
3. **Body condition**: Score using species-appropriate system. Palpate muscle mass and fat reserves.
4. **Integument**: Examine skin, fur/feathers/scales for lesions, parasites, wounds, discoloration, swelling. Note molt stage in birds.
5. **Natural openings**: Examine eyes (clarity, discharge, parasites), ears, nares, oral cavity (mucous membranes, lesions, foreign bodies), cloaca/anus (discharge, prolapse), urogenital opening.
6. **Musculoskeletal**: Palpate for fractures, joint swelling, muscle wasting, symmetry.
7. **Subcutaneous tissues**: Reflect skin to examine subcutaneous fat (amount, color, consistency), subcutaneous musculature, and lymph nodes.

## Mammalian Necropsy

### Body Cavity Opening

1. Position the animal in dorsal recumbency
2. Make a midline incision from the mandibular symphysis to the pubis
3. Reflect the skin laterally, examining subcutaneous tissues
4. Open the abdominal cavity along the linea alba; note any free fluid (amount, color, clarity, odor)
5. Examine abdominal organs in situ before removal
6. Open the thoracic cavity by cutting ribs bilaterally at the costochondral junctions; remove the sternum as a plate
7. Examine thoracic organs in situ; note presence of fluid in pleural space or pericardial sac
8. Examine the diaphragm for integrity

### Organ System Examination

**Respiratory system**:
- Nasal cavity: Open by sagittal section of the skull; examine turbinates and sinuses
- Larynx and trachea: Open longitudinally; note luminal contents, mucosal appearance
- Lungs: Note color, consistency, distribution of lesions. Palpate for nodules. Section all lobes. Float test (normal lung floats; consolidated or atelectatic lung sinks)
- Pleura: Note adhesions, thickening, fluid

**Cardiovascular system**:
- Pericardium: Note fluid, adhesions
- Heart: Weigh. Open right side first (right atrium to right ventricle through tricuspid valve), then left side. Examine valves, endocardium, myocardium. Measure ventricular wall thickness.
- Great vessels: Examine aorta, pulmonary artery, vena cava for patency and lesions

**Gastrointestinal system**:
- Esophagus: Open longitudinally
- Stomach: Open along greater curvature. Note contents (type, amount, foreign bodies), mucosal appearance
- Small intestine: Open representative segments of duodenum, jejunum, ileum. Note contents, mucosal appearance, wall thickness
- Large intestine: Open cecum and colon. Note contents and mucosal appearance
- Liver: Weigh. Examine surface and cut surface. Note color, consistency, focal lesions
- Gallbladder: Note contents, wall thickness, patency of bile duct
- Pancreas: Examine for color, consistency, nodules
- Mesenteric lymph nodes: Section and examine

**Urogenital system**:
- Kidneys: Weigh. Section longitudinally. Examine cortex, medulla, pelvis
- Ureters: Examine for patency and dilation
- Urinary bladder: Note contents, wall thickness, mucosal lesions
- Reproductive organs: Ovaries (follicular development, corpora lutea), uterus (pregnancy, contents), testes (size, consistency), accessory glands

**Endocrine organs**:
- Adrenal glands: Section; note cortex-to-medulla ratio
- Thyroid: Note size, color, consistency

**Lymphoid system**:
- Spleen: Weigh. Section. Note size, consistency, color, follicular pattern
- Lymph nodes: Sample from multiple body regions (submandibular, prescapular, mesenteric, inguinal, tracheobronchial)
- Thymus: Note size (involution occurs with age and stress)

**Nervous system**:
- Brain: Remove by craniotomy. Weigh. Examine meninges. Section coronally at standard levels
- Spinal cord: Section at cervical, thoracic, and lumbar levels if neurological disease is suspected

**Musculoskeletal system**:
- Skeletal muscle: Examine major muscle groups for color, consistency, hemorrhage
- Bones: Section long bone for marrow assessment (fat content, cellularity)
- Joints: Open representative joints to examine cartilage and synovial fluid

## Avian Necropsy

### Key Differences from Mammalian Necropsy

- Position in dorsal recumbency; wet feathers with water and detergent to improve visualization
- Reflect skin from keel to reveal pectoral muscles (assess body condition)
- Open body cavity by cutting through keel and ribs bilaterally
- Birds have air sacs that should be examined for clarity (normal = transparent, thin)
- Birds have a ventriculus (gizzard) with koilin lining and a proventriculus (glandular stomach)
- Cloaca serves as common opening for digestive, urinary, and reproductive tracts
- Bursa of Fabricius (in young birds) is a lymphoid organ unique to birds
- Lungs are fixed to the dorsal body wall and do not collapse when the body cavity is opened
- Kidneys are embedded in the synsacrum

### Avian-Specific Organ Assessment

- **Air sacs**: Examine all air sacs for thickening, exudate, fungal plaques (aspergillosis)
- **Crop**: Open and examine contents
- **Proventriculus and ventriculus**: Open; note contents, koilin lining integrity, foreign bodies
- **Ceca**: Paired in most species; examine contents and mucosa
- **Bursa of Fabricius**: Size and involution status (present in juveniles)
- **Uropygial (preen) gland**: Examine at dorsal tail base; note size and secretion

## Reptile Necropsy

### General Approach

- Body position depends on species (dorsal for chelonians and lizards, ventral for snakes)
- Chelonians: Remove plastron using an oscillating saw or bone shears at the bridge
- Snakes: Ventral midline incision from cloaca to mandible
- Lizards: Ventral midline incision similar to mammals

### Reptile-Specific Considerations

- **Spectacle**: Examine the spectacle (fused eyelid) in snakes and some lizards for retained shed or infection
- **Respiratory**: Reptile lungs vary greatly in structure. Snakes have elongated, often unipulmonary lungs with an air sac extension. Chelonians have lungs adhered to the dorsal carapace.
- **Fat bodies**: Paired coelomic fat bodies serve as primary energy reserves. Note size and color.
- **Kidneys**: Located in the caudal coelomic cavity (pelvic canal in chelonians). Prone to gout (visceral or articular).
- **Thyroid and parathyroid**: Small but important; thyroid is ventral to the trachea near the heart base
- **Salt glands**: Present in marine species (sea turtles, marine iguanas); examine for patency
- **Reproductive**: Sex determination may require gonadal examination in species without external dimorphism

## Amphibian Necropsy

### General Approach

- Small body size requires fine instruments (ophthalmic instruments, micro-scissors)
- Ventral midline incision from chin to cloaca
- Skin is an organ of interest: examine carefully for lesions (chytrid fungus causes skin thickening and hyperkeratosis)
- Magnification (hand lens or dissecting microscope) is often necessary

### Amphibian-Specific Considerations

- **Skin**: The most important organ for amphibian health assessment. Examine for erosions, ulcerations, discoloration, excess sloughing. Collect skin swabs for *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis* (Bd) and *B. salamandrivorans* (Bsal) PCR.
- **Lungs**: Simple sac-like structures; some salamanders are lungless (plethodontids)
- **Liver**: Typically dark and bilobed. Melanomacrophage centers are normal; note number and distribution.
- **Fat bodies**: Paired, bright yellow/orange structures in the coelomic cavity. Absence indicates poor nutritional condition.
- **Kidneys**: Elongated, dorsal structures. Note color and texture.
- **Spleen**: Small, spherical, dark red. Often contains melanomacrophages.
- **Lymph sacs**: Subcutaneous lymph sacs are prominent in anurans; note excessive fluid accumulation (edema/hydrocoelom)

## Fish Necropsy

### General Approach

- Examine fish immediately after death when possible; decomposition is extremely rapid
- External examination is particularly important in fish: fins, gills, eyes, scales, lateral line
- Open the body cavity by cutting from the vent anteriorly along the ventral midline, then dorsally behind the operculum and along the lateral line

### Fish-Specific Considerations

- **Gills**: The most critical organ for initial assessment. Note color (normally bright red), mucus production, lamellar fusion, parasites, necrosis. Prepare fresh wet-mount and gill clip.
- **Swim bladder**: Note inflation, position, adhesions, contents
- **Kidney**: Fish kidney is located dorsal to the swim bladder along the vertebral column. Anterior kidney (head kidney) is a major lymphoid/hematopoietic organ.
- **Spleen**: Usually small, dark red. Important immune organ.
- **Liver (hepatopancreas in some species)**: Color varies with nutritional status and species. Note pallor, discoloration, nodules.
- **Gastrointestinal tract**: Open from esophagus to vent. Note parasites, contents, mucosal appearance. Pyloric ceca (when present) should be examined.
- **Gonads**: Note maturation stage and any abnormalities (intersex, neoplasia)
- **Skin scrape and fin clip**: Prepare wet-mounts for immediate microscopic examination for ectoparasites and fungi

## Gross Lesion Description Terminology

### Standard Descriptive Framework

When describing gross lesions, use the following systematic approach (mnemonic: **LDDCC** - Location, Distribution, Dimension, Color, Consistency):

**Location**:
- Name the organ or tissue affected
- Specify the anatomical region within the organ (e.g., cortex vs. medulla, serosal vs. mucosal surface)

**Distribution**:

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Focal | Single, discrete lesion |
| Multifocal | Multiple discrete lesions |
| Locally extensive | Single large lesion affecting a region |
| Multifocal to coalescing | Multiple lesions merging together |
| Diffuse | Affecting the entire organ or tissue |
| Segmental | Affecting a defined segment of a tubular organ |
| Bilateral/unilateral | Affecting both/one of paired organs |

**Dimension**:
- Measure in centimeters (length x width x depth)
- Count the number of lesions (if multifocal and countable)
- Estimate percentage of organ affected

**Color**:
- Use standard color terms (red, white, yellow, green, tan, brown, black, gray)
- Note uniformity or variation in color
- Describe color relative to normal tissue

**Consistency/Texture**:

| Term | Description |
|---|---|
| Firm | Increased resistance to cutting; maintains shape |
| Soft | Decreased resistance; easily deformed |
| Friable | Crumbles easily when handled |
| Fluctuant | Fluid-filled; wave-like movement on palpation |
| Caseous | Cheese-like consistency (often granulomatous) |
| Gelatinous | Gel-like, translucent |
| Gritty | Mineralized; resists smooth cutting |
| Rubbery | Elastic, returns to shape after deformation |

### Additional Descriptive Terms

- **Elevated vs. depressed**: Raised above or sunken below the surrounding surface
- **Well-demarcated vs. poorly demarcated**: Sharp or gradual transition to normal tissue
- **Encapsulated vs. infiltrative**: Contained by a fibrous capsule or invading surrounding tissue
- **Exudate character**: Serous, fibrinous, suppurative (purulent), hemorrhagic, or mixed
- **Ulcerated**: Loss of epithelial surface
- **Necrotic**: Dead tissue; may be coagulative (firm, pale) or liquefactive (soft, fluid)

### Terminology to Avoid

- **Do not use histological diagnoses as gross descriptions**: Avoid terms like "neoplasia," "granuloma," or "abscess" unless you are confident in the identification. Instead, describe what you see: "2 cm diameter firm white nodule" rather than "abscess."
- **Avoid subjective or imprecise terms**: "Abnormal" is not a description. Describe the specific abnormality.
- **Avoid causal language in descriptions**: Describe the lesion, not its presumed cause. "Red discoloration" rather than "hemorrhage" is more appropriate at the gross level, though experienced pathologists may use interpretive terms when confident.
