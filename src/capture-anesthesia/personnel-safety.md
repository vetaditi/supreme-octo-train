# Personnel Safety

## Overview

Wildlife capture operations expose personnel to a unique combination of hazards: potent pharmacological agents lethal to humans in microgram quantities, zoonotic pathogens, and direct physical danger from the animals themselves. A robust safety culture, formalized training, and written emergency action plans are non-negotiable components of any wildlife capture program.

## Zoonotic Exposure Risk

### High-Risk Pathogens in Wildlife Capture

| Pathogen | Reservoir Species | Transmission Route | Clinical Concern |
|----------|------------------|-------------------|------------------|
| Rabies virus | Carnivores, bats, raccoons, skunks | Bite, scratch, mucous membrane contact with saliva | Fatal encephalitis if untreated |
| Mycobacterium tuberculosis / M. bovis | Primates, cervids, bovids, badgers | Aerosol, wound contamination | Pulmonary/extrapulmonary TB |
| Hantavirus | Rodents | Aerosol from urine/feces/nesting material | Hantavirus pulmonary syndrome |
| Brucella spp. | Ungulates, marine mammals, feral swine | Contact with reproductive fluids, aerosol | Undulant fever, chronic infection |
| Leptospira spp. | Rodents, many mammalian species | Contact with urine-contaminated water/soil | Renal/hepatic failure |
| Herpes B virus (Macacine herpesvirus 1) | Macaques | Bite, scratch, splash to mucous membranes | Fatal encephalomyelitis |
| Avian influenza (HPAI) | Waterbirds, poultry, raptors | Aerosol, contact with respiratory secretions and feces | Pneumonia, systemic infection |
| Monkeypox virus | Rodents, primates | Contact with lesions, respiratory droplets | Vesiculopustular rash, systemic illness |
| Coxiella burnetii (Q fever) | Ruminants, wild ungulates | Aerosol from birthing products, wool, hides | Acute febrile illness, chronic endocarditis |
| Francisella tularensis | Lagomorphs, rodents | Tick bite, contact with infected tissues, aerosol | Ulceroglandular or pneumonic tularemia |

### Risk Mitigation

- Maintain current rabies pre-exposure vaccination for all field personnel
- Screen personnel for TB annually if working with primates or bovids
- Implement standard precautions: treat all wildlife as potentially infectious
- Report all bites, scratches, and mucous membrane exposures immediately
- Maintain a post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) decision tree for each major zoonotic pathogen
- Brief all team members on species-specific zoonotic risks before each capture operation

## Dangerous Animal Protocols

### Physical Injury Prevention

- **Large carnivores** — never approach until confirmed immobilized (test with a long stick from a safe distance; check jaw tone, palpebral reflex, and response to foot pinch)
- **Elephants** — approach from behind the shoulder; the trunk, tusks, and feet are lethal weapons; a partially immobilized elephant can rise suddenly
- **Rhinoceros** — approach from behind; the horn and bulk are the primary threats; maintain escape routes
- **Hippopotamus** — the most dangerous large African mammal; captures should be performed only by experienced teams; aquatic captures require extreme caution
- **Primates** — great apes have enormous strength; approach only when deep anesthesia is confirmed; never enter an enclosure with a conscious great ape
- **Crocodilians** — secure the jaws before approaching; the tail strike is powerful enough to fracture limbs
- **Venomous snakes** — use appropriate snake-handling tools; never free-handle venomous species; carry species-specific antivenom on site

### Approach Protocol for Immobilized Animals

1. Observe from a distance for at least 2 minutes after the animal appears immobilized
2. Approach slowly and deliberately from behind or from the dorsal aspect
3. Test the depth of immobilization by touching the animal with a long stick, observing for response
4. Check the palpebral (eyelid) reflex: a strong blink indicates inadequate anesthesia — retreat and administer supplemental drugs
5. Check jaw tone: relaxed jaw indicates adequate depth
6. Check pedal withdrawal: pinch the foot/toe; absence of response confirms adequate depth
7. Only when all depth checks are satisfactory should personnel approach for hands-on work
8. Maintain at least one team member as a dedicated safety observer throughout the procedure

## Carfentanil and Etorphine: Human Exposure Emergency

### The Threat

Carfentanil and etorphine are ultra-potent synthetic opioids that can be fatal to humans through skin absorption, mucous membrane contact, needle-stick, or inhalation of aerosol. **As little as 1 microgram of carfentanil or 30 micrograms of etorphine can be lethal to an adult human.**

### Prevention

- **Never work alone** when handling potent opioids; a minimum of two people must be present at all times
- Wear nitrile or latex gloves (double-gloving recommended) when drawing up or handling drugs
- Wear safety glasses or goggles to prevent splash exposure to the eyes
- Wear a face mask or shield when handling dry powder formulations
- Never eat, drink, smoke, or touch the face while handling these drugs
- Cap all needles immediately after use; never recap by hand (use a one-handed technique or needle cap holder)
- Clean spills immediately with dilute bleach solution (0.5% sodium hypochlorite inactivates opioids)

### Emergency Response Protocol

**If exposure occurs (needle-stick, skin contact, splash, or inhalation):**

1. **Shout for help immediately** — do not wait for symptoms
2. **Decontaminate** — wash the affected area with copious water and soap; flush eyes with saline or clean water for 15 minutes
3. **Administer naloxone** — the designated safety officer should administer naloxone immediately:
   - Intranasal: 4 mg spray into one nostril (repeat in the other nostril if no response in 2 minutes)
   - IM: 0.4–2 mg into the deltoid or lateral thigh; repeat every 2–3 minutes as needed
   - IV (if available): 0.4–2 mg; titrate to effect
4. **Initiate CPR** if the person becomes apneic or pulseless
5. **Call emergency medical services** (ambulance/helicopter MEDEVAC)
6. **Continue naloxone** — carfentanil and etorphine have longer durations of action than naloxone; renarcotization is expected and repeated dosing or a continuous infusion will be needed
7. **Transport to hospital** — even if the person appears to recover, hospital observation for a minimum of 24 hours is required
8. **Provide the hospital with drug identification** — bring the drug vial label or safety data sheet to the emergency department

### Naloxone Availability Requirements

- Every person handling potent opioids must carry naloxone on their person (belt holster or pocket)
- A backup naloxone kit must be in the field vehicle
- Intranasal naloxone spray (e.g., Narcan) is preferred for non-medical personnel as it requires no injection technique
- Check naloxone expiration dates monthly and replace as needed
- Train all team members (including pilots, drivers, and support staff) in naloxone administration

## Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

### Standard Field PPE

| Equipment | Purpose | When Required |
|-----------|---------|--------------|
| Nitrile gloves | Barrier protection from biological fluids and drugs | All capture and sample-handling activities |
| Safety glasses/goggles | Eye splash protection | Drug handling, sample processing, necropsy |
| N95/FFP2 respirator | Aerosol and dust protection | Primate handling, TB-suspect animals, avian influenza, hantavirus-risk rodent work |
| Face shield | Full face splash protection | Necropsy, opioid handling, large animal procedures with fluid risk |
| Leather gloves (over nitrile) | Bite protection | Handling small to medium mammals and reptiles |
| Steel-toed boots | Crush protection | Large mammal operations |
| High-visibility vest | Personnel visibility | Operations near vehicles, aircraft, or in low-light conditions |
| Hearing protection | Noise reduction | Helicopter operations, cannon net firing |
| Hard hat / helmet | Head protection | Helicopter operations, work under trees (giraffe darting), cave work |
| Long-sleeved clothing | Scratch, tick, and UV protection | All field operations |

### Specialized PPE

- **Kevlar sleeves** — protection from raptor talons and primate bites
- **Chainmail gloves** — handling large aggressive reptiles
- **Dry suits/waders** — aquatic captures
- **Snake gaiters** — field work in venomous snake habitat
- **PAPR (powered air-purifying respirator)** — bat cave work, high-risk aerosol pathogen situations

## Emergency Action Plans

Every capture operation must have a written emergency action plan (EAP) that addresses both animal and human emergencies.

### Required EAP Components

1. **Chain of command** — who makes the call to abort, who contacts emergency services
2. **Communication plan** — radio frequencies, cell phone numbers, satellite phone if in remote areas
3. **Nearest medical facility** — name, address, GPS coordinates, estimated travel time, and confirmation that the facility can manage opioid exposure
4. **MEDEVAC protocol** — helicopter or ground ambulance availability, landing zone identification
5. **Emergency drug locations** — where naloxone, first aid kits, and emergency animal drugs are located in the vehicle/field setup
6. **Species-specific emergency protocols** — laminated cards with emergency drug doses for the target species
7. **Weather abort criteria** — temperature, wind, and visibility thresholds for halting operations
8. **Incident documentation** — forms for recording exposure events, injuries, and near-misses

### Emergency Drills

- Conduct a tabletop or practical emergency drill before the first capture of every season
- Practice naloxone administration with training devices
- Review the approach protocol for immobilized dangerous animals
- Test communication equipment and emergency contact numbers
- Debrief after every emergency event and near-miss

## Training Requirements

### Minimum Competencies for Capture Team Members

| Competency | Required For | Training Method |
|-----------|-------------|----------------|
| Darting system operation | Darter | Manufacturer training + supervised field practice |
| Drug calculation and preparation | Veterinarian, veterinary technician | Pharmacology coursework + supervised practice |
| Controlled substance handling | Veterinarian, designated personnel | Institutional controlled substance training |
| Naloxone administration | All team members | Annual hands-on training |
| Basic life support (BLS) | All team members | Certified course (renew every 2 years) |
| Wilderness first aid | All team members in remote operations | Certified course |
| Species identification and behavior | All team members | In-house training, field experience |
| Equipment maintenance | Equipment operators | Manufacturer guidelines + in-house training |
| Zoonotic disease awareness | All team members | Annual briefing by veterinarian |
| Data recording and sample handling | Designated data recorders | Standard operating procedure training |

### Continuing Education

- Attend professional workshops on wildlife chemical immobilization (e.g., AAZV, WDA, EWDA conferences)
- Maintain current literature awareness on capture drug protocols and safety data
- Review and update protocols annually based on new evidence and incident reports
- Participate in multi-institutional capture exercises when available

## Incident Reporting and Review

- All human injuries, exposures, and near-misses must be reported within 24 hours
- All animal mortalities and significant morbidities must be documented and reviewed
- Conduct a formal after-action review (AAR) following any serious incident
- Maintain an institutional capture incident database to identify trends and systemic risks
- Share lessons learned (anonymized if necessary) with the broader wildlife veterinary community to prevent recurrence
