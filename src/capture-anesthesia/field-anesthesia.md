# Field Anesthesia

## Overview

Field anesthesia presents unique challenges not encountered in clinical or zoo settings. The wildlife veterinarian must be prepared to deliver safe, effective anesthesia in remote locations with limited equipment, unpredictable environmental conditions, and no backup facilities. Success depends on meticulous preparation, portable equipment selection, and the ability to improvise when standard approaches are unavailable.

## Portable Equipment

### Essential Field Anesthesia Kit

| Equipment | Purpose | Notes |
|-----------|---------|-------|
| Pulse oximeter (veterinary) | SpO₂ and pulse rate monitoring | Use lingual, rectal, or ear clip probes depending on species |
| Portable capnograph | ETCO₂ monitoring | Critical for assessing ventilation in intubated animals |
| Digital rectal thermometer | Core body temperature | Rapid-read models preferred; have spares |
| Stethoscope (standard + amplified) | Cardiac and respiratory auscultation | Amplified/electronic models useful in noisy field environments |
| Doppler ultrasound unit | Pulse detection, indirect BP | Battery-operated; invaluable for reptiles, birds, and small mammals |
| Portable oxygen concentrator | Oxygen supplementation | Generates 5–10 L/min from ambient air; eliminates need for heavy cylinders |
| Oxygen cylinder (E-size) | High-flow supplementation | 15–30 L/min for rhinoceros and other megafauna; carry regulators and flow meters |
| Demand valve resuscitator | Positive-pressure ventilation | Useful for apneic large animals when an anesthetic circuit is unavailable |
| Self-inflating Ambu bag | Manual ventilation | Various sizes; infant (birds, small mammals), adult (medium mammals), large animal |

| Naloxone kit (human) | Personnel opioid exposure emergency | 0.4 mg/mL vials and intranasal spray |

### Equipment Transport and Power

- Use waterproof, shock-resistant cases (Pelican cases or equivalent) for all monitoring equipment
- Carry spare batteries for every battery-operated device; lithium batteries perform better in cold environments
- Consider a portable solar charger or vehicle-mounted inverter for extended operations
- Test all equipment before departure and carry backup manual alternatives where possible

## Oxygen Supplementation

Oxygen supplementation is one of the most impactful interventions in field anesthesia, particularly for large ungulates immobilized with potent opioids.

### Delivery Methods

- **Nasal insufflation** — insert a clean nasogastric tube 15–20 cm into one nostril; secure with tape; deliver O₂ at 5–15 L/min depending on body size
- **Nasotracheal intubation** — advance a smaller-diameter tube through the nasal passage into the trachea; provides more efficient delivery
- **Orotracheal intubation** — standard intubation for prolonged procedures; requires laryngoscope and appropriately sized ETT
- **Demand valve** — provides intermittent positive-pressure ventilation; useful for rhinoceros and elephants with severe respiratory depression
- **Face mask/cone** — suitable for small to medium mammals, birds, and reptiles; requires a tight seal for effective delivery

### Target Parameters

| Parameter | Target Range | Action if Below Target |
|-----------|-------------|----------------------|
| SpO₂ | >90% (>95% ideal) | Increase O₂ flow; reposition animal; consider partial opioid antagonism |
| Respiratory rate | Species-specific normal range | Stimulate breathing (compress chest, administer doxapram); ventilate if apneic |
| Mucous membrane color | Pink | Increase O₂; assess circulation; consider fluid therapy |

## Improvised Monitoring

When standard monitoring equipment is unavailable or malfunctions, the veterinarian must rely on clinical assessment.

### Clinical Monitoring Parameters

- **Respiratory rate and depth** — count chest excursions per minute; assess tidal volume by observing the amplitude of movement
- **Heart rate** — palpate peripheral pulses (digital, auricular, femoral); auscultate if possible
- **Capillary refill time** — compress the gum and count seconds to pink return (normal <2 seconds)
- **Mucous membrane color** — assess gum, conjunctival, or cloacal color; pink = adequate perfusion, pale/white = hypotension, cyanotic = hypoxemia
- **Jaw tone** — resistance to jaw opening indicates anesthetic depth; loss of tone may indicate excessive depth
- **Palpebral reflex** — touch the medial canthus; blink indicates lighter anesthetic plane
- **Eye position** — central eye position in many species indicates moderate surgical anesthesia; ventromedial rotation may indicate deeper plane
- **Rectal temperature** — palpate the abdomen if no thermometer is available; a hot abdomen warrants cooling measures

### Improvised Equipment Solutions

- A watch with a second hand serves as a timer for respiratory and heart rate counts
- A piece of clear plastic wrap held near the nostrils will fog with each exhalation, confirming breathing
- A length of IV extension tubing held to the chest wall may amplify heart sounds when used as a crude stethoscope
- Alcohol applied to the skin and observed for evaporation rate provides a rough ambient temperature/humidity gauge

## Environmental Considerations

### Temperature

**Hyperthermia** is the most common life-threatening environmental complication in field anesthesia, particularly in tropical and subtropical environments.

- Shade the animal immediately upon recumbency using tarps, vehicle shade, or natural cover
- Apply water to the ears, groin, and axillae for evaporative cooling
- Avoid wrapping or covering the animal unnecessarily
- If rectal temperature exceeds 40°C (mammals), initiate aggressive cooling: cold water IV fluids, ice packs to major vessels, cold water enemas
- Suspend operations when ambient temperature exceeds 35°C unless cooling infrastructure is robust

**Hypothermia** is a risk during cold weather operations and in small-bodied species.

- Insulate the animal from ground contact using foam pads or blankets
- Cover with space blankets or insulating drapes
- Warm IV fluids to body temperature before administration
- Use chemical heat packs wrapped in cloth (avoid direct skin contact)
- Small birds and mammals lose heat rapidly; minimize anesthesia time

### Altitude

- High altitude (>2,500 m) reduces barometric pressure and inspired oxygen partial pressure
- Animals are more susceptible to hypoxemia during anesthesia at altitude
- Increase supplemental oxygen flow rates by 25–50%
- Adjust drug doses conservatively; altitude may alter drug pharmacokinetics
- Capture myopathy risk is increased in oxygen-deprived environments

### Terrain

- Ensure the immobilized animal is on flat, stable ground to prevent positional complications
- If the animal goes down on a slope, reposition with the head slightly uphill
- Clear rocks, branches, and debris from around the animal to prevent injury during recovery
- In aquatic or semi-aquatic environments, ensure the animal's head is above water at all times; drowning is a recognized cause of capture mortality in hippos and crocodilians

## Field Surgery Considerations

Certain surgical procedures may need to be performed in the field when transport to a clinic is impractical.

### Feasible Field Procedures

- Wound debridement and primary closure
- Snare removal and wound management
- Abscess drainage and lavage
- Fracture stabilization (splinting, temporary external fixation)
- Cesarean section (emergency)
- Foreign body removal from the oral cavity or esophagus
- Enucleation (traumatic globe rupture)
- Biopsy collection

### Field Surgery Requirements

- Portable surgical instrument pack (basic soft tissue and orthopedic sets)
- Sterile drapes, gowns, and gloves (maintain sterility despite field conditions)
- Suture material: absorbable (PDS, Monocryl) and non-absorbable (nylon, stainless steel wire)
- Electrocautery or battery-operated cautery pen
- Portable headlamp or battery-operated surgical light
- Sterile saline or lactated Ringer's for wound lavage (use pressure bags for gravity-independent flow)
- Postoperative analgesics (meloxicam, buprenorphine, tramadol) appropriate to the species
- Antibiotics: broad-spectrum empirical therapy (ceftiofur, enrofloxacin, amoxicillin-clavulanate)

### Sterility in the Field

Maintaining sterility in a field environment is challenging but essential.

- Lay a sterile drape over the patient and create a defined surgical field
- Scrub the surgical site with chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine
- Use a no-touch technique wherever possible
- If wind or dust is excessive, erect a windbreak using tarps or vehicles
- Contaminated instruments should be isolated immediately and processed later

## Minimum Database Collection During Anesthesia

Every anesthetic event is an opportunity to collect valuable biological data. The following minimum database should be collected whenever feasible.

### Physical Data

- Body mass (portable hanging scales, platform scales, or vehicle-mounted crane scales)
- Body measurements: total length, tail length, shoulder height, chest girth (species-specific)
- Body condition score (species-specific scoring system)
- Dental examination: age estimation from tooth wear or eruption
- Reproductive status: pregnancy (palpation, ultrasound), lactation, testicular measurements

### Biological Samples

| Sample | Collection Method | Storage | Analysis |
|--------|------------------|---------|----------|
| Whole blood (EDTA) | Jugular, cephalic, or saphenous venipuncture | Refrigerate at 4°C | Hematology, genetics |
| Serum (plain tube) | Clot 30 min, centrifuge | Freeze at -20°C | Serology, biochemistry |
| Blood smear | Fresh drop on glass slide, feathered edge | Air-dry, fix in methanol | Hemoparasites, differential WBC |
| Feces | Collect from rectum or ground | Refrigerate or fix in formalin | Parasitology, microbiome |
| Ectoparasites | Manual collection, forceps | 70% ethanol | Species identification, pathogen screening |
| Hair/feather | Pluck (not cut) from standard site | Paper envelope, dry | Genetics, stable isotope analysis, cortisol |
| Swabs (oral, nasal, rectal, ocular) | Sterile swab with transport medium | Refrigerate or freeze | Microbiology, virology |
| Skin biopsy (if indicated) | 4–6 mm punch biopsy | 10% buffered formalin | Histopathology, genetics |

### Morphometric Protocols

- Use standardized measurement protocols published for the species when available
- Record all measurements in metric units
- Photograph the animal with a scale bar for morphometric analysis
- Apply permanent or semi-permanent identification (ear tags, PIT tags, brands) following institutional protocols

### Data Recording

- Use pre-printed or digital data sheets with all fields defined
- Record the time of drug administration, recumbency, first sample collection, and each subsequent event
- Note all physiological parameters at 5-minute intervals throughout anesthesia
- Assign each animal a unique identifier linked to all samples and data
- Back up digital data to a secondary device before leaving the field site
