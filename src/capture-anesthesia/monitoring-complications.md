# Monitoring and Complications

## Overview

Complications during wildlife capture and anesthesia are common and frequently life-threatening. Mortality rates of 1–5% are reported for many species during chemical immobilization, and rates may be substantially higher for high-risk taxa or poorly planned operations. The ability to rapidly recognize and treat complications is among the most critical skills for a wildlife veterinarian.

## Capture Myopathy (Exertional Rhabdomyolysis)

Capture myopathy is the most feared complication of wildlife capture. It is a non-infectious, metabolic disease resulting from extreme exertion, stress, or prolonged restraint.

### Pathophysiology

- Intense muscular activity depletes ATP and damages myocyte membranes
- Intracellular contents (myoglobin, potassium, creatine kinase, lactate) leak into the circulation
- Myoglobin precipitates in renal tubules, causing acute renal failure
- Metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia, and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) may follow
- Cardiac muscle may be directly affected, leading to arrhythmias and acute cardiac death

### Clinical Presentations

| Form | Onset | Signs | Prognosis |
|------|-------|-------|-----------|
| Peracute / Capture shock | Minutes to hours | Sudden death during or immediately after capture; ventricular fibrillation | Fatal |
| Acute | 1–72 hours | Recumbency, muscle stiffness, dark brown urine (myoglobinuria), tachycardia, hyperthermia | Guarded to poor |
| Subacute / Atrophic | Days to weeks | Progressive muscle atrophy and weakness, particularly hindlimbs; reluctance to move | Guarded |
| Chronic / Delayed | Weeks to months | Chronic wasting, recurrent recumbency, renal failure | Poor |

### Risk Factors

- Prolonged pursuit (helicopter chase >5 minutes dramatically increases risk)
- High ambient temperature and humidity
- Species predisposition: ungulates (especially zebra, wildebeest, wild equids), macropods, flamingos
- Poor body condition, dehydration, or pre-existing disease
- Physical restraint without adequate sedation
- Repeated capture attempts on the same animal

### Prevention

- Minimize pursuit time; abort capture attempts if induction is delayed
- Use rapid-onset drug protocols (thiafentanil preferred over etorphine for this reason)
- Pre-position capture teams to reduce chase distance
- Avoid capture during peak heat; schedule for early morning or late afternoon
- Ensure immediate access to shade and cooling
- Administer prophylactic vitamin E (10–20 IU/kg IM) and selenium (0.05–0.1 mg/kg IM) to high-risk species
- IV fluid therapy (10–20 mL/kg/hr crystalloids) during anesthesia supports renal perfusion

### Treatment

- Aggressive IV fluid therapy: isotonic crystalloids at 20–40 mL/kg/hr to maintain renal perfusion and flush myoglobin
- Sodium bicarbonate (1–2 mEq/kg IV slowly) to alkalinize urine and reduce myoglobin precipitation
- Dexamethasone (0.1–0.25 mg/kg IV) for anti-inflammatory effect (controversial but widely used)
- Muscle relaxants: diazepam 0.1–0.5 mg/kg IV for spasm
- Maintain normothermia
- Monitor serum CK, AST, BUN/creatinine, potassium, and urine color
- Prognosis is poor once myoglobinuria is established; euthanasia may be warranted if renal failure develops

## Hyperthermia

### Definition and Thresholds

- **Mild**: 40–41°C (mammals); requires active monitoring and passive cooling
- **Moderate**: 41–42°C; requires active cooling intervention
- **Severe/critical**: >42°C; life-threatening; may cause irreversible organ damage and DIC

### Causes

- Pursuit-related exertion
- High ambient temperature
- Ongoing muscle activity (seizures, rigidity from opioids)
- Excessive insulation (inappropriate blanket use)
- Impaired thermoregulation under anesthesia

### Treatment Protocol

1. Move to shade immediately
2. Apply water to ears, groin, axillae, and footpads (mammals)
3. Apply alcohol to skin surfaces for evaporative cooling
4. Fan the animal (vehicle fan, manual fanning, or car air conditioning)
5. Administer room-temperature IV fluids (not ice-cold, to avoid peripheral vasoconstriction)
6. Cold water enema (large animals)
7. Cease cooling efforts at 39.5°C to prevent rebound hypothermia
8. Monitor for secondary DIC, renal failure, and cerebral edema over subsequent 24–48 hours

## Hypothermia

### Risk Factors

- Small body mass (birds, small mammals, neonates)
- Prolonged anesthesia in cool or wet environments
- Wet animals (rain, aquatic capture)
- Inadequate insulation from cold ground

### Prevention and Treatment

- Insulate from ground contact with foam pads
- Cover with space blankets or insulating drapes
- Warm IV fluids to body temperature (38–39°C for most mammals)
- Chemical heat packs wrapped in cloth applied to axillae and inguinal region
- Warm air convection devices (Bair Hugger) if available
- Avoid actively warming faster than 1°C per hour to prevent afterdrop and cardiac instability

## Respiratory Depression

Respiratory depression is the most common immediate complication of opioid-based immobilization protocols.

### Recognition

- Respiratory rate below species-specific normal range
- Shallow, irregular, or gasping respirations
- SpO₂ <85% despite oxygen supplementation
- Cyanotic mucous membranes
- ETCO₂ >60 mmHg (if capnography available)

### Management

| Intervention | Indication | Details |
|-------------|-----------|---------|
| Oxygen supplementation | SpO₂ <90% | Nasal insufflation at 5–15 L/min; higher for large ungulates |
| Doxapram | Respiratory rate <4 breaths/min | 1–2 mg/kg IV; short-acting (5–10 min); may repeat |
| Partial antagonism (butorphanol) | Opioid-induced depression in rhino/elephant | 10–30 mg IV; antagonizes mu-receptor respiratory depression while maintaining kappa-mediated sedation |
| Manual ventilation | Apnea >30 seconds | Ambu bag or demand valve; 6–12 breaths/min for large mammals |
| Full opioid reversal | Life-threatening respiratory failure | Naltrexone or diprenorphine at full reversal doses; accept loss of immobilization |

## Bloat in Ruminants

### Mechanism

- Lateral recumbency compresses the rumen against the diaphragm
- Anesthesia suppresses eructation (belching) reflexes
- Ongoing fermentation produces gas that cannot escape
- Progressive rumen distension compromises ventilation and venous return

### Prevention

- Position ruminants in sternal recumbency whenever possible
- If lateral recumbency is necessary, place in right lateral (left side up) to facilitate gas escape from the rumen
- Minimize anesthesia duration
- Fast animals for 12–24 hours before planned immobilization (when possible)

### Treatment

- Pass an orogastric tube to decompress the rumen
- If tubing fails, perform percutaneous trocarization: 14G needle or trocar into the left paralumbar fossa at the point of maximum distension
- Emergency rumenotomy may be required in severe, unresponsive cases
- Resume sternal positioning as soon as practical

## Regurgitation and Aspiration

### Risk Factors

- Non-fasted animals (the norm in free-ranging wildlife)
- Lateral recumbency
- Light anesthetic plane with intact but sluggish laryngeal reflexes
- Alpha-2 agonist-induced vomiting (common in canids and felids)

### Prevention

- Position the head and neck slightly downhill to allow passive drainage
- Do not stimulate the pharynx unnecessarily
- Intubate with a cuffed endotracheal tube when practical
- Be prepared with suction (manual or battery-powered) to clear the airway

### Treatment

- Immediately position the animal with the head lower than the body
- Suction the oropharynx and trachea
- Intubate and ventilate with 100% oxygen
- Lavage the trachea with sterile saline if significant aspiration has occurred
- Administer broad-spectrum antibiotics (aspiration pneumonia prophylaxis)
- Prognosis depends on the volume and composition of aspirated material

## Cardiac Arrhythmias

### Common Arrhythmias in Wildlife Anesthesia

| Arrhythmia | Common Cause | Treatment |
|-----------|-------------|-----------|
| Sinus bradycardia | Alpha-2 agonists (medetomidine), opioids, hypothermia | Atropine 0.02–0.04 mg/kg IV or glycopyrrolate 0.005–0.01 mg/kg IV |
| Sinus tachycardia | Pain, light anesthesia, hyperthermia, hypovolemia | Address underlying cause; deepen anesthesia; fluid therapy |
| Ventricular premature complexes (VPCs) | Catecholamine surge, myocardial hypoxia, capture myopathy | Lidocaine 1–2 mg/kg IV bolus (dogs, large mammals); correct hypoxemia |
| Ventricular tachycardia | Severe myocardial disease, electrolyte imbalance | Lidocaine 2 mg/kg IV; consider defibrillation if available |
| Ventricular fibrillation | Terminal event; capture myopathy, severe metabolic derangement | Electrical defibrillation (rarely available in the field); CPR |

### Monitoring for Arrhythmias

- Auscultation: irregular rhythm, dropped beats, or racing heart rate
- Pulse quality: assess synchrony between heart sounds and peripheral pulse (pulse deficits indicate arrhythmia)
- ECG: portable monitors with alligator clip leads or adhesive pad electrodes
- Doppler: audible pulse irregularities

## Emergency Drug Doses

The following table provides quick-reference emergency drug doses for field use. All doses should be confirmed against species-specific references when available.

| Drug | Dose | Route | Indication |
|------|------|-------|------------|
| Atropine | 0.02–0.04 mg/kg | IV | Bradycardia |
| Glycopyrrolate | 0.005–0.01 mg/kg | IV, IM | Bradycardia (longer acting than atropine) |
| Epinephrine (1:1,000) | 0.01–0.02 mg/kg | IV, IT | Cardiac arrest, anaphylaxis |
| Doxapram | 1–2 mg/kg | IV | Respiratory depression |
| Lidocaine | 1–2 mg/kg | IV bolus | Ventricular arrhythmias |
| Diazepam | 0.1–0.5 mg/kg | IV | Seizures, muscle relaxation |
| Dexamethasone | 0.1–0.5 mg/kg | IV | Shock, cerebral edema, anaphylaxis |
| Furosemide | 1–2 mg/kg | IV | Pulmonary edema |
| Naltrexone | 50–100 mg per mg opioid | IM, IV | Opioid reversal |
| Atipamezole | 5× medetomidine dose | IM | Alpha-2 agonist reversal |

## Reversal Protocols

### Full Reversal (End of Procedure)

1. Ensure all personnel and equipment are clear of the animal
2. Remove any ropes, blindfold, or monitoring cables
3. Administer atipamezole IM (half dose in each gluteal muscle for rapid absorption)
4. Administer naltrexone IM (for opioid protocols) at the species-specific recommended dose
5. Step back to a safe distance and observe
6. Expected standing time: 2–5 minutes for fully reversible protocols
7. Monitor from a distance for 30 minutes to ensure no renarcotization or re-sedation

### Emergency Reversal (Life-Threatening Complication)

1. Administer reversal agents IV for maximum speed (half atipamezole IV + half IM; naltrexone IV)
2. Intubate and ventilate if the animal is apneic
3. Administer epinephrine if cardiac arrest is suspected
4. Begin CPR in small to medium animals if no pulse is detected
5. Accept that full reversal means loss of the capture; animal welfare takes absolute priority

### Post-Reversal Monitoring

- Observe for renarcotization (return of sedation after initial reversal), especially with long-acting opioids
- Monitor for ataxia, disorientation, or circling that may indicate incomplete recovery
- If possible, remain in visual contact for a minimum of 1 hour post-reversal
- Document recovery quality: smooth, moderate (mild ataxia), or rough (falling, excited)
