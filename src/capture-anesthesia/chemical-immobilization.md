# Chemical Immobilization

## Overview

Chemical immobilization is the cornerstone of wildlife capture for species that cannot be safely or humanely restrained by physical means alone. It involves the remote or close-range delivery of pharmacological agents that produce sedation, immobilization, or general anesthesia. The wildlife veterinarian must have thorough knowledge of the drugs, delivery systems, species-specific pharmacology, and emergency protocols required for safe chemical capture.

### Dart Selection

Darts must be matched to the drug volume, target species, and delivery system.

- **Type C (collared needle)** — standard for most wildlife applications; the collar prevents over-penetration
- **Type P (plain needle)** — used where thin skin or shallow muscle mass requires minimal penetration
- **Type S (side-port)** — reduces tissue trauma by expelling drug from lateral ports rather than the needle tip
- **Barbed vs. smooth needles** — barbed needles improve dart retention in thick-skinned species (elephants, rhinos) but increase tissue damage
- **Needle gauge and length** — 14G × 40 mm for thick-skinned megafauna; 18G × 25 mm for medium ungulates; 20G × 20 mm for small antelope

## Drug Combinations

### Potent Opioids

#### Etorphine (M99)

- **Potency**: approximately 1,000–3,000× morphine
- **Mechanism**: mu-opioid receptor agonist
- **Applications**: large ungulates (elephant, rhino, hippo, giraffe), large carnivores
- **Dose range**: 1–15 mg depending on species and body mass
- **Onset**: 5–15 minutes IM
- **Advantages**: small volume, rapid onset, fully reversible
- **Disadvantages**: extreme human toxicity, respiratory depression, hypertension, muscle rigidity
- **Reversal**: diprenorphine (M5050) at 2–3× the etorphine dose IV; naltrexone 50 mg per mg etorphine IM

#### Thiafentanil (A-3080)

- **Potency**: approximately 6,000× morphine
- **Mechanism**: mu-opioid receptor agonist with faster onset than etorphine
- **Applications**: preferred for ungulates where rapid induction reduces pursuit time
- **Dose range**: 0.5–10 mg depending on species
- **Onset**: 2–8 minutes IM (faster than etorphine)
- **Advantages**: very rapid onset, smaller dart volumes possible, predictable immobilization
- **Disadvantages**: extreme human toxicity, profound respiratory depression, narrow therapeutic index
- **Reversal**: naltrexone at 100 mg per mg thiafentanil IM; onset of reversal typically 1–3 minutes IV

### Alpha-2 Adrenergic Agonists

#### Medetomidine

- **Mechanism**: selective alpha-2 adrenergic agonist producing sedation, analgesia, and muscle relaxation
- **Applications**: combined with ketamine or opioids for synergistic immobilization across taxa
- **Dose range**: 0.02–0.1 mg/kg (large mammals); 0.05–0.2 mg/kg (carnivores)
- **Advantages**: fully reversible with atipamezole, provides excellent muscle relaxation, reduces opioid dose requirements
- **Disadvantages**: initial hypertension followed by hypotension, bradycardia, reduced cardiac output, vomiting (canids, felids)

### Dissociative Agents

#### Ketamine

- **Mechanism**: NMDA receptor antagonist; produces dissociative anesthesia
- **Applications**: widely used in combination with alpha-2 agonists or benzodiazepines
- **Dose range**: 2–15 mg/kg depending on species and combination drugs
- **Advantages**: wide safety margin, minimal respiratory depression, preserved laryngeal reflexes
- **Disadvantages**: poor muscle relaxation when used alone, rough recoveries, causes salivation, not fully reversible

#### Tiletamine-Zolazepam (Telazol / Zoletil)

- **Mechanism**: tiletamine (dissociative) + zolazepam (benzodiazepine); lyophilized powder reconstituted to desired concentration
- **Applications**: highly versatile; used in carnivores, primates, ungulates, bears
- **Standard concentration**: 100 mg/mL (each component at 50 mg/mL) when reconstituted with 5 mL diluent
- **High concentration**: up to 250 mg/mL when reconstituted with 2 mL for dart volume reduction
- **Dose range**: 2–10 mg/kg depending on species
- **Advantages**: single-drug simplicity, high concentration for low-volume darts, good muscle relaxation
- **Disadvantages**: prolonged recoveries in some species (especially felids due to slow tiletamine metabolism), not fully reversible

## Remote Delivery Systems

### Helicopter Darting

- Most common method for large free-ranging ungulates and carnivores in open habitats
- Requires experienced pilot-darter coordination
- Optimal darting distance: 10–20 m from helicopter skid
- Approach from the animal's rear quarter to present the gluteal or lateral neck musculature
- Minimize pursuit time to reduce capture myopathy risk (target <5 minutes)

### Ground Darting

- Vehicle-based or foot stalking approach
- Use of hides, blinds, or bait stations to draw animals to predictable locations
- Preferred for habituated animals or in areas where helicopter operations are impractical
- Longer induction distances require careful planning for animal tracking

### Drone-Assisted Darting

- Emerging technology for delivering darts via unmanned aerial vehicles
- Currently limited to experimental applications and very short-range delivery
- Advantages: reduced disturbance, access to difficult terrain
- Challenges: payload capacity, accuracy, regulatory restrictions

## Dosage Calculations

### Principles

- Base dose on estimated lean body mass; obese animals may require lower mg/kg doses
- Account for species-specific drug sensitivity (e.g., equids are sensitive to alpha-2 agonists)
- Adjust for physiological state: pregnant, lactating, or debilitated animals may require dose reduction of 25–50%
- Consider environmental temperature: hyperthermic risk increases with higher ambient temperatures, favoring lower doses
- Always round conservatively when exact body mass is unknown

### Allometric Dosing

For species where limited pharmacokinetic data exist, allometric scaling from related species may be used:

```
Dose = Reference dose × (Target mass / Reference mass)^0.75
```

This accounts for the non-linear relationship between body mass and metabolic rate across species.

## Reversal Agents

### Naltrexone

- **Target**: mu-opioid receptors
- **Application**: reversal of etorphine, thiafentanil, carfentanil
- **Dose**: 50 mg per mg etorphine; 100 mg per mg thiafentanil
- **Route**: IM preferred for field use; IV for emergency reversal
- **Duration**: long-acting (>24 hours); prevents renarcotization
- **Notes**: preferred over naloxone in wildlife due to longer duration of action

### Diprenorphine (M5050)

- **Target**: mixed opioid antagonist/partial agonist
- **Application**: reversal of etorphine
- **Dose**: 2–3× the etorphine dose, IV
- **Caution**: partial agonist activity may cause sedation in some species; renarcotization possible if metabolized before the opioid

### Atipamezole (Antisedan)

- **Target**: alpha-2 adrenergic receptor antagonist
- **Application**: reversal of medetomidine, dexmedetomidine
- **Dose**: 5× the medetomidine dose (by mg), IM
- **Onset**: 5–15 minutes IM
- **Notes**: administer half IM and half IV for rapid but sustained reversal in critical situations

### Flumazenil

- **Target**: benzodiazepine receptor antagonist
- **Application**: partial reversal of zolazepam (in Telazol/Zoletil) or midazolam
- **Dose**: 0.01–0.02 mg/kg IV
- **Notes**: does not reverse tiletamine; of limited clinical utility when tiletamine-zolazepam is used

## Drug Storage and Handling

- Store opioids in a locked, temperature-controlled container; maintain chain-of-custody documentation
- Protect light-sensitive drugs (azaperone, medetomidine) from direct sunlight
- Check expiration dates before every field operation
- Pre-draw drugs into labeled syringes only on the day of use
- Double-check all drug concentrations and volumes with a second qualified person before loading darts
- Dispose of unused drugs, darts, and needles according to institutional controlled substance protocols
