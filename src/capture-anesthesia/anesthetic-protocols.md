# Species-Specific Anesthetic Protocols

## Overview

Anesthetic protocols for wildlife must account for vast differences in physiology, anatomy, temperament, and environmental context across taxa. Unlike domestic animal practice, published pharmacokinetic data may be limited, and protocols frequently rely on clinical experience, allometric scaling, and extrapolation from related species. This chapter presents established protocols for major taxonomic groups.

## Large Ungulates

### General Principles

- Large body mass necessitates potent opioids (etorphine, thiafentanil) to achieve adequate drug concentrations in small dart volumes
- Ruminants must be positioned in sternal recumbency to prevent bloat and regurgitation
- Thermoregulation is a major concern; large muscle mass generates significant metabolic heat during induction
- Induction should be as rapid as possible to minimize pursuit-related stress

### African Buffalo (Syncerus caffer)

- **Induction**: Thiafentanil 6–8 mg + azaperone 60–80 mg IM via dart
- **Induction time**: 3–6 minutes
- **Maintenance**: Supplemental thiafentanil 1–2 mg IM if needed; isoflurane via portable vaporizer for prolonged procedures
- **Monitoring**: Respiratory rate (target 8–16 breaths/min), SpO₂ (maintain >90%), rectal temperature (watch for >40°C)
- **Position**: Sternal recumbency; pull the tongue forward and down; pass stomach tube if bloat develops
- **Reversal**: Naltrexone 100 mg per mg thiafentanil IM in the ear vein or large muscle mass
- **Recovery**: Animal should stand within 2–5 minutes of IV reversal; keep area clear of obstacles

### Giraffe (Giraffa camelopardalis)

- **Special considerations**: Extreme cardiovascular adaptations; massive blood pressure differentials between head and heart; risk of cerebral ischemia if the head is elevated during recumbency
- **Induction**: Etorphine 4–6 mg + azaperone 40–60 mg IM via dart; alternatively, thiafentanil 5–7 mg + medetomidine 3–5 mg
- **Induction time**: 6–12 minutes; prolonged pursuit increases capture myopathy risk
- **Position**: Guide to sternal recumbency using ropes as the animal becomes ataxic; the head must be supported and kept at or below heart level
- **Monitoring**: Continuous SpO₂; supplemental oxygen via nasal insufflation at 10–15 L/min is strongly recommended
- **Reversal**: Naltrexone + atipamezole IM; support the animal as it rises to prevent falling injuries

### White Rhinoceros (Ceratotherium simum)

- **Induction**: Etorphine 3–4 mg + azaperone 40–80 mg IM
- **Induction time**: 5–10 minutes
- **Monitoring**: Severe respiratory depression is common; SpO₂ may drop to 40–60% without intervention
- **Oxygen supplementation**: Mandatory; deliver via nasotracheal tube at 15–30 L/min
- **Butorphanol**: 15–30 mg IV after recumbency to partially antagonize etorphine-induced respiratory depression while maintaining immobilization
- **Position**: Lateral recumbency acceptable; blindfold and ear plugs to reduce stimulation
- **Reversal**: Naltrexone 100–200 mg IM + diprenorphine 6–12 mg IV
- **Recovery**: Rapid standing expected within 1–3 minutes of IV reversal

## Carnivores

### African Lion (Panthera leo)

- **Induction**: Medetomidine 0.05 mg/kg + ketamine 2.5 mg/kg IM
- **Alternative**: Tiletamine-zolazepam 3–5 mg/kg IM
- **Induction time**: 8–15 minutes
- **Supplementation**: Ketamine 1 mg/kg IV for additional depth; isoflurane for prolonged procedures
- **Monitoring**: HR 60–100 bpm; RR 10–20; SpO₂ >92%; rectal temperature 37–39°C
- **Position**: Lateral recumbency; protect eyes with ophthalmic lubricant and cover
- **Reversal**: Atipamezole 5× medetomidine dose IM; allow 15–20 minutes for recovery; do not reverse if ketamine alone was supplemented (risk of excitatory recovery)
- **Recovery**: Ensure the area is clear; monitor from a safe distance; full recovery in 30–60 minutes with alpha-2 reversal

### Bears (Ursidae)

- **Induction**: Tiletamine-zolazepam 5–8 mg/kg IM for most species
- **Alternative**: Medetomidine 0.05–0.08 mg/kg + ketamine 3–5 mg/kg IM
- **Special considerations**: Bears have thick subcutaneous fat layers that may delay drug absorption; dart placement in heavily muscled areas (shoulder, thigh) is critical
- **Monitoring**: Bears are prone to hyperthermia, especially in warm climates; cooling measures (water, shade) should be initiated early
- **Recovery**: Tiletamine-zolazepam produces prolonged recoveries (2–6 hours) in bears; atipamezole reversal of medetomidine shortens recovery when combination protocols are used

### Canids (Wolves, Wild Dogs)

- **Induction**: Medetomidine 0.05 mg/kg + ketamine 3 mg/kg IM
- **Monitoring**: Canids commonly vomit during induction with medetomidine; position the head downward during the induction period to reduce aspiration risk
- **Recovery**: Atipamezole reversal; observe for re-sedation for 30 minutes

## Primates

### General Principles

- Primates are strong, dexterous, and may remove darts before full drug delivery
- Ketamine alone provides reliable immobilization but with poor muscle relaxation and rough recoveries
- Combination protocols with medetomidine or midazolam improve quality significantly
- Intubation is recommended for great apes undergoing prolonged procedures

### Great Apes (Chimpanzees, Gorillas, Orangutans)

- **Induction**: Medetomidine 0.03–0.04 mg/kg + ketamine 3–4 mg/kg IM via dart
- **Alternative**: Tiletamine-zolazepam 3–5 mg/kg IM
- **Maintenance**: Isoflurane (1–2.5%) via endotracheal tube; IV propofol for short procedures
- **Monitoring**: ECG, SpO₂, ETCO₂, NIBP, rectal temperature; treat identically to human anesthesia monitoring standards
- **IV access**: Cephalic, saphenous, or femoral veins
- **Recovery**: Atipamezole reversal if medetomidine used; extubate when strong swallowing reflex returns; observe in a quiet, secure enclosure

### Old World Monkeys (Baboons, Macaques)

- **Induction**: Ketamine 5–10 mg/kg IM for short procedures; ketamine 5 mg/kg + medetomidine 0.04 mg/kg for longer procedures
- **Monitoring**: HR 100–200 bpm (species dependent); RR 20–40; SpO₂ >95%
- **Recovery**: Generally uneventful within 30–60 minutes

## Birds

### General Principles

- Birds have unique respiratory anatomy: air sacs, unidirectional flow, absence of a diaphragm
- Inhalant anesthesia (isoflurane, sevoflurane) is the preferred technique for most avian species
- Injectable protocols are used when inhalant delivery is impractical (field settings, large ratites)
- Birds are extremely susceptible to hypothermia under anesthesia due to high surface-area-to-mass ratio

### Inhalant Protocol (Standard)

- **Induction**: Isoflurane 3–5% or sevoflurane 5–8% via face mask or induction chamber
- **Maintenance**: Isoflurane 1.5–3% or sevoflurane 3–5% via face mask or endotracheal tube
- **Oxygen flow**: 1–2 L/min for small birds; 2–4 L/min for large raptors and waterbirds
- **Monitoring**: HR (Doppler over radial/ulnar artery or direct ECG), RR (observe coelomic excursions), cloacal temperature, reflex assessment (palpebral, pedal withdrawal)
- **Recovery**: Discontinue inhalant, provide 100% oxygen for 2–3 minutes; most birds recover rapidly (5–15 minutes)

### Injectable Protocol (Field)

- **Raptors and waterbirds**: Ketamine 10–30 mg/kg + midazolam 0.5–1 mg/kg IM
- **Ratites (ostrich, emu)**: Medetomidine 0.05–0.08 mg/kg + ketamine 2–3 mg/kg IM
- **Monitoring**: Avian heart rates are rapid (150–600+ bpm depending on species size); use Doppler rather than auscultation
- **Recovery**: Flumazenil 0.02 mg/kg IV to reverse midazolam; atipamezole for medetomidine; maintain thermal support throughout

## Reptiles

### General Principles

- Ectothermic physiology profoundly affects drug metabolism; keep reptiles within their preferred optimal temperature zone (POTZ)
- Intracardiac and right-to-left cardiac shunting makes inhalant anesthesia less predictable than in mammals or birds
- Recovery times are prolonged, especially at lower temperatures
- Reptiles may breath-hold for extended periods, making mask induction unreliable

### Chelonians (Turtles and Tortoises)

- **Induction**: Ketamine 5–10 mg/kg + medetomidine 0.1–0.15 mg/kg IM in the forelimb
- **Alternative**: Alfaxalone 5–10 mg/kg IM (increasingly popular)
- **Maintenance**: Isoflurane 1–3% via endotracheal tube (intubation is straightforward in most chelonians)
- **Monitoring**: Doppler over the carotid or brachial artery; cloacal temperature; reflex withdrawal of the limbs
- **Recovery**: Prolonged (1–6 hours); maintain at POTZ; atipamezole for medetomidine reversal

### Crocodilians

- **Induction**: Medetomidine 0.1–0.2 mg/kg + ketamine 5–10 mg/kg IM in the tail base or forelimb
- **Special considerations**: Jaw must be secured (taped closed) before handling; very powerful tail strikes
- **Recovery**: Atipamezole reversal; may require several hours for full recovery

### Snakes

- **Induction**: Ketamine 20–40 mg/kg IM (high doses required); alfaxalone 5–15 mg/kg IM or IV
- **Maintenance**: Isoflurane via endotracheal tube (easily intubated; glottis visible at the base of the tongue)
- **Recovery**: Very prolonged with ketamine alone (6–24 hours); alfaxalone provides faster recovery

## Marine Mammals

### Pinnipeds (Seals and Sea Lions)

- **Induction**: Isoflurane or sevoflurane via face mask or cone is preferred; medetomidine + ketamine or tiletamine-zolazepam IM for field immobilization
- **Special considerations**: Dive reflex (apnea, bradycardia) may be triggered by facial stimulation; heavy blubber layer delays IM drug absorption
- **Monitoring**: SpO₂ is critical; pinnipeds tolerate apneic episodes but must be ventilated if SpO₂ drops below 85%
- **Recovery**: Ensure a clear path to water for released animals; monitor for post-anesthetic myopathy

### Cetaceans (Dolphins and Small Whales)

- **Anesthesia is extremely high risk** in cetaceans due to voluntary respiration; general anesthesia of free-swimming cetaceans is essentially contraindicated
- **Sedation protocols**: Midazolam 0.05–0.1 mg/kg IM for minor procedures on stranded or restrained animals
- **Monitoring**: Continuous respiratory monitoring is mandatory; respiratory arrest is the primary risk
- **Support**: Keep skin moist, provide shade, monitor core temperature; never obstruct the blowhole

## Recovery Expectations

### Factors Affecting Recovery

- **Drug protocol**: Fully reversible protocols (opioid + alpha-2 agonist) provide the fastest, most predictable recoveries
- **Duration of anesthesia**: Prolonged procedures allow greater tissue accumulation of drugs, extending recovery
- **Body temperature**: Hypothermia slows drug metabolism and prolongs recovery in all taxa
- **Body condition**: Emaciated animals may have altered drug distribution and metabolism
- **Species**: Felids metabolize tiletamine slowly; reptiles have inherently prolonged recoveries

### Monitoring During Recovery

- Maintain visual observation until the animal is ambulatory and oriented
- Ensure the airway is patent; position to prevent aspiration of regurgitated material
- Continue temperature monitoring and active warming or cooling as needed
- Do not leave the animal unattended in an environment where it may injure itself or be vulnerable to predation
- Document time to first voluntary movement, time to sternal recumbency, and time to standing/ambulation
