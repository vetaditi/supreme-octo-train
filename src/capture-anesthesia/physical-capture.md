# Physical Capture Methods

## Overview

Physical capture methods remain fundamental to wildlife veterinary practice. While chemical immobilization dominates large mammal capture, physical methods are often preferred for smaller species, situations where darting is impractical, or when chemical agents pose unacceptable risk. Selecting the appropriate physical capture method requires detailed knowledge of species behavior, body size, habitat, and the objectives of capture.

## Mist Nets

Mist nets are the standard tool for capturing small to medium-sized birds and bats.

### Design and Setup

- Constructed from fine nylon or polyester mesh (typically 30–36 mm mesh size for passerines)
- Standard lengths: 6 m, 9 m, or 12 m; height typically 2.5–3 m with 4–5 shelf strings
- Denier rating determines visibility and strength — lower denier (50–70) for small passerines, higher (75–110) for larger birds
- Net color should match the environment: black for forest understory, tan for open habitats

### Deployment Protocols

- Nets should be set perpendicular to expected flight paths, near feeding areas, water sources, or habitat edges
- Poles must be secured firmly; guy lines prevent collapse in wind
- Net tension should allow adequate bagging — overly taut nets cause bounce-outs
- Maximum recommended checking interval: **10 minutes** in mild conditions, **5 minutes** or less in temperature extremes, rain, or high predator activity

### Extraction Technique

- Determine which side the bird entered from before attempting removal
- Work methodically from feet to wings to head, unwinding mesh rather than pulling
- Use blunt-tipped scissors to cut mesh only as a last resort
- Place extracted birds immediately in cloth bags for processing

### Limitations

- Ineffective in strong winds (> 15 km/h)
- Risk of injury or mortality increases with prolonged entanglement
- Predator attraction (raptors, snakes) requires vigilance
- Not suitable for birds larger than approximately 300 g without heavy-duty nets

## Hoop Nets

Hoop nets (dip nets) are versatile tools for capturing a range of taxa.

### Applications

- **Waterbirds** — capturing flightless molting ducks, geese, and swans from boats or during drive operations
- **Reptiles** — aquatic turtles, small crocodilians, and snakes
- **Small mammals** — emerging from dens or flushed from cover

### Specifications

- Frame diameter: 30–60 cm for most applications, up to 90 cm for large waterbirds
- Handle length: 1–2 m for ground use, 2–4 m for boat-based capture
- Mesh bag depth should be at least 1.5 times the frame diameter to prevent escape
- Padded rims reduce trauma during capture of birds and reptiles

## Box Traps and Cage Traps

Box traps (live traps) are among the most widely used capture devices for terrestrial mammals.

### Common Types

| Trap Type | Target Species | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Sherman live trap | Small rodents, shrews | Treadle-activated door |
| Tomahawk live trap | Medium mammals (raccoons, skunks, opossums) | Push-pull door trigger |
| Havahart trap | Varied — multiple sizes available | Gravity door with pan trigger |
| Custom wooden box trap | Large carnivores, primates | Species-specific trigger systems |
| Tube traps | Weasels, stoats | One-way entry with trap door |

### Deployment Guidelines

- **Bait selection** — must be species-appropriate and non-toxic to non-target species
  - Carnivores: meat, fish, scent lures
  - Omnivores: peanut butter, fruit, sardines
  - Herbivores: grain, apple, alfalfa
- **Placement** — along known travel routes, near dens, at habitat interfaces
- **Pre-baiting** — 3–7 days of baiting with traps wired open increases capture success
- **Trap modification** — covers or vegetation camouflage reduce stress in trapped animals
- **Substrate** — place soil, leaf litter, or bedding material on trap floor to prevent foot abrasions

### Checking Schedules

- **Minimum standard**: traps checked at dawn and dusk (every 12 hours)
- **Best practice**: every 4–6 hours, particularly in temperature extremes
- **Mandatory conditions for more frequent checks**:
  - Ambient temperature > 30 °C or < -10 °C
  - High predation risk areas
  - Trapping periods coinciding with rain or storms
  - When neonates or juveniles may be captured

## Pitfall Traps

Pitfall traps are standard for sampling herpetofauna and small mammals.

### Design

- Containers (buckets, typically 10–20 L) buried flush with the ground surface
- Drift fences (aluminum flashing or silt fencing, 30–50 cm height) guide animals into pitfalls
- Fence length: typically 15–50 m per array, arranged in Y, X, or linear configurations
- Drainage holes prevent drowning during rain; alternatively, sponge or foam inserts provide refuge

### Critical Considerations

- Cover boards or mesh lids reduce predation on trapped animals and provide shade
- Escape ramps for non-target species (small sticks or hardware cloth ramps)
- Check frequency: every 4–6 hours in warm conditions; at minimum dawn and dusk
- Pitfalls should be closed (lids sealed) when not actively being monitored

## Drive Nets and Corral Traps

Drive nets are used for the mass capture of ungulates and flightless birds.

### Method

1. Funnel-shaped net enclosures are erected in advance, with wings extending outward
2. Animals are driven toward the enclosure by helicopter, vehicles, horseback, or personnel on foot
3. Once inside the funnel, animals are corralled into a boma or holding pen
4. Individual animals are then manually restrained or chemically immobilized for processing

### Species Applications

- **Ungulates** — wildebeest, springbok, impala, zebra (southern African operations)
- **Ratites** — ostriches, emus, rheas
- **Waterfowl** — flightless molting geese and ducks

### Risk Management

- Net height must exceed the maximum jump height of the target species
- Padding and visual barriers at net edges reduce collision injuries
- Crowd density in holding areas must be managed to prevent crushing and hyperthermia
- Dedicated monitors should watch for animals showing signs of capture myopathy (ataxia, recumbency, elevated respiratory rate)

## Cannon Nets and Rocket Nets

Cannon nets (or rocket nets) propel a weighted net over aggregated animals using explosive charges or compressed air.

### Setup

- The net is carefully folded and positioned upwind or at the edge of a bait station
- Projectiles (cannons or rockets) are placed at intervals along the net edge
- Firing wires connect to a remote detonation point 50–100 m away
- The operator must have clear line of sight to all animals and net edges

### Target Species

- Waterfowl, shorebirds, and turkeys at bait stations
- Ungulates at feeding sites or mineral licks (less common)

### Safety Requirements

- Exclusion zone around the firing area for all non-essential personnel
- Misfire protocols must be established before deployment
- All personnel trained in explosive handling (where applicable)
- Animals must be processed rapidly after net deployment — teams should be staged and ready

## Manual Restraint Techniques by Taxon

### Small Mammals (< 5 kg)

- **Rodents and insectivores** — restrain by scruffing the loose skin over the shoulders; support the body with the opposite hand
- **Bats** — hold with thumbs over folded wings; use leather gloves due to bite risk and rabies exposure
- **Lagomorphs** — support hindquarters at all times to prevent spinal fracture from kicking; never restrain by ears

### Medium Mammals (5–30 kg)

- **Mesocarnivores** (foxes, raccoons, badgers) — use catch poles (control poles) or heavy leather gloves; restrain with one hand behind the head and one controlling the hindquarters
- **Small primates** — squeeze cages reduce handling stress; heavy leather gloves for manual restraint
- **Small ungulates** — approach from behind, control head and neck, straddle the body

### Large Mammals (> 30 kg)

- Manual restraint is generally inappropriate without chemical sedation
- Chutes, squeeze cages, and hydraulic crushes are used in captive settings
- In the field, large mammals should be chemically immobilized before handling

### Birds

- **Raptors** — control feet first (primary weapons); use towels or hoods to cover the head; leather gloves mandatory
- **Waterbirds** — control the neck to prevent pecking (herons, cormorants); wrap wings against the body
- **Passerines** — "bander's grip" with index and middle fingers around the neck, body resting in the palm
- **Ratites** — hood immediately to reduce stress; control with body weight; beware of powerful kicks

### Reptiles

- **Snakes** — use snake hooks and tubes for venomous species; pin the head and grasp behind the jaw for non-venomous species; never free-handle venomous snakes
- **Chelonians** — grasp the shell at the bridge or posterior margin; large species may require multiple handlers
- **Crocodilians** — secure the jaws with rope or tape immediately; control the tail; require experienced teams for animals > 2 m
- **Lizards** — gentle but firm grip around the pectoral girdle; avoid grasping the tail (autotomy risk in many species)

## Equipment Maintenance

### Trap Inspection Schedule

| Equipment | Inspection Frequency | Key Checks |
|---|---|---|
| Mist nets | Before each use | Holes, broken shelf strings, UV degradation |
| Box traps | Before each deployment | Trigger mechanism, door closure, rust/corrosion, sharp edges |
| Pitfall traps | Before each sampling period | Structural integrity, drainage, cover condition |
| Drive nets | Before each operation | Net integrity, anchor points, rope condition |
| Cannon nets | Before each deployment | Projectile condition, charge integrity, wiring continuity |
| Catch poles | Monthly or before use | Cable integrity, release mechanism, handle condition |
| Dart equipment | After each use | Barrel cleaning, seal replacement, pressure testing |

### Storage

- All traps and nets should be cleaned, dried, and stored in a dry, pest-free environment
- Metal traps should be oiled or waxed to prevent rust
- Mist nets should be stored loosely to prevent mesh adhesion
- Chemical immobilization equipment must be stored according to controlled substance regulations

## Trap Checking Schedules — Regulatory Standards

Most jurisdictions require minimum trap checking frequencies, but best practice often exceeds regulatory minimums:

- **IACUC protocols** typically mandate checks every 12 hours at minimum
- **Best practice recommendations**: every 4–6 hours for most trap types
- **Extreme conditions** (temperature, predation, weather): every 2–4 hours or continuous monitoring via camera traps
- **Mist nets and cannon nets**: continuous attendance required during operation

All trap checks should be documented with the following minimum data:

- Date and time of check
- Trap status (sprung, unsprung, occupied, malfunctioned)
- Species captured (including non-target captures)
- Condition of animal at capture
- Any injuries or mortalities
- Weather conditions
- Action taken (processed, released, euthanized)
